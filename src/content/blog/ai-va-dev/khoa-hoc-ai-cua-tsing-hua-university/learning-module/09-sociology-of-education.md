## Sociology of education as a discipline
- Subject vs. Discipline:
	- Subject: (K-12 focus)
		- Focuses on the "Object" (What we study)
		- Perceived through different content areas
		- The focus is on acquiring knowledge that already exists.
	- Discipline: (Higher Ed focus)
		- Focuses on "Regulation" (How we produce knowlegde)
		- A standardized system of knowlegde production.
		- A discipline isn't just about learning what others have discovered; it's about mastering a systematic approach to generating new knowledge.
	- The core difference between these two concepts lies in their orientation. Subject-based learning focuses on absorbing specific facts and content—it's about what we know. Discipline-based learning, however, centers on mastering a professional mode of inquiry—it's about how we come to know. In a discipline, scholars don't just consume knowledge; they produce it according to established conventions and rigorous standards that have been developed and refined by the academic community over time.
- **Five Essential Elemements**:
	- **Specific Object/Field**: Consensus on what is being researched. Scholars within the discipline have reached a fundamental consensus about what phenomena, questions, or domains they investigate. This shared understanding creates boundaries that distinguish one discipline from another and provides focus for collective inquiry
	- **Specific Methodology**: Shared paradigms on how to conduct research. A mature discipline develops and refines particular approaches to knowledge production. Scholars share common understandings about what counts as valid evidence, how to design studies, and what procedures ensure rigor. These methodological norms guide research practice and enable meaningful evaluation of scholarly work.
	- **Concepts & Theories**: A shared language and symbolic system. Disciplines develop specialized vocabularies and conceptual systems that facilitate precise communication among practitioners. This common language prevents misunderstanding and enables cumulative knowledge building, as scholars can build upon each other's work with confidence in shared meanings.
	- **Scholarly Community**: Institutions (colleges/institutes) for self-management. institutionalized through academic departments, research institutes, and professional associations. These organizational structures provide mechanisms for training new scholars, maintaining standards, allocating resources, and ensuring the discipline's continuity across generations.
	- **Academic Journals**: Platforms for dissemination and feedback. These venues serve multiple functions: they circulate new findings, enable peer review, establish quality standards, and create an archival record of the discipline's intellectual development. Together, these five elements form the foundation upon which any discipline stands and through which it advances knowledge in its domain.
- **The Identity Conflict: "Education" or "Sociology"?**: 
	- Educational Sociology: Focuses on pedagogical perspective to study phenomena in schools (e.g. teacher-student interaction)
	- Sociology of Education: Focuses on sociology perspectives to analyze educational institutions (e.g., inequality, social mobility)
	- Modern Consensus: It is primarily the application of the "Sociological Perspective" to education
- **The Sociological Imagination**: The Disciplinary "Eye"
	- C. Wright Mills: Introduced the concept of "Sociological Imagination"
	- Definition: The ability to connect individual life experiences with larger historical and social structural forces.
	- Application: Seeing how a student's struggle is linked to social class or policy shifts.
- Mapping the Educational Science Cluster: Classification of Sub-disciplines

| Category          | Basis of Formation  | Examples                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Method-based      | Research Methods    | History of Education, Comparative Education  |
| Object-based      | Specific Domains    | Preschool, Special, Higher Education         |
| Practice-based    | Educational Process | Management, Curriculum, Policy               |
| Perspective-based | Disciplinary Lens   | Psychology, Sociology, Economics             |
| Meta-discipline   | Philosophy/Theory   | Philosophy of Education, Pedagogy Principles |
- Locating Our Discipline:
	- Category: Perspective-based sub-discipline
	- Role: Provides a unique "Sociological Lens" within the educational science cluster
	- Interdisciplinary Nature: Interacts with practice-based and method-based discipline to analyze educational equity and institutional logic 
# Structural Functionalism
## Structural Functionalism & Sociological Orientations
- Fundamental question: How do societies hold together? What keeps millions of people cooperating, following rules, and maintaining stability?
	- Structural functionalism offers us a unique lens to answer these questions by examining how different parts of society work together like organs in a living body.
- Do Individuals Create Society: 
	- Margaret Thatcher's Quote: "There is no such thing as society. There are individual men and women"
		- Tuyên bố này thể hiện lập trường cá nhân chủ nghĩa cực đoan, phủ nhận sự tồn tại của xã hội ngoài những cá nhân cấu thành nên nó.
	- Reflection: Do you agree? Is 'society' just a label for a group of people, or is it a real entity that exist above us?
		- Nếu xã hội chỉ đơn thuần là một nhãn hiệu tiện lợi cho một tập hợp các cá nhân, thì các vấn đề xã hội thực chất chỉ là tổng hợp của các vấn đề cá nhân. 
		- Nhưng nếu xã hội tồn tại như một thực thể thực sự với các đặc tính và sức mạnh riêng, thì chúng ta cần những công cụ khác nhau để hiểu và giải quyết các hiện tượng xã hội. 
		- Cuộc tranh luận này tạo tiền đề cho việc hiểu tại sao chủ nghĩa chức năng cấu trúc lại nổi lên như một quan điểm xã hội học quan trọng.
- Is Society Inherently Good or Bad?:
	- Jean-Jacques Rousseau: "Nature made man happy and good, but society deprave (tha hoá) him..."
		- Theo quan điểm này, văn minh và các thể chế xã hội làm tha hóa sự ngây thơ và tốt đẹp tự nhiên của chúng ta. 
		- Điều này đặt ra những câu hỏi sâu sắc: Liệu việc sống trong các xã hội có tổ chức có cải thiện chúng ta bằng cách cung cấp giáo dục, văn hóa và hướng dẫn đạo đức? Hay nó hạn chế sự tự do và tính chân thực tự nhiên của chúng ta?
	- Discussion: If society is a real force, does it have a moral direction? Does it improve us or corrupt us?
		- Chủ nghĩa chức năng cấu trúc, như chúng ta sẽ thấy, có một lập trường đặc biệt về vấn đề này, xem các thể chế xã hội nói chung là những cơ chế có lợi giúp duy trì trật tự và đáp ứng nhu cầu của con người.
- Comparing Sociological Perspectives: We distinguish theories based on whether they see society as a real entity and whether that entity is harmonious or conflictual
	- Three Major Sociological Orientations: Chủ nghĩa chức năng cấu trúc chiếm một vị trí đặc biệt trong bối cảnh này: nó xem xã hội là có thật và nhấn mạnh sự hài hòa, hội nhập và hợp tác giữa các bộ phận xã hội. Điều này trái ngược với lý thuyết xung đột, cũng xem xã hội là có thật nhưng nhấn mạnh sự đấu tranh và cạnh tranh, và với các phương pháp diễn giải, tập trung nhiều hơn vào ý nghĩa và tương tác cá nhân.

| Orientation              | View of Society                                  | Focus Level               |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| Social Nominalism        | Society is just a name/collection of individuals | Micro (Interactions)      |
| Structural Functionalism | Society is a real, harmonious entity             | Macro (Order/Stability)   |
| Conflict Theory          | Society is a real entity based on power struggle | Macro (Inequality/Change) |
- The 19th Century Crisis: Why did Functionalism emerge? - The 19th Century was a time of chaos and transformation.
	- Industrial Revolution: Collapse of old economic orders
	- Enlightenment (Thời kỳ Khai sáng): Shift from faith to reason
	- Political Revolutions: Seeking a new basis for social stability.
- Auguste Comte & Positivism: Sociology as a 'Social Physics' 
	- Auguste Comte believed human knowledge evolves through specific stage, leading to a scientific study of society. 
	- The Law of Three Stages:

| Stage        | Basis of Knowledge     | Method                   |
| ------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Theological  | Religious belief/Gods  | Imagination              |
| Metaphysical | Philosophical concepts | Abstract Logic           |
| Positive     | Scientific laws/Data   | Observation & Experiment |
- The Biological Analogy: Society as a Living Organism
	- Structure: Just as a body has organs (heart, lungs), society has institutions (family, education, government)
	- Function: Each part must perform its role to keep whole system alive
	- Interdependence: If one past fails, the whole system is affected
- Social Health and Sickness: 
	- Normal State: When structures function correctly and maintain equilibrium (balance)
	- Pathological State: When functions are disrupted, leading to social 'sickness' or instability 
	- Goal of Sociology: To diagnose social ills and find ways to restore 'health' (order)
- Is Crime Always 'Sickness'? - Durkheim's Paradox: "A certain level of crime is 'normal'"
	- Defines Moral Boundaries: Show what a society considers right vs. wrong. When someone is punished for theft, it reinforces the value of property rights for everyone watching.
	- Strengthens Social Bonds (communities unite against the criminal)
	- Allows for Social Change: Today's deviance can become tomorrow's norm. Many behaviors once considered criminal, from women voting to interracial marriage, are now accepted or even celebrated.
	- Pathology: Only 'too much' or 'zero' crime indicates a sick society - because that would indicate such rigid conformity that innovation and adaptation become impossible. This insight reveals the sophistication of functionalist thinking: it's not simplistically saying everything is good, but rather analyzing how even seemingly negative phenomena can serve important social functions within certain limits.
## Structural Functionalism: Key Figures and Their Perspectives
- Three foundational figures: 
	- **Herbert Spencer** (1820-1903): Biological Foundation (The Organism)
		- **Background**: Known as the "Father of Social Darwinism"
		- **Core Philosophy**: Applied biological evolutionary principles to social science 
		- **Social Organism**: Comparing society to a living body where parts function to maintain the whole.
		- **Key Viewpoints of Spencer:**
			1. **Functional Differentiation**: Societies evolve from simple, uniform structures to complex, specialized ones
			2. **Survival of the Fittest**: Social progress occurs through competition and natural selection
			3. **Specialization**: As societies grow, institutions (economy, government) become more distinct
	- **Émile Durkheim** (1858-1917): The Methodological Architect (Social Facts & Solidarity)
		- **Background**: Frenh founding father of sociology
		- **Social Facts:** Objective realites (laws, customs, moral norms) that exist outside individuals and exert control over them
		- **Methodology**: Treating social facts as "things" to be studied scientifically. This methodological approach was revolutionary because it established sociology as a legitimate, independent scientific discipline and provided the analytical tools that would later support functional analysis.
		- **Mechanical vs. Organic Solidarity**:

| Features          | Mechanical Solidarity | Organic Solidarity         |
| ----------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Basic             | Similarity/Tradition  | Difference/Interdependence |
| Division of Labor | Low                   | High                       |
| Social Bond       | Common Conscience     | Functional Need            |
| Modernity         | Traditional Societies | Industrial Societies       |
		-  **Functional Analysis Paradigm**: had a profound impact on the entire functionalist tradition
			- **Social Integration**: How society holds itself together through shared values - understand what keeps communities cohesive and prevents them from fragmenting into isolated individuals.
			- **Anomie**: The state of normlessness during rapid social change - When society undergoes dramatic changes, the traditional rules and standards that once guided behavior may no longer apply, yet new norms have not yet crystallized.
			- **Utility of Deviance**: Analyzing how phenomena like crime or suicide serve (or disrupt) social functions. - while seemingly destructive, may actually serve to reinforce social boundaries, clarify collective values, or signal areas where social systems require adjustment
	- **Talcott Parsons** (1902-1979): Systemic Synthesis (AGIL & Action Theory)
		- **Background**: Dominant figure in mid-20th century American sociology - title of systemic synthesizer
		- **Grand Theory**: Attempted to create a unified theoretical framework for all social action
		- **Structural-Functionalism**: Focus on how social systems maintain equilibrium - elevated functionalism to its systematic peak. He was not content with piecemeal functional analysis but instead sought to build a comprehensive theoretical system capable of explaining all social phenomena. While this theoretical ambition would later face criticism, it played a crucial role in advancing sociological theory during its time.
		- **The AGIL Model**: argues that any social system, to maintain its existence and function effectively, must fulfill four fundamental requirement. These four functions are interlocking and interdependent. Parsons argued that a social system can only operate stably when all four functions are adequately fulfilled. The AGIL model provides us with a systematic framework for analyzing the position and role of any social institution within the broader social system.
			- **A & G**: These two functions—one oriented outward to acquire resources, the other inward to set direction—together form the foundation of social system operation.
				- **A**daptation: How a system secures resources from the environment. (The Economy - ensuring that society can produce and distribute material wealth.)
				- **G**oal Attainment: How a system defines and achieves its objectives. (The Political System - establishing collective goals and organizing efforts to reach them.)
			-** I & L**: 
				- **I**ntegration: Coordinating the parts of the system to prevent conflict (The Legal System - Law/Religion - Community: binding diverse groups into a cohesive whole through norms and shared beliefs.)
				- **L**atency: Maintaining values and managing internal tension (Family/Education - Families transmit core values across generations, while educational systems institutionalize these values and pass them on to young people.)
		- **Parson' Pattern Variables**: understand the different orientations that guide social action. The theory presents several pairs of opposing choices that actors face in social situations.
			- **Universalism vs. Particularism**: Following general rule vs. personal relationships. Modern bureaucratic organizations exemplify universalism, where everyone is treated according to the same standards regardless of personal connections. In contrast, family relationships operate more on particularistic principles, where special treatment based on kinship is not only accepted but expected.
			- **Achievement vs. Ascription**: Status based on performance vs. status based on birth traits. Modern societies increasingly emphasize achievement orientation, rewarding individuals for what they do rather than who they are by birth. Traditional societies, however, often placed greater weight on ascribed status, where one's position was largely determined by circumstances of birth.
			- **Affectivity vs. Affective Neutrality**: Emotional expression vs. professional detachment. Consider the difference between a close friendship, where emotional expression is natural and valued, and a professional consultation, where maintaining appropriate emotional distance is essential.
- Conclusion: Functionalism provides a lens to see society as a balanced system of interdependent parts
## The social Functions of Education: Beyond the Individudual
- Is Homeschooling Enough?: Some parents believe homeschooling or private tutoring is more efficient for academic success
	- Question: What might a child miss by not attending a formal school?
	- Thinking: Is school just about learning facts, or is there a larger social purpose? 
	- The Functional of Socialization: 
	- 
		- Level 1: Basic Rules: Learning how social institutions work and the art of human interaction
		- Level 2: Values & Morals: Developing a shared identity, patriotism, and social consensus
		- Historical Examples: 
			- Sparta: Educating warriors
			- Athens: Educating democratic citizens
			- Rome: Educating knights
- Why is the Gaokao so important?: In many societies, high-stakes testing is a life changing event. This page serve as a starting point for us to explore its deeper societal implication;
	- Question: Beyond personal success, what role does this examination play for the entire society?
	- Perspective: How does society decide who becomes a doctor, an engineer, or a worker?
- Breaking Hereditary Patterns: education's second social function. Three interconnected dimensions: 
	- Social Differentiation: Matching individual talent and ability to specific roles in the social division of labor (→ [Economics of Education](/blog/ai-va-dev/khoa-hoc-ai-cua-tsing-hua-university/learning-module/04-economics-of-education/))
	- Social Mobility: Moving from "Ascribed Status" (inheritance) to "Achieved Status" (merit)
	- Key Outcome: Allowing individuals to escape the constraints of their family background through education
- Transmission of Knowledge & Culture: Cultural Continuity: Education's third crucial social function
	- Function: Preserving the collective knowledge and culture of a community
	- Process: Passing the "torch" of human civilization to new members
	- Significance: Ensuring that knowledge does not disappear and that the community maintains its unique identity across generations. 
- Why do textbooks matter for security:
	- Controversy: Often arises regarding history or values are presented in textbooks
	- Question: Why is the content of a textbook considered a frontline of national security?
	- Thinking: How does education influence a nation's political stability and economic future?
- Education as a System Support: Education systems do not exist in isolation
	- Political System: Shaping national identity and political stance, which directly relates to social stability
	- Economic System: Providing a workforce that matches labor market demands to drive economic growth.
	- Integration: Education acts as the engine that powers other social subsystems

We can observe that education's contributions to other social systems fundamentally represent extensions of its core functions. When education shapes citizens' political stances, this is essentially an extension of the socialization function, as it involves cultivating shared values and fostering a sense of collective identity. Similarly, when education prepares qualified workers for the labor market, this exemplifies the differentiation function in action, guiding individuals toward social roles that align with their capabilities and society's needs.

These interconnected functions lead us to a crucial insight: education serves as the backbone that maintains, reproduces, and evolves social structure. Education doesn't merely preserve traditions from the past or address present-day needs. It actively prepares society for future possibilities by equipping new generations with the knowledge, skills, and values necessary for continued social development. Through socialization, education ensures cultural continuity. Through differentiation, it enables social mobility and adaptation. Through knowledge transmission, it builds upon accumulated human understanding.

Understanding these multifaceted social functions helps us recognize an essential truth: education transcends its role as merely a tool for individual advancement. It constitutes the very foundation upon which society sustains itself and progresses across generations. Without education's integrative work across these various dimensions, societies would struggle to maintain coherence, adapt to change, or pass their accumulated wisdom to future members.
