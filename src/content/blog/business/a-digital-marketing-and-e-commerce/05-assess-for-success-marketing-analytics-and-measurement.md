### Media planning
#### Welcome to module 1
- Campaigns require planning for the best outcomes → create **media plans** for campaigns and set **performance goals** to measure their success
- Reporting capabilities in **Google Ads** and **Google Analytics** → use their metrics to monitor campaign performance; you can tell if a campaign is a success by the metrics (the numbers)
#### Media plans and performance goals
- Analogy: architectural plans help people visualize a building before it's built → **media plans** help people understand what's in a marketing campaign before it's run
	- Plans have dimensions (height, width) ↔ media plans have requirements (number of **impressions**, number of **clicks**) to be met when a campaign is run
	- **Impressions**: how many times an ad is displayed; **clicks**: measure responses
- **Performance goal**: a target that has a measurable numeric value → can apply to marketing goals or specific campaigns in a media plan
	- When they apply to marketing goals → sometimes called **marketing objectives**
- Real example: Google's marketing goal to reach **Black and Latinx audiences** in the United States → media plan called for **15%** of Google's brand campaign budget to be spent on culturally relevant media (the performance goal)
	- A campaign targeting media events with high viewership among Black audiences → **13%** increase in brand advocacy among Black adults 35–49
	- Sponsoring the **Latin Grammys** → **14%** increase in Spanish-speaking adults 18–49 likely to consider purchasing a **Pixel 5** phone
	- Full article on thinkwithgoogle.com: *Inside Google Marketing: Our Approach to Inclusive Media Planning and Buying* (→ [Course 1: Inclusive marketing](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/))
- The most important performance goal for an ad campaign: **return on ad spend (ROAS)** = ratio of revenue generated to the amount spent on advertising → often included in media plans
- For best results, always create a media plan
#### What is media planning?
- **Media plan**: details about where, when, and how often an ad will appear across all media channels (radio, TV, newspapers, magazines, paid search, social media) → this program focuses on digital media → **digital media plans**
- What to consider:
	- **Target audience**: whom do I need to reach?
	- **Budget**: how much you can spend
	- **Media mix**: which channels get how much of the budget
	- **Duration**: how long the campaign will run
	- **Key performance indicators (KPIs)**, performance goals, and metrics to measure progress toward targeted performance
- Performance goals are created from business and marketing goals and KPIs (→ [Course 1: Define your marketing goals](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/)):
	- **Business goal**: a desired aim, achievement, or outcome for a business (growing revenue, increasing profit, gaining market share, improving customer service)
	- **Marketing goal**: a specific objective in a marketing plan or strategy that supports a business goal (raising brand awareness, increasing web traffic, generating new leads, increasing customer value)
	- **KPI**: a measurement used to gauge how successful a business is in its effort to reach a business or marketing goal → numeric, often serve as performance targets for marketing goals, but not specific enough for individual campaigns → create an additional **campaign-level performance goal** for each campaign (included in the digital media plan)
	- Good alignment: campaign-level performance goals map back to a marketing goal and even to a relevant business goal
- Example: business goal to grow revenue → marketing goal: a targeted marketing effort across a mix of media (a ROAS performance goal may be set here) → three digital ad campaigns, each with its own performance goal → each channel's ROAS contributes to the overall ROAS
- **ROAS calculation** (single product): **number of products sold × cost per unit ÷ ad spend**
	- $80 in advertising to sell three units of a $100 product → ROAS = **3.75** → every $1 spent on advertising makes $3.75
	- Same ROAS as a number (3.75), a **ratio** (3.75:1), or a **percentage** (375%)
- Example with numbers: business goal to grow revenue by **10%**, **5:1** ROAS for the marketing goal → individual targets per channel: **3:1** for search ads, **4:1** for display ads, **2:1** for social media ads → all individual targets work together to achieve the overall 5:1
	- Set ROAS targets from previous campaigns (commonly the same or slightly below the previously achieved value); without historical data, make your best estimate and adjust after viewing initial metrics → dynamic changes are allowed

#### Steps for media planning
- **Digital media plan** contents and their purpose:
	- **Target audience**: the group of people most likely to purchase the products or services → may be defined by a combination of **customer personas** (→ [Course 2: Customer personas](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/)); can include demographics (age, income, devices used, preferred media)
		- Purpose: spend a limited budget on the people most likely to make a purchase (purely economic and strategic reasons)
	- **Budget**: usually a fixed budget allocated from a larger marketing budget; spending more often requires justification and approval
		- Purpose: prevent over- or under-spending for a particular channel
	- **Media mix**: how much of a budget will be spent across each media channel → actual amounts or percentages (e.g. $20,000 or 30% of the budget to social media)
		- Purpose: enables the right content decisions based on the allocated budget per channel
	- **Duration (flight)**: the length of a campaign → with a **pay-per-click (PPC)** model, ensure the budget is sufficient for the entire duration → duration heavily influenced by budget
	- **KPIs**: include KPIs for each media channel (e.g. each channel has its own ROAS)
	- **Performance goals and metrics**: if metrics meet or exceed a performance goal, the campaign is successful
		- **Clickthrough rate (CTR)** = clicks ÷ impressions → a popular metric for ads
- Steps to create a digital media plan:
	1. **Confirm business goals**
	2. **Confirm marketing goals**
	3. **Conduct market research** (optional) → determine customer personas and target audience if not clearly identified
	4. **Define performance (campaign) goals** → related to a higher-level goal (e.g. sales growth in a region → increase brand awareness through ads directed at users in that region)
	5. **Select the media channels** → align with the media preferences of the target audience; then create the media mix by priority
	6. **Determine and document all media plan items** → document or media planning software
- Format: documents, spreadsheets, or specialized media planning tools → the format isn't as important as the contents
- Example template (marketing goal: grow brand awareness of target audiences, budget **$75,000**, duration **3 months**):

| Target audience        | Media channel | Media mix | KPIs                  | Performance goals and metrics           |
| ---------------------- | ------------- | --------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Adults 50–65 years old | Display       | 60%       | CTR                   | 5% increase in page views; CTR > 2%     |
| All adults             | Search        | 30%       | CTR                   | 10% increase in page views; CTR > 2%    |
| Adults 18–49 years old | Social media  | 10%       | Audience growth rate  | 20% or greater audience growth rate     |

- Can include a reference image of a **marketing funnel** to map performance goals and metrics to the relevant funnel stage (e.g. Awareness) (→ [Course 1: The marketing funnel](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/))

### Performance goals
#### Set performance goals
- Performance goals and their associated metrics **define success** → without them, it's a feeling or guesswork
- Example 1: business goal to improve **customer acquisition** by **20%** over the next three months
	- **Customer acquisition**: the process of gaining new customers → for an e-commerce store, depends on traffic to the site
	- Campaign to generate more traffic → initial performance goal: **20% increase in weekly new visitor counts** → monitor new visitors vs. previous baseline numbers
	- But new visitors may leave without taking action → **bounce**; **bounce rate** = bounce sessions ÷ total sessions
	- Second performance goal: **reduce the bounce rate by 50%** → monitor new visitor counts and bounce rate; returning visitors holding steady or increasing
- Example 2: business goal of **$50,000 in incremental sales** over the next month; marketing goal to increase marketing **ROI** by two times its current value
	- **Average order value (AOV)** = sum of individual order amounts ÷ number of orders → AOV = $148
	- Additional orders = $50,000 ÷ $148 = **338** → performance goal: an additional 338 orders
	- Current marketing ROI is 2, goal is to double it → assume a **4:1 ROAS**
	- Incremental budget = incremental sales ÷ ROAS = $50,000 ÷ 4 = **$12,500** of additional campaign budget
- Example 3: marketing goal to increase **conversion volume** from social media by **25%** over the next six months
	- **Conversion volume**: the total number of conversions or total monetary value of conversions over a period of time; a **conversion** happens when a potential customer takes a desired action
	- By number: e.g. increase by **10%** the conversions and sessions referred from **Instagram** over six months
	- By monetary value: assign monetary values to different conversion types (leads, purchases), set up and monitored in tools like Google Ads → e.g. **$100,000** attributed to conversions and sessions referred from Instagram
- Reverse direction: given a report from a completed campaign → view the performance metrics and identify related business or marketing goals

#### Cost-related performance goals
- **Smart Bidding**: Google Ads monitors goals so strategies are optimized to reach them; both Smart Bidding and manual bidding use targets for metrics like **CPA** and **CPC** (→ [Course 2: Google Ads bid strategies](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
	- With Smart Bidding: **Target CPA** and **Enhanced CPC** strategies → Enhanced CPC combines manual CPC bidding with Target CPA or Target ROAS
- **Lower CPA to improve campaign value**:
	- **Cost per acquisition (CPA)**: the average cost paid for each conversion → use the historical average CPA as a performance goal, then optimize for a lower CPA
	- No historical data → use an **industry average** (typical values across an entire industry; e.g. CPA for auto industry search ads, CPA for Facebook ads in clothing, CPA for e-commerce)
	- Example: real estate firm, industry-average CPA for real estate search ads **$41.14** → target CPA **$40**; search ads budget **$10,000**
		- Number of conversions for improvement = Budget ÷ CPA = $10,000 ÷ $40 = **250** → if a conversion = requesting information about realty services, need at least 250 people to take that action; more than 250 → value above the industry average
- **Use daily spend to manage CPC**:
	- **Cost per click (CPC)**: a metric used in **pay-per-click (PPC)** advertising → manage CPC per campaign, allocate more budget to the highest-priority PPC campaigns
	- Example: national search ads, the northwest region has higher priority than the southwest; maximum CPC **$0.50** for both
		- Northwest: daily spend $200 ÷ $0.50 = **400 clicks**; southwest: $100 ÷ $0.50 = **200 clicks** → control daily spend during the campaign to manage outcomes per region
- Performance goals vary by campaign; cost figures are for illustration only

### Introduction to tools for marketing analytics
#### Marketing analytics skills
- Skills so far: contribute to marketing and ad strategies, create search, display, and social media ads, run email campaigns, expand customer reach, raise brand awareness, engage customers with content

- Software tools: mainly **Google Analytics** and **Google Ads** in this course; Google Ads can run tests on pages, ads, and target groups; tools like **Google Optimize** plug in to a website to test content options
- **A/B test** (also **split test** or **bucket test**): an online test of two variants that determines the better-performing option
	- E.g. two versions of a **direct response** → traffic split equally (**50%** randomly to each page) → one gets more clicks → deploy the direct response ad that got more clicks
- Teams choose tools based on capabilities, features, and cost → some for monitoring events (click analysis), visuals and graphics, dashboards; others for more sophisticated analytics → depends on organization, team, and project needs
	- Be open to new tools; know what a tool can or can't do → choose the metrics that work best for the project

#### Introduction to Google Analytics
- **Google Analytics demo account**: available to everyone with a Google account; live data from the **Google Merchandise Store** and **Flood-It!** (a gaming app in Google Play)
- **Property**: a website, mobile application, or web page associated with a unique **measurement ID** to enable metrics collection
	- An account contains one or multiple properties; a single property can combine metrics for a website and app; multiple properties useful for multiple websites/apps or very distinct user segments
	- **UA properties**: older version of Google Analytics (Universal Analytics), website metrics only; **GA4 properties**: collect metrics from both websites and mobile apps → new accounts should use GA4
	- Switch properties via the drop-down for the current view → Open
- **Attribution**: the act of assigning credit for conversions from ads, last clicks, or other touchpoints along a user's path to conversion completion ("gives credit where credit is due")
	- **Macro conversion**: typically a completed purchase transaction
	- **Micro conversion**: a completed response that indicates a potential customer is moving toward a macro conversion (the "other touchpoints")
	- **Attribution projects** organize both macro and micro conversions
- Reports in the GA4 property (→ [Course 7: Analytics tools in e-commerce](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/)):
	- **Reports snapshot**: tabs for all users, new users, average engagement time, total revenue; insights, user and traffic acquisition, user trends, top campaigns, most page views, top events, top conversions, top-selling products, conversions by platform
		- **Automated insights**: monitor spikes or unforecasted changes (e.g. an unforecasted spike in conversions)
	- **Realtime**: current user activity → users by device, geography, source, audience, page; events and conversions
	- **Life cycle**:
		- **Acquisition**: user and traffic acquisition (Acquisition overview)
		- **Engagement**: events, conversions, pages and screens → compare **begin_checkout** and **purchase** events to estimate how many users leave products in the cart
		- **Monetization**: website and in-app purchases
		- **Retention**: user retention and lifetime value over a **120-day** period
			- **User retention**: how many new users returned over a period of time
			- **Customer lifetime value**: the average revenue generated by customers over a certain period of time
	- **User**: demographics (country, city, gender, interests, age, language) and tech (platform, operating system, device, browser, screen resolution, app version, mobile device model)

#### Big data for marketing analytics and automation
- Marketing is changing with the rise of **big data**, **predictive analytics**, and **AI** → two trends for analytics and two for automation
- **Big data**: a field in analytics that systematically mines and extracts information from very large datasets for insights (also the large datasets themselves) → finance uses it for risk analysis, manufacturers to optimize supply chains
- Trends:
	1. **Real-time analytics**: monitors immediate data to gain insights to respond to events more quickly → marketers can adjust a campaign only as fast as they can monitor the data → e.g. adjust the message right away if a target audience isn't responding
	2. **Predictive analytics**: uses historical data to predict what might happen → models from collective browsing histories identify the right audience early; choose an optimal page or ad without an A/B test (saves time and money)
	3. **Autonomous marketing**: uses real-time analytics to automate marketing activities → e.g. adjust an underperforming message automatically; increases impact of multi-channel campaigns; effective for customer loyalty programs
	4. **Artificial intelligence (AI)**: a field developing intelligent machines and software that simulate human thought or work → help create and personalize content for multi-channel campaigns → context-specific experiences turn more browsers into buyers
- Automation and AI are a new standard: Google Ads **automated bidding**; **Smart Bidding** uses machine learning to analyze data in real time → the right message to the right customer at the right time
- New roles will open up in marketing → "the future in analytics is already here"
#### The history of cookies in advertising and e-commerce
- **Cookie**: a small file saved on computers to store user preferences and other information → first developed in **1994** to save users' settings; initially vendor-specific, tracked user sessions in early e-commerce
- Uses:
	- **Remarketing**: using ads or follow-up emails to engage a potential customer who visited a website but didn't make a purchase → relies on user information saved in cookies
	- **Analytics tools**: cookies record user session and device information → Google Analytics collects session start and traffic source information; Google Ads conversion counts rely on cookies
	- **Third-party data collection**:
		- **First-party cookie**: set by the website owner, uses the website's domain
		- **Third-party cookies**: distributed by third-party tools, use the advertiser's domain → e.g. delivered via a banner ad that persists across sites → information might not be anonymous and can be sold → major privacy concern; some third parties deliver hundreds of cookies on a first visit
- Alternatives for a cookie-less world:
	- **Interest-based advertising (IBA)**: relies on generalized browsing behaviors instead of specific individuals' → e.g. **Topics**: the browser uses categories based on interests and recent browsing history to let sites show relevant ads
	- **Trusted servers for website remarketing**: store certain information for a campaign bidding process, without third-party cookies
- Solutions must let marketers selectively engage users while maintaining their right to privacy (→ [Course 1: Data ethics](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/))

## Module 3: Measure the success of marketing campaigns
### Moving targets: Adjusting media and performance goals
#### Welcome to module 3
- Success is more than a good feeling → commonly measured by how well potential customers moved forward in the customer journey; **ultimate success = number of completed sales**
- Metrics on returns are critical (a business must make a profit): **return on investment (ROI)**, and for ad campaigns **return on ad spend (ROAS)**
- **A/B tests** (split tests, bucket tests) → determine which of two variants performs better → decisions that impact campaign success
- Case studies of campaigns with varying success → learn from best practices and from what went wrong
#### ROI and ROAS calculations
- **ROI**: a measure of the profit generated from a marketing campaign = ratio of **net income** (money made) to **investment** (money spent) (→ [Course 4: What is email marketing?](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
- Method 1: **ROI = (sales growth − marketing cost) ÷ marketing cost**
	- **Sales growth**: a positive increase in sales compared to prior sales (positive change in sales, **sales lift**)
	- E.g. sales growth $200,000, marketing cost $55,000 → (200,000 − 55,000) ÷ 55,000 = 145,000 ÷ 55,000 = **2.6**
	- ROI of **1** = break-even point (sales growth = marketing cost); > 1 is on the upside, the higher the better
- Method 2: **customer lifetime value (LTV)**: the average revenue generated per customer over a certain period of time
	- GA4 reports LTV for users acquired through different channels over an acquisition date range up to **120 days** → **Acquisition overview** report → an upward LTV trend indicates an increasing ROI
- **ROAS** = revenue generated ÷ amount spent on advertising (→ [Course 6: ROAS in product research](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
	- Channel ROAS targets set relative to an overall campaign-level ROAS: e.g. marketing goal **5:1** → **3:1** for search, **4:1** for display → per-channel results contribute to overall ROAS
- If ROAS isn't meeting the target (not a complete list):
	- **Lengthen the campaign duration**: measuring conversions is noisy, ROAS can temporarily dip due to market fluctuations → be patient; evaluate ROAS after a minimum of **50 conversions**
	- **Set ROAS targets by product groups**: e.g. a clothing store (evening gowns, dresses, tuxedos, suits) wanting **4:1** overall → split targets into formal and more casual wear → more accurate measurement
	- **Review how the ROAS target was set**: not based on previous campaigns or on overly optimistic margins → reset to a realistic number based on the data you now have
	- **Adjust the automated bidding strategy** (automated campaigns only)
- Measuring marketing ROI and ROAS → key metrics in marketing analytics (→ [Course 7: ROI and ROAS for products](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
#### Determine the ROI of a marketing project
- **ROAS = revenue generated ÷ ad spend** → short-term performance: did the campaign bring in more revenue than was spent?
- **LTV** → strategic question: did the campaign increase the **"stickiness"** of customers so they made additional purchases? → ROAS + LTV value both numeric and strategic aspects of ROI
- LTV time period can be years, quarters, or months (months most common in retail)
	- **Total LTV**: from the past to the present
	- **Predicted lifetime value (pLTV)**: includes future dates → becomes more accurate with each purchase and interaction; estimates impact before every sale comes through (e.g. use similar customer types' history for newly registered customers' first purchase)
	- Pro tip: use **total LTV** for completed campaigns, **pLTV** for campaigns still in progress
- **LTV by channel** (attribution by channel must be enabled in advance): **LTV = average order value (AOV) × purchase frequency** → compare channels for ROI by channel
- **LTV to CAC ratio**:
	- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)**: the average cost of acquiring a paying customer
	- **LTV:CAC = LTV ÷ CAC** → is the value of new customers enough to cover the cost to acquire them? Higher = better ROI
	- **≥ 2** normally good, **~3** ideal; **< 2** may be intentional (spending more to gain market share), otherwise cut budget spend to reduce CAC; **> 3** solid ROI, steady revenue stream → budget to expand (e.g. diversify products with adequately funded campaigns)
- **Marketing mix models** (media mix models): statistical models to predict the effectiveness and ROI of advertising spend → need at least **two years** of historical data; developed in the **1960s**, more reliable now with AI and machine learning; building them requires programming and statistics skills (→ [Tim: marketing mix modeling](#tim---use-marketing-analytics-data))

### Performing and analyzing results from A/B tests
#### Introduction to A/B tests
- Choices supported by data are the best kinds of decisions → an A/B test helps choose the best content or marketing strategy
- **A/B test** (split test, bucket test): an online experiment with two variants and a **random 50% split** of users between them → determine the better-performing option
	- E.g. randomly direct web traffic to two versions of the same web page → the version with higher performance on the chosen metrics wins (→ [Course 7: A/B testing a store](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
	- Typically performed on live web pages; possible on a non-live site if enough users are tested for a **statistically meaningful** result
	- Example: a direct response ad to join a **loyalty program** → version with a **celebrity endorsement** vs. version with a **savings offer** → more conversions with the savings offer
- A/B tests rely on **statistical tests**; which test depends on the metric type (→ [Data Science Course 4: Test your data](/blog/process-data-from-dirty-to-clean/)):
	- **Discrete metrics**: specific values, counts, or binary → CTR, conversion rate, bounce rate; new and returning user counts; clicked or didn't click
	- **Continuous metrics**: measured and change over time → revenue per user, average session duration, average order value
	- A/B testing software performs the statistical analysis on a large enough sample
- Usually testing whether a new version improves a metric vs. the original → document **past performance**, **desired improvement**, and the **performance metric** (organizations may have a template)
- Tools (not endorsements): **Google Ads** (ads), **Google Optimize** (website content), **HubSpot** (email messages and landing pages), **Optimizely** (touchpoints across the customer journey), **Intellimize** (personalizes webpages with machine learning, "continuous A/B testing")
- Benefits: greater confidence in meeting performance goals, more conversions, improved customer experiences from tested pages

#### How to plan for A/B testing
- **A/B testing plan**: structures the experiment by outlining key information and success metrics
- **Define the problem**: identify a specific problem or goal (low conversion rates, a new customer need) → start with small changes to get a baseline
- Elements of a plan:
	- **Hypothesis**: the "why," "what," and "how" + a prediction about the outcome; backed by research or data, focused on a single issue → describes the problem or insight, what you'll change, the expected impact
		- E.g. customers are more likely to click elements near the top of an email → changing the position of a link will boost the **click-to-open rate** by **15%** (→ [Course 4: Common email marketing metrics](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
		- Hard to write a compelling hypothesis → gather more data first
	- **Variants**: different versions of the content → **variant A** = original, **variant B** differs meaningfully; limit modifications to a single variant change (e.g. moving the link to the top, not also new CTA text and a button)
		- A "failed" test still provides valuable data → may need different variants
	- **Metrics**: decide how to measure results before testing → primary metric (click-to-open rate = percentage of recipients who clicked one or more links after opening) + others (conversion rate)
	- **Current performance and expected improvement**: define success (5%? 10%?) → ambitious but realistic according to the data
	- Other details: overview and purpose, channel (e.g. Google Ads), asset type (display ad, button copy), duration (start and end date), number of users per variant, **confidence level** (estimated probability that results would remain consistent if the test ran longer)

	1. Choose a variable to test
	2. Identify the goal of the test
	3. Clearly identify the **control** and the **variant**
	4. Verify it's the only A/B test running for the campaign or webpage
	5. Split sample groups randomly*
	6. Determine the required sample size*
	7. Decide on the confidence level for statistical significance* (*can be managed by the A/B testing platform)
	8. Select an A/B testing platform

	10. Run long enough to collect enough data
	11. Review results against the goal
	12. Decide on actions based on the results
	13. Plan additional A/B tests if required

	- **Multivariate test** (multivariable test): simultaneously tests variants of two or more elements on a page → the most effective variant for each element + how variants work together → best combination

#### Monitor A/B test results in Google Ads
- Statistical results in Google Ads experiments (→ [Data Science Course 4: Consider the margin of error](/blog/process-data-from-dirty-to-clean/)):
	- **Confidence level**: e.g. **95%** = running the same A/B test 100 times gives similar results 95 times → selected before data collection; most researchers use 95%, Google Ads default → needs a minimum number of users → run an experiment at least **four weeks**
	- **Margin of error**: the statistically calculated difference between the test result and the theoretical result with many more users
	- **Confidence interval**: the range of possible values = test result ± margin of error → e.g. 5% difference ± 2% → **3% to 7%**; Google Ads' "expected range of results" is the confidence interval
	- **Statistical significance**: whether a result could be due to random chance → the greater the significance, the less due to chance; Google Ads calculates at 95% and shows a **blue star (asterisk)** next to significant results
- How long to run: at least four weeks; online **sample size calculators** (e.g. AB Tasty: enter average daily visitors and number of variations → possible duration)
	- Account for normal swings in e-commerce sales (e.g. best on Sundays, worst on Saturdays → at least **two weeks**, preferably longer)
	- A sales promotion during a test can skew results → run longer; otherwise a variation may look better than it normally would

#### Glossary terms from module 3

- **Redirect testing**: A method of testing where two ads or webpages with different URLs are tested against each other to determine which yields better results

#### Create data visualizations in spreadsheets
- Advantage of charts in spreadsheets: copy and paste them into slides to present data
- Basic chart types (→ [Data Science Course 6: Design data visualization](/blog/share-data-through-the-art-of-visualization/)):
	- **Vertical column chart**: each measurement is a vertical column → how a metric changes (e.g. new users in January vs. February)
		- **Grouped column chart**: two metrics side by side (new users and returning visitors in January and February)
		- **Stacked column chart**: related metrics without the spread of a grouped chart (total users, users who abandon carts, users with completed purchases)
	- **Horizontal bar chart**: a column chart rotated 90° → useful when measurements differ greatly (e.g. a large spike in new users would make a column too tall); single, grouped, or stacked
	- **Line chart**: data points connected with a continuous line → how a metric changes over an extended period; single or stacked lines for two or more metrics
	- **Area chart**: like a line chart with the space under the line filled → single, unstacked, or stacked; **unstacked** areas have different shapes when data points don't align on the x-axis (measurements not collected on the same days, still showing a trend)
	- **Pie chart**: partial and whole results (e.g. percentage of sales per campaign, adding up to 100%)
	- **Histogram**: data points categorized into ranges, frequency shown by column height (e.g. online sales by age groups)
- Example: *Sorting and Filtering* spreadsheet, new users vs. total users per browser type → select A1:C11 → Insert → Chart (column chart by default) → Chart editor → Setup → Stacking: **Standard** → stacked column chart
	- Excel: select A1:C11 → Insert → choose the chart type icon
#### Data visualization in spreadsheets: charts
- Stakeholders should get the main points of a visualization **in a few seconds**
- **Avoid crowded charts**: e.g. percentage of conversions in each of the 50 US states → a column chart can't label all states, a pie chart has too many slices
	- **Stacked charts by region**: group states by region (**Northeast, Midwest, South, West**) → stacked column chart with 4 columns instead of 50
	- **Display top states and combine others**: show states with the largest percentages and group the rest (e.g. states with less than 3% of conversions) → a pie chart with 8 slices instead of 50 (Idaho, Louisiana, North Carolina, North Dakota, South Dakota, Texas, Utah + all others)
- Select the most appropriate chart type; sometimes try different charts or adjust the data for a more visually appealing chart

#### Make presentations accessible
- Design reports, presentations, and content for colleagues with disabilities (especially vision or hearing impairments); you won't always know when someone has a disability → prepare for every possibility
- Checklist:
	- **Visuals**: high-contrast color combinations (dark text on white, light text on dark); large text and easy-to-read fonts; **captions** on videos + a downloadable transcript; label important data directly instead of using a **legend**; don't rely solely on colors (use varying **patterns** in graphs)
	- **Designing the slides**: take your time when presenting to a group; **alternative text** on images and visuals; don't crowd slides (negative space)
	- **Presenting**: read all slide text clearly and slowly, call attention to visuals and key details; virtually → make sure everyone can hear you, use the caption feature; share the presentation so the audience can zoom in and revisit it; leave time for questions
- Accessibility is integral to quality presentations and a healthy working environment (→ [Course 3: Make social media posts accessible](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))

### Present insights to stakeholders
#### Create effective presentations
- Last step after monitoring metrics, preparing data, and creating visualizations: a **presentation** to share findings with stakeholders
- A presentation is like a map: GPS data gets people to a destination ↔ marketing analytics data leads the audience to your **conclusion**
	- Develop a clear **outline** (flow) → decide which data to use (the most relevant metrics that led to your insights) → too little data raises questions, too much loses focus
- Best practices for slides (→ [Data Science Course 6: Weave data into your presentation](/blog/share-data-through-the-art-of-visualization/)):
	- Progress from **general to specific** topics
	- State **insights** clearly and up front
	- **Repeat** the insights when presenting the supporting data
	- Introduce data visualizations with **titles that relate back to the insights**
	- Allow time for **questions**
	- End with a **call to action** (e.g. making a decision)
- Example: requesting an in-progress budget change → based on ongoing ROAS for two channels, **double the spend** on the more profitable channel by cutting the less profitable one (the conclusion)
	- Outline: summary of current spend → projected outcome if spend is adjusted → benefits of the change → lost opportunities if not → call to action
	- Data: ROAS for both channels, conversions attributed to each, conversion rates → support **display ads** as the more profitable investment
	- Visualization titles: "Higher spend for higher ROAS", "Spending where conversions happen"
#### Best practices for presenting data
- Tips (→ [Course 4: Tips on presenting](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/)):
	1. **Know your flow** (general → specific): background depends on the audience
		- **Executives**: brief, high-level background; focus on results and recommendations (forward thinking, improvement)
		- **Managers**: more interest in background (performance and target audiences of previous campaigns)
		- **Team members and individual contributors**: most freedom and time to go deeper
	2. **State insights up front**: satisfy the urge to skip to the ending; people who leave early still hear the insights
	3. **Introduce visualizations and repeat insights with metrics**: titles relay the main point (e.g. not "YouTube Spend by Competitors" but "We're spending 200% less on YouTube than competitors")
	4. **Allow enough time for questions**: be aware of timing (number of slides, time per slide), combine indistinct slides, split crowded slides (one insight or chart per slide)
	5. **End with a call to action**: a decision, process change, budget, recognition of a team → action items with deadlines
- Sample slide layout:
	- **Agenda** (most presentations 30 minutes to an hour): e.g. 30-minute agenda → team introductions (3 min), background (3), insights and recommendations (5), metrics and data (10), call to action and next steps (5), questions (4); insights can come before the background unless the background provides required context
	- **Background**: purpose and importance of the campaign or project
	- **Insights and recommendations**: possible to tell the story in one slide → emphasize by fading in one bullet at a time, showing only the relevant bullet, using arrows or callouts
	- **Metrics and data**: label data points with annotations or legends; prepare several ways to rephrase what charts show; as many slides as needed
	- **Call to action and next steps**: a simple, powerful summary + steps for the audience, with a timeline
- Best practices for presenting data: https://www.coursera.org/learn/assess-for-success/supplement/n0Y1e

#### Evaluate and improve presentations
- Design principles for visualizations in slides (→ [Data Science Course 6: Design visualizations in Tableau](/blog/share-data-through-the-art-of-visualization/)):
	- **Choose the best format**: a simple table may be best for executives or managers (big picture); more complex visualizations for technical teams
	- **Minimize distractions**: **data-ink ratio** = ink (or pixels) used for actual data ÷ total ink used in the visualization → maximize it (remove lines or boxes around charts and legends, text shadow effects)
	- **Use the appropriate orientation**: labels easy to read
	- **Choose colors thoughtfully**: red signals danger, green "go"; red-green combinations are problematic for color blindness → meaningful, consistent, inclusive colors
- Questions to evaluate visualizations:
	- Is the **scale** appropriate for order-of-magnitude comparisons? (changing the y-axis can exaggerate small differences)
	- Am I artificially **limiting the scope** of the data? (only favorable data is misleading)
	- Did I hide or misrepresent trends when **regrouping** data? (e.g. combining car loan and home loan trends makes insights inaccurate for both)
	- Do individual parts add up to the **whole**? (pie chart parts must equal 100%)
- Data visualization is a bit of an art form → improves with experience

#### Glossary terms from module 4
- **Area chart**: Represents data in the same way as a line chart, but with the space under the line filled in to form a visual area
- **Average session duration**: Reported in seconds, a calculation that divides the total duration of all sessions by the number of sessions
- **Cell**: A position in a spreadsheet with a column and row designation; for example, cell A2 is the unique position in column A and row 2
- **Dashboard**: A tool to track, analyze, and display KPIs, metrics, and insights dynamically based on interactive user criteria
- **Data-ink ratio**: The proportion of ink (or pixels in digital content) that is used to present actual data compared to the total amount of ink (or pixels) used in an entire visualization
- **Histogram**: Shows individual data points that have been categorized into ranges, with the frequency of each range represented by the height of a unique column
- **Horizontal bar chart**: A 90-degree rotation of a vertical column chart
- **Influence**: The degree to which a stakeholder can convince people to take certain actions
- **Interest**: The degree to which a project informs or impacts a stakeholder's objectives
- **Line chart**: A chart in which individual data points for a changing variable are connected with a continuous line
- **Pie chart**: A chart that shows data with partial and whole results
- **Pivot table**: A visualization that changes the view of data in a spreadsheet to a different perspective to categorize it or to identify an insight or trend (without changing the data itself)
- **Power grid**: (refer to stakeholder map)
- **Stakeholder**: Someone with an interest in or a concern for a project and its results
- **Stakeholder map**: A grid with four quadrants and two variables—interest and influence—that can be used to keep track of the influence and needs of stakeholders and the level of communication required to work with them
- **Structured query language (SQL)**: The standard language used to communicate with databases developed by different vendors and hosted on multiple platforms
- **Vertical column chart**: A chart in which individual measurements are each shown as a vertical column
