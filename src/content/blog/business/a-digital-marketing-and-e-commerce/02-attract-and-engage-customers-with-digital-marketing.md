### Create customer personas for your target audience
#### Welcome to module 1
- Module 1 roadmap: **customer persona** (a representation of an ideal customer for a business) → how to research and create multiple personas → overview of the **marketing funnel** (attract customers who don't know a brand → turn them into repeat customers and brand advocates)
	- **Awareness**: get customers introduced to the brand
	- **Consideration**: build interest in potential customers
	- **Conversion**: customers are ready to purchase → build trust so they become paying customers
	- **Loyalty**: encourage paying customers to become repeat customers and brand advocates
- Case studies on how businesses apply strategies at each stage
#### Customer personas for your target audience
- Before creating marketing materials, you need to know who you're marketing to → define your **ideal customer**
- **Customer persona** (also **buyer persona**, **marketing persona**, **audience persona**): represents a group of similar people in a desirable audience → reach people at the right time with the right message, offer, and products
	- Simple version includes:
		- **Who** the person represents (characteristics)
		- **Goal**: what they want to achieve
		- **Barrier** to reaching their goal (**pain point**)
	- Marketers usually create several personas (some businesses have over 10), because most businesses have more than one type of customer; personas can get complex (interests, behaviors, needs, skills)
- Why create customer personas:
	- Understand the **customer's perspective**: more specific than a **target audience** (broad and general) → consider the customer's priorities (which message or visuals appeal, do they help reach goals or relate to pain points)
	- Easier to create **customized content**: how they think, what they want, what holds them back from purchasing
	- Effective **ad targeting** (**targeted advertising**): most digital advertising platforms offer targeting tools
- Example: company selling camping tents, campaign for a **six-person tent**
	- Who: parent or guardian, mid-30s to mid-50s, two to three children
	- Goal (from in-person interviews): a budget-friendly tent that lasts several years
	- Barrier: concern about setting up a large tent due to a frustrating prior experience
	- → Online ad: "easy to set up tent for the whole family", targeted to people who fit the demographic profile
#### How to create a customer persona
- Don't guess: you need actual data → research methods:
	1. **Review customer data**: existing data (demographics, location, sales history, customer service notes)
	2. **Conduct customer interviews**: what brought them to the product, how it solved their problem, other interests
	3. **Analyze web data**: demographic data about social media followers; website analytics like **Google Analytics** (age, gender of visitors); online reviews and comments
	4. **Send out surveys**: email surveys to current customers; participation may be low → give away something for free to one respondent
- Step 1 – define **who** the customer is: interests, traits, and **demographics** (age, gender identity, income, family size, occupation, education, location)
	- E.g. pet business: a man in his 30s with two children who loves the outdoors and lives in the suburbs → more specific: a 32-year-old man with two children who owns a large dog and likes hiking
	- More detailed personas → usually more personas → advertising language aligned to many audience members
- Step 2 – **goals**: rely on collected data (surveys, interviews); goal must relate to the product or service
	- Landscaping example: great looking greenery; peace of mind that plants and property are taken care of; pride in the look of their landscaping (you can infer additional details, e.g. pride in their home)
- Step 3 – **barriers**: what prevents the customer from achieving their goal, or from hiring the company
	- Landscaping: no time to take care of the lawn; tried but lawn doesn't look good; no equipment; don't trust landscaping companies after poor service in the past
- Combine into personas (landscaping):
	- A 55-year-old woman in the city with a small yard and a garden, cared for her own yard for five years but no longer has time
	- A 30-year-old new homeowner who just moved to the suburbs, never worked with landscaping
	- A 27-year-old budget-conscious renter in a rural area keeping up with homeowners' association demands
- Best practice: keep personas readily available; for any marketing material ask: who are you trying to reach, what visuals and messages appeal to them, what online platforms do they spend time on?
#### Learn to create customer personas
- **Customer personas**: fictional users whose goals and characteristics represent the needs of a larger group of users → focus marketing on people most likely to buy
	- Businesses create multiple personas; each represents an individual from one **target group** (a set of people with similar interests, goals, or concerns)
- Simple template with three components:
	- **Who**: short description (name, age, location, household, education) + a photo
	- **Goals**: what the person wants to achieve (several related goals)
	- **Barrier**: a pain point that prevents them from achieving their goals
	- Optional: personality, hobbies, values, lifestyle
- **Researching your customers**: demographics (age, location, household, education, occupation) + goals and barriers → analytics tools, customer interviews, surveys, focus groups
	- Example questions: age, education level, where do you live, whom do you live with, occupation, primary activities on a workday/weekend, challenges, what you value most, goals (+ questions tailored to your products or services)
	- Organize the data: find trends, group similar answers (e.g. a spreadsheet with Name, Age, Location, Occupation, Education, Values: Francis, 26, Suburbs, Store manager, High school, Community involvement; ...) → create a fictional persona for each target group
- Examples (camping tents company):
	- **Persona 1 – Antonio Weber**: 35-year-old dad with three young kids, electrician with an associate's degree, suburbs
		- Goals: share a love for the outdoors with his kids; a large, budget-friendly tent that lasts several years
		- Barrier: setting up the tent alone (kids too young to help)
	- **Persona 2 – Juliana Soto**: 50-year-old parent with two college-age daughters, insurance agent with a bachelor's degree, city
		- Goals: an affordable month-long family road trip across the United States; a waterproof tent for all weather
		- Barrier: concern about the tent leaking in a rain storm (prior experience)
- Personas based on research help you approach work from the customer's point of view (marketing messages, brand voice, email and social media campaigns)

### Awareness and consideration marketing funnel stages
#### Introduction to the marketing funnel and its benefits
- **Marketing funnel**: a process to take people who are unfamiliar with your products or services and turn them into paying customers → help a business accomplish its goals (profit, revenue, new customers, ...)
	- Reach and resonate with target audiences in all stages of the **customer journey**
	- Just a **framework**: many kinds exist (e.g. a six-stage funnel); this course uses a **four-stage funnel**: **awareness, consideration, conversion, loyalty**
- Benefits:
	- **Consider the buyer's journey**: instead of random strategies or only what drives sales at the bottom of the funnel → when do they first experience the brand, how, and what do they do after?
	- **Optimize your marketing strategy**: find and fix gaps (e.g. display ads not connecting, or a landing page that loads slowly)
	- **Increase the conversion rate** and drive business results: e.g. 100 people visit a product page, 1 purchases → **1% conversion rate**
- Main goal: a great experience for the customer + more revenue for the business; learn what does and doesn't move customers through the funnel
#### Awareness: Strategies to get customers introduced to a brand
- **Awareness stage**: the potential customer first becomes aware of the product or service (has a pain point, e.g. buying new furniture, or encounters the brand without searching, e.g. a Facebook or Instagram ad) → the first impression counts
- **Strategies vs. tactics** (often used interchangeably):
	- **Strategies**: support the plan to achieve the marketing goal; more general ideas (e.g. social media marketing)
	- **Tactics**: actions taken to make the plan happen (e.g. actions on social media)
- Strategies and tactics to build awareness:
	- **Search engine optimization (SEO)**: improving a website to increase its visibility in a search engine (Google Search, Microsoft Bing)
	- **Search engine marketing (SEM)**: increase a website's visibility in a search engine through **paid advertisement**
	- **Display ads**: graphic-based advertising (photos, videos, or text) on websites and applications your potential customers visit
	- **Social media marketing**: posts on social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn), typically at no cost; e.g. a follower shares the brand's post to their followers
	- **Social media advertising**: paid; e.g. ads shown in non-followers' feeds, targeted by interests
	- **Video marketing**: e.g. a YouTube ad that plays before a video, targeted by previously watched videos
	- **Influencer marketing**: partner with a person or brand with a large following who promotes your product for a fee; e.g. a **review** (video, photo, or text) → the influencer must **disclose the relationship**; follow regulatory guidelines
	- **Content marketing**: creating and distributing valuable content (e.g. a downloadable e-book, an educational webpage); often tied with SEO/SEM → creating content is the first step, then get it in front of potential customers
#### How a business creates awareness for their products
- In the awareness stage, customers don't know the brand and aren't seeking its products → the business must introduce them
- Example: fictional company **Lirio Cases** (premium leather phone cases)
	- **SEM**: their website appears on the first page of Google and Bing for "leather iphone case" but near the bottom → search ads with **Google Ads** and **Microsoft Advertising** to appear at the top (e.g. for "black leather iphone case")
	- **Social media marketing**: large Instagram following; high-quality photos pairing cases with pens, wallets, watches → style and fashion recommendations give new customers a reason to follow
	- **Social media advertising**: target based on previously created customer personas; reuse the new high-quality photos → reaches new customers, grows Facebook and Instagram following
	- **Influencer marketing**: pay an influencer with a large following → tens of thousands of new potential customers; the post promotes a **15% discount** on the next 200 purchases
- Reaching potential customers is only the first step → then build interest and persuade them to purchase
- In practice you build one strategy at a time; a small to medium-sized business may focus on only **two or three** strategies; in larger companies marketers specialize (e.g. SEM or social media marketing)
- How a business creates awareness for their products: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/9onaR
#### Case study: Okabashi uses marketing strategies to attract customers and build interest
- **Awareness**: customers haven't heard of or encountered the brand; **Consideration**: customers know the brand but aren't ready to purchase
- Company background: **Okabashi Brands**, family-owned shoe company in Buford, Georgia since **1984**; run by **Sara Irvani** since 2017; colorful, moldable flip-flops and sandals designed for foot health; factory over 100,000 square feet
	- Value: **sustainability** → each pair made of ~**25% recycled** products from its own **closed-loop manufacturing** process; recycle old sandals → 15% off next purchase
- Challenge: reaching new customers and building interest (building its pipeline of new customers)
- Approach: purpose-driven language (local-based, made in the USA, women-owned, sustainably produced)
	- **Awareness**:
		- **Google Ads** (Search ads and Shopping ads): very specific about the product, even the color (e.g. "coral" instead of "pink") → display for specific, targeted searches
		- Facebook and Instagram ads: lifestyle-based, promoting the general brand
		- **Influencer marketing**: hand-written notes + a free product to over **100 influencers** → key: the influencer's audience must be interested in this type of product
	- **Consideration**:
		- **Remarketing**: products viewed on the website are remarketed through ads on Google Ad supported websites, Facebook, and Instagram
		- **Email collaborations**: with non-competitor brands sharing similar values (e.g. made in the USA) → each promotes the other to its email list
		- **Trust**: reviews on its **Google Business Profile**; sharing its company story on social media, email, and product pages
- Results: over **35 million pairs** of shoes sold
- Influencer marketing and brand partnerships are **relationship-based** strategies → win-win when done well
- Case study: Okabashi uses marketing strategies to attract customers and build interest: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/uQ3S5
#### Consideration: Strategies to build interest in your product or service
- **Consideration stage**: build interest; the potential customer knows the product and is ready to learn more
	- Awareness strategies still apply (social media marketing, content marketing), but the content is more **in-depth and persuasive** (e.g. a case study) and ad targeting focuses on **previous visitors**
- Strategies and tactics:
	- **Unique selling proposition (USP)**: explains why a product or service is better than the competition → make it clear and repeat it often
	- **Testimonials**: customers share their experience (written, audio, or video) → build trust; the prospect imagines themselves as the satisfied customer
	- **Case studies**: like testimonials but more in-depth, often written (e.g. projects lasting several months)
	- **Remarketing ads**: delivered to previous purchasers, subscribers, or visitors of a brand's website or social media (e.g. shoes you viewed follow you on other webpages or apps)
	- **Webinars**: online presentations, typically educational → educate and promote; challenge: getting people to attend → offer something enticing and of value
	- **Email marketing**: promotional or educational emails to an email list (educational emails, testimonials for those who haven't purchased)
	- **Social media marketing**: prospects are now followers → post consistently with varied content
#### How a marketer increases consideration for a business
- Consideration: help potential customers understand how the product solves their problems → more in-depth and persuasive information than awareness
- Lirio Cases' strategies:
	- **Remarketing ads**: e.g. visitor views the leather iPhone case product page → ads for that product on Instagram → link back to the website or to the Instagram account
	- **Email marketing**: sign up for the email list for a **15% first order discount**; most don't buy immediately → weekly emails about leather products + the brand's story (how it started, quality leather, sustainability)
	- **Testimonials**: on product pages, social media, and periodically in emails → confidence in product quality
	- **Unique selling proposition**: "Phone cases with premium leather and design that can go everywhere you do." → stated clearly and often throughout the website and marketing materials
- Strategies vary: Lirio Cases' products are under **$80**; higher-priced products may need webinars, case studies, free trials → the higher the price, the longer it takes to build interest
- How a marketer increases consideration for a business: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/A0Xim
### Conversion and loyalty marketing funnel stages
#### Conversion: Strategies to increase the conversion rate on a website
- **Conversion**: the process to get a potential customer to take a **desired action** (sign up for a consultation, come into the store, an online purchase, ...)
	- Obstacles: difficulty with checkout, distraction, still undecided → the marketer/e-commerce analyst ensures smooth checkout and increases the likelihood, volume, or total value of purchases
- **Conversion rate**: the percentage of users or website visitors that take a desired action
	- Typical e-commerce conversion rate: around **1–2%** (e.g. 2 of 100 visitors purchase = 2%)
	- From 2 to 3 of 100 → business revenue **+33%** with a simple website change
- Strategies and tactics:
	- **Optimize the checkout process**: every additional **field** (any information the customer must enter, e.g. phone number) or page may decrease conversion → remove unneeded fields; "add to cart" → "buy now"; more payment options (Google Pay, Shopify's Shop Pay)
	- **Better product photos**: high-quality, varied angles; **lifestyle photos** (someone actually using the product); encourage customers to add lifestyle photos to reviews
	- **Strengthen the copy**: **copy** = any written material that encourages a customer to buy → explain the product + make an **emotional appeal**
	- **Live chat**: answer checkout or product questions without the customer leaving; small businesses can use **chatbots** (software that answers basic questions)
	- **Pursue abandoned carts**: customer starts checkout, enters email, doesn't buy → reminder email (many e-commerce softwares do this) + remarketed ads
	- **Increase website speed**: per *The State of Online Retail Performance*, every **second of delay** in page load → conversions can fall by **20%** (e.g. reduce image size)
	- **Offer a trial or money-back guarantee**: common for expensive purchases (mattresses, glasses, clothes)
	- **A/B tests**: compare two versions of a webpage to determine which has the highest conversion rate; change **one element at a time** (e.g. product photo with white background vs. outside vs. lifestyle setup); applies beyond e-commerce
- Keep the **return rate** in mind: higher conversion doesn't help if returns also go up
#### How a marketer increases the conversion rate of an e-commerce website
- Lirio Cases' conversion strategies:
	- **Pursue abandoned carts**: follow-up email reminding customers of products left in the cart; review the whole checkout flow (e.g. too many information fields) → reduce the abandoned cart percentage
	- **Add better photos**: professional photographer for updated product photos + **lifestyle photos** (at home, at work, outdoors; represent the diversity of users) + mini animations by a graphic designer explaining special features
	- **Strengthen the copy**: from features only → **emotional appeal** (how the customer's life might be different); rework the **FAQ** page with common email questions (e.g. how to care for the leather case)
	- **A/B tests**: test photos, copy, placement of product reviews and product recommendations → conversion rate **+0.8%** → **$150,000** increase in yearly sales revenue
- Next: turn customers into repeat customers and loyal brand advocates
- How a marketer increases the conversion rate of an e-commerce website: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/6FxrC
#### Loyalty: Strategies to increase the loyalty of customers after a purchase
- **Loyalty stage**: customers become **repeat customers** and **brand advocates** (voices of the brand: tell family and friends, share on social media, actively seek out the service)
	- Not strictly necessary (you already earned the customer), but leads to repeat customers, higher conversion rate or conversion value, and free marketing
- Post-purchase strategies and tactics:
	- **Reward programs**: incentivize customers to come back (Starbucks, Chipotle, McDonald's apps); many **point-of-sale (POS) systems** (accept payments, manage customer information) have built-in reward programs
	- **Email marketing**: keeps the brand in front of the customer; always provide value (or they unsubscribe) → educate or tell the brand story
	- **Social shares**: customers share your product to their followers → first encourage them to follow, then deliver shareable content
	- **Remarketing** to paying customers: recommend another product or add-ons
	- **Encourage positive reviews** (social media, email) → reuse as content on the website, social media, emails
	- **Birthday discounts** or discounts on the anniversary of becoming a customer
	- **Freebies**: sticker, magnet, t-shirt (e.g. a complimentary branded sleep mask with a pillow)
	- **Amusing confirmation messages**: add personality to confirmation emails/texts, describe how they'll feel receiving the product
#### How a marketer increases the loyalty of customers post-purchase
- **Brand advocates**: voices of the brand who recommend it to family and friends → passionate recommendations that cost no additional marketing dollars
- Lirio Cases' loyalty strategies:
	- **Social shares**: Instagram content on style and fashion advice + **monthly product giveaways** to one customer who tags their account in a post about a recent purchase
	- **Encourage reviews**: reviews persuade prospects and also build loyalty in the reviewer → follow-up email asking for a review **one week after delivery**
	- **Send freebies**: branded sticker, small magnet, instructional card on leather care → public display of the brand (fridge, laptop, water bottle) turns customers into advocates
	- **Remarket other products**: leather watch bands, wallets, tablet cases → e.g. bought a brown leather phone case → ads for a matching brown leather wallet
- Loyal promoters = a low-cost or no-cost way to get more sales

## Module 2: Understand search engine optimization (SEO)
### How search works
#### Welcome to module 2
- Getting your website found in search engines is a really important aspect of digital marketing
- Module 2 roadmap: how the Google search engine works and determines rankings → structure of **search engine results pages (SERPs)** → **search engine optimization (SEO)** (the practice of getting a website found in a SERP) → pre-steps → first steps: **keyword research** and **website structure**
- Coach AK's story: SEO was confusing at first; after several months a friend found his article and image while searching for advice → now a Google search of his name shows dozens of images, content, and videos → online trust and credibility
#### How does the Google search engine work?
- **Search engine**: any software that locates information on a search query (e.g. Google, Amazon, Etsy search engines; each works slightly differently but concepts apply to all)
	- Search content in this course is public (Google's official search documentation)
- Three main processes of Google Search:
	1. **Crawling**: finding new and updated webpages with automated programs called **crawlers** → page URLs stored in a big list to review later
		- **Page URL**: a web address, e.g. `www.example.com/blog`
		- Main discovery method: following **links** (any web addresses on a webpage) from pages already identified (within the site or from another site)
		- Crawlers check whether the website owner allows crawling
	2. **Indexing**: storing crawled pages in an **index** (like a book index: content + its location, the URL) → text, photos, video content
		- Owners can block crawling/indexing/appearing in Google Search in the website's code
	3. **Serving**: the Google Search **algorithm** (automated software that helps locate information to answer a query) sorts billions of webpages to deliver the most relevant results
		- Considers many factors (on-site and on other websites), e.g. location and language; five key factors in the next video
- **Algorithm** in general: a set of instructions for solving a problem or accomplishing a task (e.g. a recipe)
	- E-commerce search algorithm: the products a shopper is most likely to buy (price, shipping time)
	- Social media algorithm: what's most entertaining (popularity, content length)
#### How Google determines website rankings
- Five key factors for ranking results in SERPs (concepts, open to interpretation):
	1. **Meaning of the query**: **query** = the words typed into the search bar → establish the **intent**; Google uses **language models** → marketers should understand searchers' intent (→ build customer personas)
	2. **Relevance of webpages**: basic signal = the page contains the same **keywords** as the query (in headings or body text)
		- **Keyword**: a word or multiple words people use to find information, products, or services online
		- Algorithms also match phrases and topics (e.g. "dogs" → dog breeds, dog photos, not "dog" repeated); different content types may be relevant (video vs. text, photos)
	3. **Quality of content**: e.g. whether prominent websites link or refer to the content; factors capture the bigger picture rather than technical switches; refined with aggregated feedback from a **search quality evaluation** process
	4. **Usability of webpages**: with the same content, the page with better user experience may perform better → **page experience ranking factor** (mobile friendly, loads quickly)
	5. **Context and settings**: location, past search history, search settings
		- E.g. "football" in Chicago → American football, Chicago Bears; in London → soccer, Premier League
		- Results tailored by Google account activity (e.g. "events near me"), but not designed to infer sensitive characteristics
#### Breakdown of the Google search engine results pages (SERPs)
- **SERPs** appear whenever someone performs a search query; features differ by search type (product → shopping; timely news → news articles)
	- Best practice: Google your product or service by brand name and related words → analyze possible placements
	- **Paid ads** usually at the top (and sometimes at the bottom), then **search listings**
- Common **SERP features**:
	- **Featured snippet**: a special box displaying information about a search; non-paid; you can mark a page as a featured snippet but Google systems decide
	- **Rich results**: specific information that makes a website display with additional features (e.g. product review rating, price, availability)

### Understand the basics of search engine optimization (SEO)
#### What is SEO and why is it important?
- **Search engine optimization (SEO)**: the process of increasing the visibility of website pages on search engines in order to attract more **relevant traffic** (the ideal customers you'd like to visit) → the non-paid process
	- "SEO" can also be a job title ("We just hired a new SEO")
	- Worth learning even on platforms like Blogger, Wix, or Squarespace
- Why important: SEO makes it easier for algorithms to crawl, index, and understand content → if a site can't be crawled or understood, it may not be found
	- Often small modifications to parts of a website → large combined impact
	- **Google Search Console**: tool to monitor, maintain, and troubleshoot a website's presence in Google results (covered later)
	- Many opinions on SEO: Google publishes guidelines; implementation is up to the marketer/company; many paths to visibility, no need to match other sites one to one
- Common SEO tasks:
	- **Review website content or structure**: a well-organized website that is easy to crawl
	- **Technical website development** (advanced): hosting, webpage redirects, error pages, **JavaScript** (a programming language), page load speed
	- **Content development**: anything a visitor experiences (text, videos, photos) + **user experience**
	- **Keyword research**: finding terms and phrases potential customers type into search engines → often combined with content development to inform content strategy; no perfect way to do it
#### First steps before implementing SEO
- Pre-work for two groups: the **organization** requesting SEO and the **potential website visitors** → otherwise work may not align with organizational goals, or may not connect with (or even turn away) visitors
- Factors to consider before any SEO task:
	1. **Know the website or organization's goals**: more sales, emails, or phone calls? → work on tasks that positively impact these goals (you may influence the goals; e.g. if email collection leads to sales → prioritize content for email collection)
	2. **Know the customers well** (→ customer personas): visitors who don't connect with the text or visuals simply leave; know the visitor's **intent** and help them fulfill it
	3. **Brainstorm content for people first**: great SEO content marketers create content for **people first, Google second** → what does the searcher need, is information missing, should it be more visual (photos, video) even if all results are text?
	4. **Know your competitors well**: to rank higher, create content **better** than the competition ("better" may mean video instead of text, more in-depth content like a story or case study, or shorter, more concise content) → study what they did and do it better

### Keyword research and website structure
#### Keyword research and recommendations
- Before creating webpage content, know the terms potential customers type into Google (e.g. a page about a couch: couch, sofas, sectionals, lounges, or combined "sofa sectional"?) → content that connects → more likely to take a desired action
- **Keywords**: the search terms people use to find information, products, or services online
- **Keyword research**: a process to find the terms people use in search engines
	- No single correct way; each organization has its own process (partly opinion); best learned on the job
	- Tools provide data on **how many times** people search for keywords and **how competitive** a keyword is
- **Keyword stuffing** (not recommended by Google's SEO quality guidelines): loading a webpage with keywords or numbers to manipulate ranking → often in a list or group, out of context, unnatural → negative user experience, can harm ranking
	- Examples: blocks of text listing cities and states the page tries to rank for; repeating words unnaturally ("We sell custom candles. Our custom candles are handmade. If you're thinking about buying a custom candle, contact a custom candle specialists.")
	- Instead: useful, information-rich content using keywords appropriately and in context
- Keyword research before content is as crucial as customer personas before marketing materials; **keywords are the topics of your webpage**

#### Organize your website’s pages: Website structure and navigation
- **Site structure**: how webpages are linked and connected, and how a visitor navigates the website (generally **technical SEO**; large companies have departments for website architecture and user experience)
	- Small business: ~10 pages, easy; e-commerce with thousands of products: complicated
	- Good structure is **hierarchical** (pages fall under and are associated with another page), e.g. product categories on a home improvement site: hardware, electrical, plumbing
	- Should be a **flat structure**: every page only **three, maybe four clicks** from the homepage
- Reasons to plan the structure:
	- **Assist search engine crawlers**: crawlers discover pages mainly via links; a page with no links may take longer to be discovered; also via a **sitemap** (important for larger websites)
	- **Consistent and readable URL structure**: avoid extremely long cryptic URLs; URLs may display in Google results near the title
	- **Improved user experience**: trouble navigating → users leave → higher **bounce rate** (percent of visitors who view one page and leave) → they leave without converting
- Tips:
	- Use **HTTPS** (`https://`) for every URL: an Internet communications protocol that protects the integrity and confidentiality of data between the user's computer and the site (vs. older HTTP)
	- Create a **navigational page**: a simple page displaying the site structure (hierarchical listing of all pages), mainly for human visitors
	- Show useful **404 pages**: a URL telling the user the page doesn't exist (from a broken link or wrong URL) → custom 404 page with a link to the homepage or popular content
	- Make navigation **crawler friendly**: all pages reachable via links (e-commerce: category page → subcategory pages → product pages)
#### Site hierarchy best practices
- An organized site hierarchy → easier crawling and indexing, simple readable URLs, better SEO, customers find what they want
- **Planning your site hierarchy**:
	- **Homepage** (**root page**): top level, general information about the business or products
	- **Subpages**: lower-level pages with more specific information; three or four levels; every page no more than three or four clicks from the homepage
	- E-commerce example (online bookstore): homepage (current deals, new releases, bestsellers) → **category pages** (fiction, nonfiction, children's books) → **subcategory pages** (under children's books: chapter books, picture books, graphic novels, boxed sets, ...) → **product detail pages** (a specific children's book title)
- **Breadcrumbs**: a row of internal links at the top or bottom of the page to navigate back to a previous section or the homepage → start with the homepage and include each level up to the current page (homepage > category > subcategory > ...)
- **Simple, descriptive URLs**: text describes the page content → visitors understand, search engines understand
	- Keep URLs as **short** as possible (long URLs overwhelm; short ones are easier to share)
	- Make URLs **easy to understand**, e.g. `https://www.booksgalore.com/fiction/mystery` (fictional); use words, not cryptic numbers; avoid generic words like "page1" or excessive keywords like "books-fiction-books-fictionbooks"
	- Keep URLs **updated** (avoid **broken links** after pages are deleted or moved)
	- Create useful **404 pages**: vague messages lead to a dead end; custom 404 pages guide visitors back to a working page

### Optimize a website’s content for search engine optimization
#### Welcome to module 3
- Applying SEO: create SEO-friendly content so potential customers find it when they search
- Module 3 roadmap: optimize a website's content → internal linking → optimize images → mobile-friendly websites → help search engines understand content (titles, meta descriptions, structured data markup, unique content) → **Google Search Console** (SEO reports and metrics) and **Bing Webmaster Tools**
#### Optimize a website's content
- SEO comes down to creating **great content**: well-written, informative, on topic → satisfy the searcher's query better than the competition
- Five content recommendations from Google's SEO documentation (no guarantee of ranking):
	1. **Make the website useful and interesting**: compelling, useful content influences the website more than any other factor → users engage, share, and direct others → builds reputation with visitors and Google
	2. **Know what visitors want and give it to them**: starts with keyword research → well-written, easy to follow, no spelling or grammar errors; **fresh, unique content** (don't rehash old content; duplicate or similar pages are likely not shown)
	3. **Act in a way that cultivates user trust**: reputation for expertise; information about who publishes the site, context, goals; clear customer service information for e-commerce; proper technologies (a checkout page without a **secure connection** can't be trusted)
	4. **Make expertise and authoritativeness clear**: content created or edited by people knowledgeable in the topic, or cite expert/experienced sources
	5. **Provide an appropriate amount of content**: high-quality content takes time, effort, expertise, and talent or skill → factually accurate, clearly written, comprehensive (e.g. a complete, easy-to-follow recipe, not just ingredients)
#### Best practices for links and promoting a website
- Two main types of links:
	- Links **on** your website: **internal link** (to another page within the website, e.g. hotel list → specific hotel page) or **external link** / **outbound link** (to a page on another website, e.g. the hotel's official website)
	- Links **pointing to** your website: **backlinks** (**inbound links**), e.g. an article on the best vacation packages linking to your travel planning site
- **Anchor text**: the visible text in a hyperlink (e.g. "Learn more") → helps visitors navigate and Google understand the linked page; should be:
	- **Descriptive**: avoid generic "page", "article", "click here"; usually avoid the URL itself
	- **Concise**: a few words or a short phrase, not a whole paragraph
	- **Easy to spot**: formatting that stands out (not only visible on mouse-over)
	- **Helpful**: no lengthy anchor text just for search engines, no keyword stuffing
- **Use caution with external links**: avoid unreputable sites or information you don't endorse; spam links in comments, message boards, or third-party apps (e.g. live chat)
	- **Nofollow links**: links with HTML code telling search engines to ignore them → automatically use nofollow for all user comments and messages
- **Promoting your website**: Google analyzes the **quality and quantity** of sites linking to a website → best way to attract backlinks: quality content others want to link to (gradual process)
	- Ideas: blog posts about new content or services; share quality content on social media; feature helpful articles (e.g. buying guides) in email marketing; connect with related online communities (forums, message boards, social media groups); only share content that interests users
	- **Paid links**: Google warns against them unless for advertising and properly labeled → buying/selling links to manipulate results can hurt ranking and bring low-quality backlinks
- More: Google Search Central, how to use links wisely
- Best practices for links and promoting a website: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/5ewoi
#### Optimize images for a website
- Images in Google SERPs mostly come from websites → website images can appear in **Google Images** results; recommendations are for users, not just search engines
- Image best practices:
	1. **Great user experience**: visual content relevant to the page topic, only where it adds original value; place images prominently near relevant text (most important image near the top); **device-friendly** (computer, tablet, mobile; not cut off on mobile)
	2. **Descriptive titles, captions, and filenames**: Google auto-generates a title and snippet → help with a descriptive page title and captions; filename gives clues (`my-new-grey-kitten.jpg` vs `IMG0023.jpg`)
	3. **Descriptive alt text**: **alt text** = text that describes an image → accessibility (screen readers, low bandwidth); Google uses it with **computer vision algorithms** and page content
		- Useful, information-rich, keywords used appropriately and in context; no keyword stuffing (may be recognized as spam)
		- Bad: "puppy dog baby dog pups doggies litter retriever Labrador Dalmatian terrier cheap dog food puppy food" → Good: "Dalmatian puppy playing fetch"
	4. **Optimize for speed**: images are often the largest contributor to page size → resize without losing quality; **PageSpeed Insights** (free Google tool) analyzes load time and suggests fixes (e.g. which images load slowly)
#### Make your website mobile-friendly
- **Mobile-friendly**: functional on a smartphone; in many countries smartphones outnumber personal computers; in the US, **95%** of smartphone users search for local information on their phones
- Why: non-mobile-friendly sites require pinch/zoom → frustration → users abandon; mobile-friendly = readable and immediately usable
	- Many themes and builders (e.g. **Wix**) automatically make content mobile-friendly → always check the mobile version
- What to know when building:
	- **Make it easy for customers to use**: consider their objectives (be entertained, find a restaurant's address, read reviews) → outline the steps of the customer journey (e.g. search for a lamp → click a website → browse lamps → purchase)
	- **Measure effectiveness** by how easily mobile customers complete common tasks → list common tasks, have several people test them; consistent interface, unified experience across platforms
	- **Use a responsive web design**: the same code on desktop, tablet, and mobile, display adjusts to screen size → recommended by Google; maintain only one version of the site
- Top three beginner mistakes:
	- **Forgetting the mobile customer**: mobile-formatted but not fully functional; be inclusive for people who do most tasks on a smartphone
	- **Implementing the mobile site on a different domain**: separate mobile URLs increase maintenance and technical problems → responsive design serves desktop and mobile on the same URL
	- **Working in isolation**: review competitors' sites for inspiration and best practices

### Help Google (and users) understand your content
#### Craft effective titles and meta descriptions
- You control the page name in the results (**title link**) and have some control over the description
	- **Webpage title element**: provides users and search engines with a page's topic (a few words or a phrase)
	- **Meta description**: provides search engines a summary of what the page is about (a sentence or two, or a short paragraph)
- Page title recommendations:
	- **Accurately describe** the page's content, read naturally; no unrelated titles or default text ("Untitled", "New Page 1") → otherwise searchers click back to the SERP
	- **Unique title** for every page (e.g. not "Services" for all; name the specific service)
	- **Brief but descriptive**: too long or less relevant (e.g. keyword stuffed) → Google may show only a portion; include a keyword but don't repeat it
	- Google may still show title links different from the title element (different context or space limits)
- Meta description recommendations (Google **might** use it, or a relevant section of the page text, in the snippet):
	- A meta description for **every page** → may influence the snippet
	- **Accurately summarize** the page content; inform and interest searchers; **no minimum or maximum length**
	- **Unique descriptions** for each page; for large e-commerce sites with thousands of pages, descriptions can be **automatically generated** from page content (advanced)
- A great page with a mismatched title frustrates searchers (may show in analytics; Google may show different title links)
#### Control title links and snippets
- Search results: a bold **title link** (the title of a result linking to a webpage) above a **snippet** (the description or summary) → generated from the title element and the meta description or a relevant section of visible text
- **Influence your title links** (generated from page content and references to it on the web):
	- Do: accurately describe each page's content; descriptive but concise; read naturally; unique titles per page; give each page's main headline greater visual weight and prominence
	- Don't: unrelated text; default or vague text ("Home", "Untitled", "New Page 1"); a single title across all pages; lengthy or wordy titles; repeated or boilerplate text; excessive keywords
- **Control snippets with quality meta descriptions**:
	- Do: accurately summarize the content; include all information users need to judge usefulness and relevance; unique descriptions per page
	- Don't: unrelated descriptions; generic descriptions ("This is a web page", "Page about women's clothing"); too many keywords; copy-paste the entire page content; a single meta description across all pages
- Control title links and snippets: https://www.coursera.org/learn/attract-and-engage-customers/supplement/ndhBH
#### Create structured data markup
- **Rich results**: enhanced results in Google Search with extra visual or interactive features (star rating, price, recipe)
- **Structured data**: code used to better describe a webpage's content to search engines → **structured data markup** = additional machine-readable information within a webpage
	- E.g. a product page: price and customer reviews; an organization: location, opening hours, events, videos, recipes, logo
	- No guarantee: the Google algorithm decides whether and for which searches rich results display
- **Schema**: the type of code used for structured data markups; third-party software on some platforms adds markups without coding; larger companies may have engineers do it
- Check implementation with the **Google Rich Results Test** (enter the URL, results in a minute or two; invalid markup won't show); **Google Search Console** shows which pages have a specific markup type
- **Never add fake data** (fake reviews, irrelevant markups)
- Popular rich results:
	- **Product**: price, availability, review ratings
	- **Review snippet**: review rating (usually an average) and number of ratings (book, recipe, movie, product, software app, local business)
	- **Article**: websites publishing timely information (not only news sites)
	- **Video**: option to play, specific video segments, livestream
#### Establish your business details on Google
- **Google knowledge panel**: information boxes that appear when you search for entities (people, places, organizations, things), displayed separately from general results → like a personal ID card for a brand; automatically generated from sources across the web
	- Business details (e.g. Coursera's panel): official website link, site logo, brand industry, brand description, social profile links
- Steps to set up business details:
	1. **Create a Google Business Profile** (for local brick-and-mortar businesses): a free tool to tailor how business information appears on Google Search and Google Maps (hours, products and services, online orders, ...)
	2. **Verify website ownership** in **Google Search Console** → control over how the site is presented and access to sensitive Google Search data
	3. **Update the knowledge panel**: sign in with the Gmail account associated with the entity → overrides information found by Google algorithms
	4. **Add structured data for rich results** (carousels, images, non-textual elements beyond the standard blue link); not automatically guaranteed → read *Understand how structured data works* (Google Search Central), check with the **Rich Results Test**
	5. **Highlight customer support methods**: contact number, email address

### Analyze search performance and user behavior
#### Introduction to Google Search Console
- **Google Search Console**: a no-cost tool to understand how a website performs on Google Search (how many people visit via Google, whether Google crawls your pages, technical errors)
	- Examples use **Happy Clothes**, a fictional company for demonstrating SEO and SEM tools
- First steps for a beginner:
	1. **Add and verify website ownership** (prove you own the site) → Search Console starts collecting data, full access
	2. After a few days: ensure Google can find and read your pages → **index coverage report** (all pages Google indexed or tried to index) → fix errors and warnings
	3. **Mobile usability report**: issues affecting users on mobile devices (specific issues, sample affected pages, how to fix)
	4. Consider **submitting a sitemap**: not required (Google likely still crawls), but best practice for larger sites (over **500 pages**) or if Google has difficulty discovering important pages; platforms like **WordPress, Wix, Blogger** create sitemaps automatically
	5. **Monitor performance**: **search performance report** → traffic from Google Search by queries, pages, countries; trends for impressions, clicks, and other metrics
- General usage: minimal day-to-day (emails on unusual events, e.g. hacking or crawling issues)
	- Every month or so: review the dashboard for a quick health check (errors, unusual dips in clicks)
	- When adding new content: submit a new sitemap if needed, confirm indexed pages rise a few hours later
#### Google Search Console reports and metrics
- **Overview page** (after logging in): summary of site health (security issues, metric graphs, enhancements like structured data) → check periodically for security issues, enhancement errors, dips in clicks
- **Web Search Performance report**:
	- **Impressions**: how many times your site has been seen by someone in Google Search
	- **Clicks**: how many times a searcher clicked on your results in the SERPs
	- **Click-through rate (CTR)**: the percentage of people who saw your listing and then clicked on it
	- **Average position**: where your site ranks in the results (position one = top)
	- Metrics are connected: many impressions but a low average position → few clicks → low CTR
- **Links report**: which websites link to yours, the link text, your top-linked pages → why does a page get more links? Spammy linking sites can generally be ignored
- **Coverage report**: index status of all pages (grouped by whether they are or can be indexed, with reasons) → scan for spikes in errors or drops in index counts (crawling problems)
- **Manual Actions report**: a **manual action** is Google's way to demote or remove pages not compliant with its webmaster quality guidelines (e.g. attempts to manipulate the index, keyword stuffing)
- **Security Issues report**: signs the site was hacked or harmful behavior (e.g. installing unwanted software on a visitor's computer) + details and how to fix

#### Understand the benefits of SEM
- Two main ways to get found in SERPs: **search listings** and **paid ads**
- **Search engine marketing (SEM)**: increasing a product or service's visibility on a SERP through paid advertising
	- You're typically charged when the ad is **clicked**, not when it shows
	- Also possible on other search engines and **product-based search engines** (searches on e-commerce sites themselves)
- Pay models:
	- **Pay-per-click (PPC) advertising**: businesses only pay when someone clicks on their ad → aligns Google with advertisers (Google is motivated to show ads that get clicked)
	- Other models: pay per **impressions** (how many times someone sees an ad)
	- vs. a magazine ad: large upfront cost and no tracking of reader actions; digital ads can be optimized based on user actions
- Placement: typically at the **top and bottom** of Google Search; depends on ad type (e.g. **shopping ads** at the top for product searches or on the Shopping tab; **local search ads** for local searches); sometimes no ads appear
- Advantages of SEM:
	1. **Reach customers likely to buy**: e.g. a plumbing business in Phoenix, Arizona, shown for "plumber in Phoenix Arizona"
	2. **Quickly appear in SERPs**: useful when a (new) website hasn't built enough authority or reputation for competitive terms
	3. **Learn which ads drive sales**: drive traffic to specific pages to collect data on which content performs and converts
	4. **Control where you send searchers**: e.g. homepage ranks for "leaking water heater repair", but SEM can send them to a page with a lead form or phone number → tailored messaging
		- **Landing page**: any webpage where a visitor enters a website; should directly address the problem the customer is trying to solve

### Understand Google Ads
#### How Google Ads works
- Steps to create any Google ad (regardless of format):
	1. **Define your campaign goal** (also **objective**): more sales, leads, website traffic, store visits → informs other options (e.g. traffic goal → bidding like "maximize clicks", putting your **ad spend** towards clicks)
	2. **Choose your campaign type**: search, shopping, local, video → determines where ads show and how they look (search → SERPs; video → YouTube; local → Google Maps, websites, YouTube)
		- **Campaigns**: ads that share a budget, location targeting, and other settings; often organize categories of products and services; an account can run numerous campaigns
	3. **Set a budget**: maximum spend, changeable anytime → an **average daily budget** over one month; Google Ads optimizes spend for days with more likely clicks and conversions (some days under, some over), but never exceeds the **monthly spending limit**
	4. **Choose your bidding strategy**: recommended based on the goal (e.g. focus on conversions); **automated bidding strategies** use **machine learning** (software instructions that help the ad achieve its goal) → bids set by likelihood of conversion or sale, learning as ads are served
	5. **Select audience targeting**: no targeting = widest reach; narrower targeting reaches specific interested customers (→ customer personas); common forms: **keywords, audiences, locations, topics, devices** → plan before creating the ad
	6. **Create your ad**: primary text ad format = **responsive search ads** (several headlines, descriptions, and a landing page URL; Google tests combinations) → the landing page must deliver what the customer expects
	7. **Set up conversions**: **conversion tracking** shows whether ads, targeting, and campaigns work → e.g. connect **Google Analytics** to Google Ads
		- Provide clear information about data collected and get user **consent** (e.g. website pop-up / **cookie consent bar**)
#### Google Ads bid strategies
- **Bid strategy**: designed to help achieve campaign goals based on budget
	- **Manual Cost Per Click (CPC)**: you set your own maximum cost per click
	- **Automated bidding**: Google Ads sets bids based on the likelihood of a click or conversion
	- **Smart Bidding**: a subset of automated bidding using machine learning to optimize for conversions or conversion value in each auction, with **auction-time signals** (device, location, time of day, remarketing list, language, operating system) → decides whether and how much to bid
- Types of bid strategies:

| Bid strategy | Definition | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maximize conversion value | Advanced machine learning automatically optimizes and sets bids, with auction-time bidding tailored for each auction | Increase business value |
| Target ROAS | Optional target for maximizing conversion value; predicts the value of a potential conversion for each search and adjusts bids to maximize return (bids high on likely high-value conversions) | Increase business value |
| Maximize conversions | Sets bids to get the most conversions within your budget | Increase sales or leads |
| Maximize clicks | Sets bids to get as many clicks as possible within your budget | Increase website visitors |
| Target CPA | Optional target for maximizing conversions; you set an average cost per conversion (target cost per action) | Increase sales or leads |
| Target impression share | Shows ads on the absolute top of the page, the top of the page, or anywhere on the page, based on placement settings | Increase awareness and visibility |
| Cost per 1000 impressions (CPM) | Pay based on impressions on YouTube or the Google Display Network | Increase awareness and visibility |
| Cost per view (CPV) | Default for video ads; pay for video views or interactions | Increase awareness and visibility |

- Google Ads guides you to a bid strategy with questions about campaign goals; also shows recommended strategies for existing campaigns
- Identify the goal first, then match a strategy; often trial and error

#### Identify keywords and understand the ad auction
- Keywords are a foundational component of SEM → accurate keyword research
- **Keyword match types**: how closely the keyword must match the user's query
	- **Broad match**: ads show on searches related to the keyword, may not include it (e.g. "low-carb diet plan" → "carb-free foods", "low-carb diets", "low-calorie recipes"); may use recent search activity and **landing page** content → pair with an **automated bidding strategy**
	- **Phrase match**: searches that include the meaning of the keyword; more flexible than exact, more targeted than broad (e.g. "tennis shoes" → "shoes for tennis", "buy tennis shoes on sale", "red tennis shoes"; not "tennis rackets", "training shoes", "can you wear running shoes for tennis?")
	- **Exact match**: same meaning or intent; most control (e.g. "shoes for men" → "shoes men", "men shoes", "men shoe"; not "men's tennis shoes", "shoes for boys")
	- All match types cover **close variants** (plurals, misspellings, synonyms, reordered words) → no need for exhaustive keyword lists
- **Negative keywords**: exclude search terms from campaigns to avoid irrelevant searches; negative keywords **don't match close variants**
	- E.g. negative broad match "running shoes": ad may show for "blue tennis shoes" or "running shoe", not for "blue running shoes", "shoes running", "running shoes"
- **Ad auction**: how Google determines which ads to show and in what order for each search with text ads
	- **Ad rank**: a value Google uses to determine your ad's position; main factors:
		1. **Bid**: how much you're willing to pay per click
		2. **Quality of your ads and landing page**: relevance to the query and likelihood of a click → **quality score** (diagnostic tool, scale **1–10**, a guide only, not used in the auction)
		3. **Ad extensions**: phone number, additional links → use all relevant ones
		- Other factors: contents of the search, other results on the page
- Closely matching keywords + right bid + quality ad + helpful landing page → more likely at the top
#### Best practices when creating a Google Ad in Search
- Effective ads can appear higher, get more clicks and conversions, sometimes at a lower **cost per click** than competitors
- Best practices:
	1. **Focus messaging on user needs and benefits**: e.g. reliability → "official site"; wide selection → "view our inventory" (use personas' goals)
	2. **Include at least one keyword from your ad group in your headlines** (the **headline** is the clickable link; the **description** is below); Google tests combinations, so every combination must read well; advanced tools can insert keywords automatically
	3. **Avoid generic sales language**: "call us today" creates false urgency; generic CTAs ("sign up today", "book today") decrease engagement → specific, relatable CTAs (e.g. "book your dream vacation rental" instead of "book now")
	4. **Implement all ad extensions that make sense** (location, call, app install help mobile users) → enable at least **four**; Google picks the best combination per auction
	5. **Optimize your landing page**: relevance to keywords should be clear right away (useful, well organized, clear directions); **load quickly**
- Treat Google Ads as an experiment: create different ads, get results, tweak lower-performing copy

### Apply display advertising
#### Introduction to display advertising
- **Display advertising**: visual ad formats placed on websites or applications that are not search engines (e.g. children's furniture ads on a furniture trends website)
- Instead of contacting websites one by one → **display advertising networks**, e.g. **Google Display Network**: more than **two million** websites, videos, and apps; reaches over **90%** of Internet users worldwide
	- Targeting: contexts (e.g. "outdoor lifestyles", a specific website), audiences (young moms, people shopping for new sedans), searcher locations, remarketing to previous visitors
	- Unlike search ads, viewers aren't searching for your product → delivering to the likely customer is crucial
- Two types of Google Display Ads:
	- **Uploaded ads**: you create the ad graphic to required specifications (sizes like **banner, leaderboard, skyscraper**) → for a specific design vision; needs design resources and time
	- **Responsive display ads**: upload content (images, headlines, logos, videos, descriptions) → Google Ads automatically generates combinations for websites, apps, YouTube, Gmail; less control but easier
- Benefits of responsive display ads:
	1. **Optimization by Google Ads software**: best combinations of assets delivered automatically, no manual testing
	2. **Broader reach**: Google adjusts size, appearance, and format to fit almost any ad space (manually uploaded ads only show where that size is supported)
	3. **Use with videos**: Google tests videos against photos and shows the better performer
	4. **Save time**: less time managing a complicated ad portfolio, more on performance improvement
#### How to optimize a responsive display ad for your goals
- Responsive display ads are the most popular type of display ad
- Creative best practices:
	1. **Upload the recommended number of assets** (**assets** = ad content: headline, image, description, logo, business name) → Google recommends **five headlines, five images, five descriptions**; on average **10% more conversions** with multiple assets
	2. **Unique and effective copy**: the headline shows the brand or product's value and works without the description; the description complements it and explains the value
	3. **Cohesive landing page**: messaging matches the ad; make the next desired action clear
	4. **Rotate in new display ads every few weeks**: swap lower-performing assets → prevent **ad fatigue** (the audience sees your ads so often they become bored and stop paying attention)
- Image tips (images are the most crucial element):
	1. **Strong visual focus**: images that support the main point; Google's free **stock image library** if needed
	2. **Don't overlay a logo, text, or button** (repetitive logos, unreadable text in small formats; buttons like "Book Now" or "Download" may violate Google's ad policy)
	3. **Make the product the focus**: blank space no more than **80%** of the image
	4. **Background suits the product**: no fake backgrounds (e.g. product in outer space); physical setting with natural lighting and shadows
- Google released responsive display ad technology in **2016**
