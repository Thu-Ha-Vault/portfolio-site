### Customer loyalty and rewards
#### Welcome to module 1
- Refresher: **loyalty** is the last stage of the marketing funnel (after awareness, consideration, conversion) → customers become **repeat customers** and **brand advocates**, ideally voices of the brand
- This module: how loyalty is created in an e-commerce business, loyalty strategies and tactics (**rewards programs**, **dynamic remarketing**), techniques businesses use to develop relationships with customers
#### Introduction to customer loyalty
- We show loyalty when we form an **emotional attachment** to someone or something; in relationships, loyalty is built on **trust**, fostered by a sense of **belonging and community**, and motivates people to stay connected → in e-commerce it's built the same way
- Loyalty is based on the trust a customer places in a business; a sense of community and connection makes it even stronger (→ [Course 2: Loyalty strategies](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
- Three ways to build loyalty:
	- **Build trust**:
		- Offer **quality products** that meet customers' needs; a **quality guarantee**, a generous **return policy**, **free shipping and returns** → remove worries that the product might not meet expectations
		- Quality **customer service** throughout the customer journey, including after the purchase
		- Protect customers' **personal and financial information**: a **trust badge** or seal on the checkout page, assure customers their email address won't be shared with other companies
		- Loyal customers are the building block for a strong brand
	- **Create a sense of community and belonging**:
		- Engage with customers on social media
		- **User-generated content**: product reviews, photos or videos, the Q&A section on a product page, forums
		- Share the story behind your brand and show how your values align with customers' values → **77%** of consumers buy from brands who share their values (e.g. passion for the outdoors, caring for the environment, changing society's perception of beauty)
		- Donate a portion of sales to communities in need (e.g. a clothing company donates a pair of socks to a homeless shelter for every purchase) → values in action, customers take part in doing good
	- **Make it easy for customers to stay connected**:
		- **Subscriptions** for products bought regularly (razors, vitamins, printer ink) → let customers choose delivery frequency and make it easy to cancel so they don't feel locked in
		- **Dynamic remarketing**: show previous visitors ads containing the products and services they viewed on your site → personalized experience
		- **Email marketing**: personalized content based on interests, e.g. a reminder if someone abandons their cart, suggestions for related products, reminders to reorder contacts or flip a new mattress (→ [Course 4: Email marketing](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
		- **Rewards program** (loyalty program): incentives for customers to return
- Loyalty is the last funnel stage but one of the most valuable opportunities to grow: happy customers come back and tell others about your business
#### Rewards programs
- A good loyalty strategy creates **repeat customers** and increases **conversion rates**
- **Rewards program** (loyalty program): a marketing method designed to build customer loyalty by providing **incentives** to returning customers (free products, free shipping, discounts, coupons, cashback)
- Two common ways to prompt enrollment (make it as simple as possible):
	- **Pop-up messages** within the first couple of seconds on the website → usually an incentive just for signing up with an email address
	- At the **end of the purchasing journey** (more subtle) → customers can choose to enroll and receive future offers
	- **Automatic enrollment** in free programs (customer must opt out): pro → builds a larger customer base quickly; con → may weaken trust (some customers think it's deceptive)
- Common types:
	- **Points model**: a point equivalent for a set monetary value spent over several purchases → redeem for an incentive at a certain number of points (e.g. a kitchenware store: 1 point per $1, 100 points = a free bowl)
	- **Spend-based model**: incentives based on the amount spent in a **single purchase** → larger short-term value (e.g. free shipping on purchases over $100)
	- **Tier-based model**: customers graduate to new **status levels** based on the money spent over time → higher tier, better benefits (e.g. bronze below $50, silver $50–$100, gold over $100)
	- **Subscription model**: customers make a **recurring payment** to receive an exclusive incentive (e.g. next-day delivery for an annual fee of $20; others wait the standard week)
		- For paid programs, never automatically enroll customers in an unwanted service → separate messaging so customers can explore details on their own
- Short-term promotions alone don't always create true loyalty (competitors may have better prices on similar products) → questions to build a more effective program:
	- **How can we personalize the rewards program experience?** → use the customer's name in all communications (e.g. "Hi Mike, check out our new shoe launch" vs. a generic email); incentives for groups of customers based on shopping habits (e.g. a "just because" free shipping offer for customers who usually wait for free shipping)
	- **How can the program be both desirable and attainable?** → attractive offers that are also easily accessible (spending $100 for 25% off the next purchase is more desirable than $100 for 5% off)
	- **How can we create community within our customer base?** → e.g. a book company creates a **virtual book club** as part of its rewards program
#### Dynamic remarketing
- **Dynamic remarketing** with Google Ads: re-engage customers who previously viewed products on your site but haven't completed their purchase (need more time or a reminder) → ads with a personalized message tailored to your audience
- How it works: displays ads for products customers already viewed or **similar products**; the **Google Ads product recommendation engine** determines the best mix of products for each ad (based on popularity and what the visitor viewed) → helps them discover products they might not have found otherwise
- Example: an online **jewelry store** whose customers consider a big purchase, need extra time, and shop around → ads for viewed and similar products keep the brand in front of the customer during the purchasing decision
- Effective because it reaches customers who are already interested and motivated to buy (some have purchased before) → retain them and continue the relationship (→ [Course 6: Google Ads campaigns](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
#### Case study: How Wayfair uses email marketing for personalization
- **Personalization**: the practice of delivering a customized experience for each customer
- Company background: **Wayfair** (Boston, Massachusetts), online-only home retailer started in **2002** by Niraj Shah and Steve Conine to offer more choices than fit in a brick-and-mortar space → began as a collection of **200+** e-commerce stores by product category → combined into wayfair.com in **2011**; goal: help everyone, anywhere, create their feeling of home
- **Challenge**: email marketing is a core growth driver (the company's **second largest** marketing channel) → provide each customer the content they need at the right time; customers are in different funnel stages (hesitant new customers = awareness; researching = consideration; recent buyers needing an accessory = loyalty) → the same email for everyone isn't personalized
- **Approach**: emails based on where customers are in the shopping journey:
	- New customer → **welcome email** introducing the company and a variety of product categories (e.g. subject line "Welcome home!", body "Welcome to Everything Home" with a **Start Shopping** button)
	- Recent site visitor → email featuring products or categories they viewed
	- Recent purchase → related products or accessories (e.g. an area rug → a rug pad)
	- Hasn't visited in a while → inspirational content and ideas
- **Results**:
	- **Machine learning** to test and improve personalized product recommendations → test, evaluate, change
	- Recommendations worked when the customer recently purchased or viewed products; less effective the longer since the last visit → **content and timing** both critical
	- Best **subject lines** most closely matched the email content; for promotions, call out the discount (e.g. "Save $20 on your next Wayfair purchase of $100 or more") (→ [Course 4: Email marketing](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
	- A smooth checkout and delivery process matters more than anything in emails; after a bad purchase experience, an email with a discount on the next purchase → about **50%** of these customers returned to buy again
- Conclusion: personalization builds loyalty; testing ideas and applying results are keys to refining a marketing strategy

### Develop client relationship management skills
#### Post-purchase follow-up methods
- **Post-purchase communication**: any direct interaction customers have with a brand after they make a purchase → returning customers often make up a large portion of e-commerce sales, so it focuses on building relationships with existing customers
	- Boosts traffic to your store, keeps your business in customers' minds and ahead of competitors, makes customers feel they made the right decision → more brand loyalty and advocacy
	- **Buyer's remorse**: regret after making a purchase → over **50%** of online shopper survey respondents have experienced it; post-purchase communication eases it by reinforcing positive feelings
- Methods:
	- **Email** (the easiest way to reach existing customers):
		- **"Thank you" emails**: short follow-up showing appreciation for their business
		- **Review request emails**: two jobs → data on the customer's experience + (if positive) an endorsement of your product
		- **Customer satisfaction survey**: customers rate products on a scale → measure how products and services meet or surpass expectations
		- **Replenishment emails**: prompt a repeat purchase when previously bought items are about to run out (e.g. a reminder to reorder a favorite lavender shampoo bought two months ago) → highly effective
	- **Social media**: monitor hashtags and mentions to see the conversations your brand is part of (e.g. a baseball fan posts a photo in a new hat and tags the sportswear company → the company responds, and with permission reposts the photo → sparks more interest from both sets of followers)
	- **Direct mail**: letters or brand information through the mail → classic charm; shows thoughtfulness and care; more time-consuming but appreciated by some
- Tips:
	- **Personalize** the experience: greet customers by their actual names (not "customer" or "resident"); reach out on days they identified as important (birthday)
	- **Pace your communication** → don't overwhelm customers with messages
	- **Offer incentives** to shop (discounts, free money) → shows you want them to shop and appreciate their business
- Balance **promotional** and **engaging** communication → be genuine and sincere while growing the business (→ [Course 4: Retention emails](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
#### Survey customers and ask effective questions
- **Customer survey**: a tool to measure how products and services supplied by a company meet or surpass customer expectations → learn demographics (age, where customers live and work), interests and hobbies, issues with the shopping experience → better reach and communicate with groups of customers
- Two categories:
	- **Pre-purchase survey**: conducted before products are made
	- **Post-purchase survey**: conducted after the customer has checked out
- **Define your goal** first (what do I want to learn? e.g. how to improve the customer retention rate, which parts of the website need improvement) → avoids random data
- Tips for survey questions:
	- **Avoid leading questions** (e.g. "Do you like our website, right?" → biased data) → ask e.g. "On a scale of one to five, rate your shopping experience today" + an open forum to explain the answer
	- **Avoid overlapping answer options** (25–35 / 35–45 confuses a 35-year-old) → use 25–34 / 35–44
	- **Be respectful and inclusive** with personal questions
	- Use your **brand's tone of voice** (e.g. a playful kids' clothing store: "Hi there. Do you mind completing our customer survey?" instead of "Dear Sir or Madam...")
- Example questions:
	- Pre-purchase: who the customer is and what they're shopping for ("Who are you shopping for?", "Which of our products or categories are you most interested in?")
	- Post-purchase: the customer experience ("What is one thing we can improve about our website experience?", "Do you anticipate purchasing from us again?") + information for future targeting ("When is your birthday?", "On a scale of one to ten, how likely are you to recommend our company to a friend?")
- Checklist for each question:
	- Is the wording **simple** and easy to understand (no jargon)?
	- Does it ask for **too much personal information**? → make it more general
	- Is it **too complex** (multiple sentences or steps → users quit early)?
	- Does it make an **assumption** about the customer (e.g. a handbag store assuming customers identify as women)? → more neutral wording
- Publish the survey on your website or send by email → use the data to build your customer list and improve buying or marketing experiences
#### How to provide excellent customer service
- James Cash Penney: courteous treatment turns a customer into a walking advertisement → a great shopping experience makes shoppers return and recommend your business
- Globally **61%** of consumers view customer service as very important; customer service recently overtook price and product as the main factor that sets brands apart
- **Customer service**: the advice and support a company provides to its customers before, during, and after a purchase
- Customer service channels:
	- **Email** (most common): an email address or contact form on the website → Google requires businesses to list their email clearly to advertise on **Google Shopping**; state your expected reply time in the Contact us section
	- **Live chat**: customers communicate in real time with a business representative → often used by larger companies with resources to answer around the clock
	- **Chatbots**: programmed systems that respond to common customer questions → quick responses, but can't handle complex interactions (pre-programmed responses only)
	- **Social media** (e.g. Twitter, Facebook): respond publicly or privately → offer it on platforms where you already have a presence or that your customers use most
	- **Multi-channel customer service**: providing customer service across multiple channels (also phone support, forums) → interact with customers the way they choose
- Four key aspects: **resolve the issue quickly**, **resolve it in one interaction**, provide **friendly** service throughout, **follow up** afterwards to ensure satisfaction
	- Example: Menal orders a green winter coat from an outerwear store but receives a blue one → posts on social media and tags the company → the company responds within a few hours with kind language, moves to private direct messages, promises to correct the issue, offers a coupon for her next purchase → follows up after the new coat arrives
- **FAQ** (frequently asked questions) page: answers questions about the business, products or services, policies, processes
	- Saves time on general questions; builds trust (shows you've considered common scenarios); can bring new traffic via Google's **featured snippet** (→ [Course 2: Featured snippets](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
- Even outside customer service, your e-commerce job will likely impact the customer experience → know customers' issues and consider their feedback
#### Measure customer satisfaction
- **Measuring customer satisfaction**: research to determine the performance of products or services based on how customers perceive them → helps gain profit and **competitive advantage**
- Steps:
	1. **Define goals**: be specific about what to improve (selecting the item, checkout, delivery can each go wrong); goals should be **SMART** (→ [Course 1: SMART goals](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/))
		- Examples: reduce redundancies and customer friction, provide self-service options, replace the sales rep with e-commerce customer service agents, take customer reviews seriously, resolve issues quickly and in one interaction, friendly representatives, follow up with the same person if necessary, record/print/save a copy of the interaction, follow up afterwards
	2. **Choose a customer satisfaction survey type**:
		- **Customer Satisfaction (CSAT) survey**: measures a customer's satisfaction with products or services
		- **Net Promoter Score® (NPS) survey**: a single question → likelihood to recommend a company, product, or service to a friend or colleague from **0** (not at all likely) to **10** (extremely likely) → measures **loyalty** to a company (not satisfaction with a specific service)
			- **Promoters**: 9 or 10 (enthusiastic about the brand)
			- **Passives**: 7 or 8 (satisfied but not enthusiastic enough to promote)
			- **Detractors**: 6 or less (unsatisfied, unlikely to shop again)
			- **NPS = % promoters − % detractors** (e.g. 60% promoters − 15% detractors = **45%**)
		- **Usability survey**: the customer's satisfaction with the company's website + problems along the shopping journey
	3. **Select a survey medium**: in-app or on-site surveys, post-service or post-purchase surveys, long email surveys → implement feedback based on trends in the data
- Key takeaway: returning customers make up a large portion of future sales → collect positive and negative feedback and implement it

#### Welcome to module 2

#### Analyze data to measure e-commerce success
- Analyzing data shows what's working and what isn't (for major online retailers and small businesses) → which sales and marketing tactics are most effective, customer behavior → invest time and money where they deliver the most **return on investment** (→ [Course 5: Marketing analytics](/blog/assess-for-success-marketing-analytics-and-measurement/))
- Data sources: **Google Analytics**, built-in analytics of e-commerce platforms, email marketing and social media tools, customer surveys, **A/B testing**, **heat maps**
- Measuring success:
	- Set **SMART** goals for each area of the business (specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, time-bound)
	- Decide which metrics to track → **KPI** (Key Performance Indicator): a measurement used to gauge how successful a business is in its effort to reach a business or marketing goal
	- Newer stores: compare **quarter-over-quarter** (a **quarter** = a three-month period based on a company's financial calendar; four per year)
	- High-priced products (diamond jewelry, upscale furniture) → longer conversion process → track **micro conversions** (signs a potential customer is moving towards a completed purchase)
	- Stores around for at least a couple of years: quarter-over-quarter + **year-over-year** comparisons; more data and longer relationships → focus more on loyalty metrics
- Example: an online office supply store sets a goal to increase its conversion rate by **1%** in the next six months → track conversion rate, separate **mobile vs. desktop** visitors (lower mobile conversion → improve the mobile experience); a **heat map** shows customers getting stuck on the promotion code field at checkout → make it less prominent to reduce friction and improve conversion
- Analytics across the marketing funnel:
	- Discovery: where traffic comes from, which channels attract the most visitors and sales, geographic location, interests, behavior
	- Checkout: sales and shopping cart metrics → **conversion rate**, **average order value**, **cart abandonment rate**
	- Loyalty: **customer lifetime value** (total amount a customer is expected to spend with the business over their lifetime) → higher is better, since retaining customers is more cost-effective than acquiring new ones; can be based on a subscription (meal kits), repeat purchase (water filters), or one-time purchase (musical instrument)
	- **Brand advocacy** (the strongest form of loyalty): number of customers who promote a brand through **word-of-mouth marketing** → measured by the **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** (0–10 likelihood to recommend)

#### Analytics tools used in e-commerce
- **Google Analytics** (used by a majority of e-commerce sites): web analytics service that tracks and reports website traffic → visits, traffic sources, engagement with content, products generating the most revenue, total conversions and sales (→ [Course 5: Google Analytics](/blog/assess-for-success-marketing-analytics-and-measurement/))
- **Shopify analytics**: recent store activity, insights about visitors, website speed, analysis of transactions
- **Email marketing analytics**: Google Analytics tracks some details (e.g. visitors from email links); more detail in the email platform's tool (**Mailchimp**, **Constant Contact**); can integrate with a **CRM** tool (**HubSpot**, **Salesforce**) to track multiple sources in one place
	- **Open rate**: % of users that open your email
	- **Click-to-open rate**: % of email recipients who clicked one or more links in an email
	- **Unsubscribe rate**: % of recipients who unsubscribe after opening an email
	- **Conversion rate**: % of recipients who clicked a link and took a desired action (e.g. purchase)
	- **Email bounce rate**: % of emails that could not be delivered to the recipient's inbox
	- **Complaint rate**: % of complaints recipients send to mailbox providers about your email
- **Social media analytics** (built-in tools on platforms such as X, or cross-channel tools like **Sprout Social**, **Hootsuite**) → understand your brand, audience, competitors:
	- **Brand awareness**: attention your brand received across all social platforms during a reporting period
	- **Impressions**: number of times content is displayed to your target audience
	- **Potential reach**: how many people have potentially seen a post since publishing
	- **Applause rate**: approval actions (likes, mentions, retweets, favorites) relative to total followers
	- **Referrals**: number of times someone was guided to your website from another site
	- **Conversion**: completion of an activity that contributes to the success of a business
- **Paid advertising analytics** (Google Ads, X Ads; integrate with Google Analytics): ad views, clicks, conversions, **conversion value per cost** → improve ROI

### Monitor e-commerce stores for growth and revenue
#### Monitor growth and revenue for an e-commerce store

- Example: an e-commerce store selling **leather phone cases** wants better marketing and sales decisions based on its data:
	- **Traffic and revenue**, compared quarter-over-quarter or year-over-year → traffic matters especially for a new store (few visitors = even fewer sales)
		- **Qualified traffic**: visitors who are likely to become customers → focus most of the advertising and marketing budget on the sources that deliver it

	- **Cart abandonment rate**: % of customers who add a product to their cart and leave without completing the purchase → capture an email address and send **cart reminders** to recover lost sales

	- **Cost per acquisition**: the average cost of acquiring a **potential** customer → the customer lifetime value must be greater than it
	- **Customer acquisition cost**: the average cost of acquiring a **paying** customer → if higher than the average order value, the company spends more than it receives; can be an acceptable trade-off if the customer lifetime value is high enough

	- **Customer lifetime value**: the average revenue generated by customers over a certain period → measures loyalty; important for subscription-based products or services

	- **NPS** scores → go beyond loyalty to measure **brand advocacy**; passionate customers spread the word and are one of the company's greatest assets
#### Key metrics for monitoring an e-commerce store's performance
- Metrics may look different (or be unavailable) depending on the analytics tool; some need to be calculated with a formula
- **Comparing metrics over time**: stores less than two years old → **quarter-over-quarter**; stores at least two years old → both quarter-over-quarter and **year-over-year** (e.g. revenue per quarter vs. the same quarter the previous year)
- **Traffic and revenue**: most traffic should be **qualified traffic**; Google Analytics shows which traffic types bring the most visitors and how much revenue each generates (quarter-over-quarter comparisons of traffic, revenue by traffic type, total revenue)
- **Conversion rate (CVR or CR)**: % of users who complete a desired action (e.g. sign up for a software trial, buy from an apparel retailer)
- **Cart abandonment rate (CAR)**: % of customers who add a product to the cart and leave without purchasing → an increase may indicate issues in the checkout process; email reminders to recover sales (→ [Course 6: Cart abandonment](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
- **Average order value (AOV)**: average amount spent per completed order
- **Cost per acquisition (CPA)**: average cost of acquiring a potential customer
	- **CPA = Total cost of conversions / Number of conversions** (conversions can be email subscriptions, add-to-carts, or another micro-conversion)
	- E.g. $10,000 on campaigns over 90 days, 5,000 add-to-carts → 10,000 / 5,000 = **$2**
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)**: average cost of acquiring a paying customer
	- **CAC = Total cost of sales and marketing / Number of customers acquired**
	- E.g. $10,000 over 90 days, 2,000 buyers → 10,000 / 2,000 = **$5**
- **Customer lifetime value (CLV)** (lifetime value): average revenue generated per customer over a certain period → measures loyalty
- **Customer retention rate (CRR)**: % of customers retained over a certain period → key for recurring purchases or subscriptions
	- **CRR = [(E − N) / S] × 100** → E = total customers at the end of the period, N = new customers added during the period, S = existing customers at the start
	- E.g. subscription software store: S = 5,000, N = 500, 300 cancellations, E = 5,200 → [(5,200 − 500) / 5,000] × 100 = **94%** → most customers are satisfied

#### Conversion rate optimization
- **Conversion rate optimization**: the process of increasing the percentage of website visitors who complete a desired action
	- **Micro conversions**: actions indicating a potential customer is moving towards a purchase (sign up for email offers, watch a product video, add to cart) → lead to a **macro conversion** (a completed purchase transaction) → the most important conversion to track
- Data reveals: traffic sources with the highest conversion rate, page elements with the biggest impact, where in the buying process customers leave or abandon carts, where they get stuck in checkout
- Tools (besides Google Analytics):
	- **Heat maps**: data visualization of how visitors interact with a website using color variations (more clicks → darker, more intense color)
	- **Session recordings** (session replay, user recording): capture a visitor's actions (mouse movement, clicks, taps, scrolling)
	- **A/B testing** (split test, bucket test): online test of two variants, **50%** of testers to each → if the change has a higher conversion rate than the original, update the website
- Ways to increase conversion rates (depend on the business and its customers):
	- **General**: remove distractions; put the most-used elements in prominent locations; clear, easy-to-find **CTA** buttons and test CTA copy; remove unnecessary form fields; test sales promotions; add live chat; mobile-friendly website; improve speed (especially on mobile)
	- **Product pages**: product guarantee; **virtual try-on** experience; detailed descriptions, high-quality images from multiple angles, product videos; a **product recommendation engine**
	- **Emails**: ask customers to review products after purchase; send **abandoned cart emails**
	- **Checkout flow**: free shipping, guest checkout, flexible payment options, a clear and easy-to-find return policy
- CRO is a **continuous process**; even a small increase in conversion rates can make a noticeable difference in revenue

#### Understand visitor behavior with heat maps
- **Heat map**: a data visualization tool that uses a color scale to represent how visitors interact with an individual webpage → usually a rainbow (thermal) scale
	- "Hot" areas (red, orange) = more focus or engagement → elements with high click or tap rates are **hotspots**; "cold" areas (blue, cyan) = less engagement
	- Shows what visitors read, where they click, and what they miss → decide how to test or adjust each page
- Types (often used together):
	- **Scroll heat maps**: % of visitors who scroll down to specific points → what visitors are likely to see; hottest area usually **above the fold** (displayed before scrolling)
		- Put key content where it gets the most attention; identify **false bottoms** (visitors stop scrolling because they think the page ended); propose changes to encourage scrolling; optimize for web and mobile
	- **Click heat maps** (touch maps on mobile): where visitors click or tap → clusters appear as blobs of color
		- Are visitors clicking links or **CTAs** where you want? Clicks on unclickable elements or low link engagement → page may be overcrowded or confusing
		- Find which elements visitors respond to, gauge CTA effectiveness, decide on A/B tests, clarify content or structure
	- **Hover heat maps** (mouse maps, movement maps): track mouse movement → a sense of where visitors focus (not a perfect indicator of attention)
		- Focus on a paragraph may mean it's interesting or helpful, or confusing and hard to read → check whether visitors pay attention to the right elements, get distracted by unimportant details, or struggle with content
- Together, scroll, click, and hover maps provide actionable insights to improve clicks and conversions

### Identify and update listings based on data
#### Evaluate product performance
- Focus shifts from the whole store to **specific products**
- **Product analytics**: monitoring and evaluating data to gain insights into how users interact with a product or service → why it matters:
	- Evaluate whether a product is successful; find why it underperforms and opportunities for improvement

	- Evaluate **marketing campaigns**: do orders and revenue rise during a product-focused campaign? Do promotions raise sales enough to make up for the discounted price?
- Metrics and improvements:
	- **Number of product views** → can visitors find the product? Low vs. similar products → highlight it in campaigns, feature it on the homepage or category page, include it in product recommendations
	- **Product conversion rate**: % of customers who purchase a product after viewing it → small increases make a noticeable revenue difference
		- Update the product detail page: higher-quality photos with zoom, all available colors, multiple angles (e.g. a t-shirt front and back, a video of a model wearing it), product packaging
		- Description: benefits, technical specifications, size chart, packaging information → make up for not being able to try or inspect the product in a store
		- Enable **customer reviews**; few reviews → send email reminders asking for reviews
	- **Unique vs. recurring purchases** → important for short-lifespan or subscription-based products (more recurring purchases → stronger growth)
	- **Net profit margin** and **return on ad spend** → evaluate regularly (→ [Course 6: Product research](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
		- A negative profit margin may still be worth it (e.g. printers sold at a loss, made up through recurring sales of ink and toner)
	- Effect on **average order value**: a product priced far above the AOV may cost more than customers want to spend; products bought together (bicycle + helmet) raise the AOV → cross-selling, upselling, **bundle offers** (e.g. "Save 10% on a helmet when you buy a bicycle")
	- **Return rate**: % of products sold that are returned → higher than the category average → investigate (design flaw? inaccurate description?) and ask customers why they return it
- After updates, track performance over time → if no improvement, try something else or stop selling the product
- Entry-level roles: provide updates on product performance, suggest improvements
#### Key metrics for product performance
- Product monitoring → evaluate success, find improvements, plan inventory, avoid consistently underperforming products
	- Launched in the past six months → monitor over the product's entire lifespan; sold six months or longer → compare quarter-over-quarter or year-over-year
	- Compare to **similar products** (several backpack styles) and between **variations** (gray vs. blue backpack in the same style)
- **Number of product views** (item views in Google Analytics): how many visitors found the product + interest in this type of product
- **Number of add-to-carts**: a strong indicator of interest in buying
- **Number of units purchased**
- **Product revenue**: revenue generated by a product (consider with other metrics)
- **Product conversion rate** = (Product conversions / Unique visitors to the product page) × 100
	- E.g. a best-selling coat: 50 units sold, 2,000 page viewers in 90 days → (50 / 2,000) × 100 = **2.5%**
- **Unique vs. recurring purchases**: unique = bought once; recurring = bought twice or more → should be high for e.g. electric toothbrush heads, meal delivery kits
- **Net profit margin**: % of revenue left after expenses → compare profitability of products regardless of cost
	- = (Net profit / Total revenue) × 100 → e.g. (1,500 / 5,000) × 100 = **30%**
- **Return on ad spend (ROAS)**: success of advertising for a specific product
	- = (Number of units sold × Cost per unit) / Ad spend → e.g. (50 × 100) / 1,250 = **$4** (ratio 4:1 or 400%) (→ [Course 5: ROI and ROAS](/blog/assess-for-success-marketing-analytics-and-measurement/))
- **Average order value (AOV)**: an underperforming product priced above the AOV may be too expensive; accessories (hat, gloves bought with a coat) raise the AOV
- **Return rate** = (Number of units returned / Number of units sold) × 100 → e.g. (5 / 50) × 100 = **10%**
	- High vs. similar products → issues with quality or how the product is represented online; some categories (clothing, shoes) naturally have higher return rates
- **Quarter over quarter performance**: the percent change of quarterly results
	- Metric change = most recent quarter metric − prior quarter metric → QoQ % change = metric change / prior quarter metric
	- E.g. units purchased: 188 − 101 = 87 → 87 / 101 = **86.13%**

#### Optimize product offerings based on data
- Product analytics helps increase sales of a product, discover new product ideas, and decide which **SKUs to retire** (stop selling)
- **Increase product views** (low views → visitors can't find it or aren't interested; if findability changes don't work → retire the SKU):
	- Include it in the navigation menu where visitors expect it; make sure it shows in on-site search results
	- Feature it prominently (homepage, category page); add it to the product recommendations engine
	- Highlight it in email, social media, and advertising campaigns
	- **Bundle** it with a related strong seller (bicycle + helmet package)
- **Increase product conversion rate** (changes to the product page):
	- **Images**: high quality, zoom, multiple angles and all colors, packaging (if it has product information), product in use
	- **Videos**: product in use, a **360-degree view**, instructional videos
	- **Description**: benefits and features, technical specifications (size, weight), size chart, **comparison chart** for similar products
	- **Reviews**: enable reviews; email requests after the customer receives the product
	- **Pricing**: competitive vs. similar products → too low makes customers question quality; too high exceeds what customers will spend
- **Discover new product ideas**:
	- Analyze the **keywords customers search** on the website → many searches for a product you don't sell → consider adding it (after product research, e.g. the **best sellers report** in **Google Merchant Center**: most popular brands and products in Shopping ads and free listings)
	- Brainstorm products related to **best-sellers** (e.g. a store selling many mattresses → sheets, pillows, mattress pad, bed frame) → research demand and **viability** (sales potential); surveys or interviews with customers
	- Test with **dropshipping** before committing to inventory, or buy a limited supply to avoid **dead stock** (inventory unsold for a long time with little chance of selling) (→ [Course 6: Product sourcing](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
- **Decide which SKUs to retire**: performance doesn't improve enough after changes → retire the SKU
	- May mean retiring only a variation (e.g. a lunch box sells well in blue but rarely in green → retire the green SKU)
	- Frees time for products customers really want; no one can predict performance before launch

#### Boost your digital marketing skills with AI
- AI gets routine work done faster → more time where you have the most impact; Justine (marketing at Google; previously large-scale event series for small businesses and partnerships; now researches and designs digital skills training) uses AI daily for copy editing and finding themes in data
- **Prompt**: the input you provide to an AI model to elicit a specific response → framework **T-C-R-E-I**: Task, Context, References, Evaluate, Iterate (mnemonic: "Thoughtfully Create Really Excellent Inputs")
	- **Task**: what you want the model to do, clear and specific
		- **Persona**: the expertise the tool should draw from (e.g. a professional speech writer, a marketing executive with 15 years of experience) or the audience for the output (a customer, your manager)
		- **Format**: how the output should look (bulleted list, short sentences, table)
	- **Context**: necessary details (e.g. "five ideas for a birthday present, budget $30, for a 29-year-old who loves winter sports and recently switched from snowboarding to skiing" vs. a vague request)
	- **References**: examples for the tool to use (e.g. past presents you've given); not always available for abstract or inspiration tasks → use natural language and complete thoughts
	- **Evaluate**: did the input give you the output you needed?
	- **Iterate**: add information or edit the prompt and try again
	- The order matters less than the substance of the prompt
- **Human-in-the-loop** approach: combines machine and human intelligence to train, use, verify, and refine AI results → be mindful of inputs, always evaluate and verify outputs; check whether confidential or sensitive information is needed and follow your organization's policies; avoid personal or confidential data even outside work
- Demonstrated in Gemini, but techniques apply to other tools (ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude) → AI frees marketers from time-consuming tasks (manual data analysis, email processes) to focus on strategic, high-value work
#### Use AI to help you understand your audience
- Defining the **target audience** first → focused, relevant marketing that reaches likely buyers and maximizes ROI; traditionally via extensive market research, surveys, and focus groups (time-consuming, costly) (→ [Course 6: Target audience](/blog/make-the-sale-build-launch-and-manage-e-commerce-stores/))
- Gen AI tools (e.g. Gemini) rapidly process large amounts of data from diverse sources → key audience segments, preferences, unmet needs
	- Justine used gen AI to analyze thousands of **anonymized** customer comments → like a focus group on demand → pinpointed problem areas quickly
	- Can surface market gaps: unmet needs, emerging trends, underserved segments
	- AI **complements** (doesn't replace) traditional market research → a starting point, then decide what other research to do
- Example: a company considering a line of **affordable sneakers** → prompt Gemini as a marketer to analyze the target market for inexpensive everyday shoes (demographics, spending habits) → output: price-conscious people aged **18–45** with active lifestyles needing comfortable shoes for daily activities; strategies focused on **durability and comfort**
- **Tree of thought (ToT)** prompting: ask the tool to explore multiple reasoning paths at once (like branches on a tree, or trying different paths through a maze) and evaluate as it goes → more comprehensive exploration, best outcome
- Review the output for accuracy, usefulness, and relevance → keeps the human in the loop and aligns with your goals
#### Kickstart your marketing strategy ideas with AI
- A blank page can be intimidating even for experienced marketers → gen AI helps **kickstart the creative process**: generate a range of ideas, find angles, refine messaging
- Example: an **app that recommends books** to readers; market research already shows the audience is adults who love to read → prompt: you're a marketer launching the app, target audience adults **21–60** who are avid readers, ask for an initial marketing plan with messaging for channels **other than social media** (a separate team handles social)
	- Provide context about the product, the target audience, and what can be **excluded** → being specific gives useful suggestions
	- Output expanded on existing ideas with specifics like **partnerships** (e.g. with a library)
- Use the output as a **thought starter** and refine it to fit your brand and budget
- Tip: explore different prompts, tweak them, or ask the tool to **regenerate** its response until it fits your goals → embrace experimentation
#### Use AI to compare two campaign proposals
- AI as a **neutral confidant** offering a fresh perspective (not a replacement for your gut instinct)
- Example: a marketer at a **sustainable clothing brand** choosing between two social media campaigns → prompt: describe the company (sustainability, ethical fashion, reducing environmental impact), ask for advantages and disadvantages of each in a clear, structured format + additional considerations; paste details and brand background (**context**, the C in TCREI)
	- **Campaign A – Earth First**: sustainability and ethical practices via influencer collaborations and user-generated content
	- **Campaign B – Style with a Conscience**: fashionable design and versatility with subtle eco-friendly nods, visually appealing imagery, fashion bloggers
	- Output: a tailored breakdown + a recommended **hybrid approach** blending both, with a suggested campaign name
- Tip: Gemini's **microphone** feature → add information, start or iterate on prompts by voice; handy for sharing lots of context or brainstorming out loud
#### Improve your email marketing with AI
- Email marketing is cost-effective and measurable (stays top of mind, drives engagement and sales), but writing compelling copy repeatedly is draining (→ [Course 4: Newsletters](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
- Example: a **monthly newsletter**
	- Simple prompt first: ask for a reusable template and **four templates** to choose from → generic placeholders, a different brand personality each
	- **Evaluate** (the E in TCREI): review and pick one (e.g. "The Classic"), then customize sections for your company's needs
- **Iteration**: refining and improving AI prompts and outputs through repeated cycles of testing and adjustment → tips:
	- Where didn't the output match expectations? → give more specific guidance (the tool only knows what you tell it)
	- Add **references** (an example of the expected format or analysis)
	- Check phrasing → break instructions into shorter sentences
	- Build on what you liked (e.g. liked two of five ideas → explain why and ask for a new list; explain why you disliked the others)
- Iterated prompt: a marketer at a **nonprofit**; newsletter for donors, volunteers, supporters; sections: impact reports, volunteer opportunities, fundraising campaigns, event invitations, success stories, financial transparency reports; about two pages / **1,000 words**; placeholders for images or video; four templates → output adds image/video placement, body structure ideas, unique benefits of each
- Templates are a starting point → tailor to your organization; also use AI to brainstorm **subject lines** for open rates, A/B test options, edit and proofread drafts
#### Brainstorm website copy ideas with AI
- Marketers write a lot (emails, strategy documents, ad scripts, campaign copy) → Justine uses gen AI most for refining and developing copy
- **A/B testing**: two versions of content with a single differing variable compared to see which yields better results → data-driven decisions from real user behavior, better conversion rates and ROI (→ [Course 5: A/B tests](/blog/assess-for-success-marketing-analytics-and-measurement/))
	- AI helps brainstorm **variations** to test (e.g. a website headline)
- Example: a digital marketing specialist at a footwear e-commerce company with draft landing page copy for a campaign about shoes that fit all types of feet → prompt for **two additional versions** (three drafts to A/B test) plus a paragraph on how each differs; paste the original copy, headline, and sub-headline
	- Output: one version focuses on **foot pain** with the product as the solution; another on how **personal** choosing shoes is
- Tip: in product descriptions, focus on **benefits, not just features** → how the product solves problems or makes life easier
- Challenge: find something in your work that takes a lot of time or needs ideas, and commit to experimenting with AI on it
