### Understanding e-commerce and why it matters
#### Welcome to module 1
- Module overview: how e-commerce works and what types of products or services you can sell online → benefits of selling online → basic e-commerce terms and entry-level roles → market research (identifying your target audience, product research) → product sourcing and the importance of branding
#### What is e-commerce?
- **E-commerce** (also **e-comm**): the buying and selling of goods or services using the internet → list products or services on your website and receive payments online
- **Brick-and-mortar store** (traditional retail store): visit in person, shop the shelves, observe or try on the product (dressing room), ask an employee for help, pay at the register, leave with your purchase
- **E-commerce store**: visit online (e.g. after searching on Google or clicking a social media ad) → navigate the web pages → add a product to your **virtual shopping cart** → pay through a **digital checkout system** → the product is shipped to your address or sent to a specific location for pickup
- Four categories of products sold online:
	- **Physical goods**: something you can touch and hold → shoes, books, appliances, phone cases
	- **Digital goods**: e-books, digital photos, digital music files, online courses
	- **Services**: online tutoring, health coaching, business consulting, professional services (graphic design)
	- **Software**: e.g. Adobe Photoshop bought online and downloaded to your computer
- Use your own experience as a customer to create a better experience for the customers who visit your store
#### The benefits of selling online
- How customers shop has changed: the majority research products online before buying (often from a smartphone) → discover what's available, choose the right product, make the final purchase
	- They interact with a brand in multiple channels (social media, mobile app, website, online chat, brick-and-mortar store) → the **customer journey** is more like a winding road than a straight path from A to B (→ [Course 1: Customer journey](/blog/foundations-of-digital-marketing-and-e-commerce/))
- Brick-and-mortar: a specific location, certain opening hours, limited by how many customers can get there ↔ e-commerce: shop online **from anywhere at any time** → reach more customers (only a device connected to the internet needed) → one of the biggest benefits
- Two types of e-commerce businesses:
	- **Online only**: saves on startup and overhead costs (no retail space or in-store staff); start selling new products more quickly (no shelves to stock); adapt to customers' needs more easily by improving the customer experience from **data insights**
	- **Click-and-mortar**: sells online as well as in a brick-and-mortar store
- Reach customers looking for specific products not available locally (a specific textbook for a class, a swimsuit in the middle of winter) → grow even when it's hard to attract enough customers locally
- Challenges:
	- Customers can't observe or try products in store → possibly a **higher rate of returns**
	- **Shipping costs** → free shipping (a perk many customers expect) shrinks profits
	- No in-person customer service
- Why a brick-and-mortar store (e.g. an office supplies shop) might become click-and-mortar:
	- Most customers shop online first, even if they buy in store → according to Google, shoppers go online first in **over 60%** of shopping occasions (check stock before the trip)
	- Some browse in store, then buy online (more time to decide, better price, more options such as sizes or colors)
	- Sell a much wider variety of products than fits on store shelves
	- **Buy online, pick up in-store** → drives in-store traffic (additional purchases), same-day orders, saves on shipping costs
- Reasons to buy online or in-store: need it the same day? available nearby? convenience of shopping from home? want to interact with the product or get help in person?
- Both are valuable, but customers increasingly expect to browse inventory and buy online → an e-commerce presence is important for almost any business

### Terms, tools, and strategies in e-commerce
#### Get to know the basics of e-commerce
- Terms for how customers navigate an e-commerce website:
	- **Traffic**: the number of visits that a website receives
	- **Landing page**: the first page a visitor encounters when they go to a website (homepage, product page, or any other page)
	- **Call to action**: the instruction provided to the customer that tells them what to do next → e.g. the **Add to Cart** button on a product page, a "Sign up for email" link on the homepage
	- **Digital shopping cart**: the virtual equivalent of a physical shopping cart → keeps track of all items the customer plans to purchase
	- Online **checkout** → the customer enters payment information through the **payment service provider**: a secure way to process transactions online (credit cards, debit cards, third-party payment services)
	- **Conversion**: the completion of an activity that contributes to the success of a business (sign up for an email list, fill out a contact form, spend a certain amount of time on the site) → in e-commerce, most often turning a visitor into a paying customer by completing checkout
- Tools and platforms:
	- **E-commerce platform**: a software application that allows you to sell products or services online → e.g. **Shopify**: manage your website, marketing campaigns, shipping and fulfillment, payment services, analytics, all in one place
	- Business tools such as **Salesforce** → manage and monitor sales, marketing, analytics, and customer service

#### Essential e-commerce strategies
- A Morning Brew-Harris Poll: **50%** of people aged 26–41 (millennials) do most of their shopping online
- **E-commerce strategy**: a working plan to promote an online store and increase its sales → every online business should build and execute one; methods of discovery that help customers find a business online:
	- **Business profile**: a **Google Business Profile** customizes how business information appears on Google Search and Google Maps (Maps for online businesses that also have local stores) → broadcasts "I exist and am open for your business!" (→ [Course 2: Establish your business details on Google](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
	- **Organic social media**: according to Sprout Social, **40%** of online customers find new brands from their personal network, **32%** from word of mouth; with YouTube and TikTok, much organic social media will be video
	- **Paid search, display, social media, and shopping ads**: a combination of organic and paid content yields the best results; direct control over branding, ad copy, landing pages, bidding strategies, performance → drive a significant percentage of e-commerce revenue
		- Some users block branded content, but paid social ads are still useful; **shoppable ads** on Facebook and Instagram can be quite effective (some users engage without recognizing they've clicked paid ads)
	- **Influencer marketing**: around **35%** of online customers find new brands from influencers they follow → reach the right customers with a tailored message (→ [Course 3: Influencer marketing](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))
		- Practices that limit impact: **stealth marketing** (content that appears to be word of mouth without disclosing sponsorship → users wary); some customers buy only when the influencer shows evidence of personal use (photos of empty packaging); **fake followers** powered by **bots** (a high follower count doesn't always mean engagement)
	- **In-store ads** (hybrid businesses): inform in-store customers about the benefits, convenience, and cost-savings of online ordering → incentives such as special inventory (hard-to-find sizes or colors) and deeper-discounted clearance inventory online only
- **E-commerce innovation** to improve business value: improved communication and customer tracking of shipments; more local fulfillment (**micro-fulfillment**); fulfillment networks and services; **dropshipping** by wholesalers to reduce retailers' inventory; **curbside returns** (the reverse of curbside pickup); diversification of payment methods
- Key takeaway: quality of goods, price, speed of delivery, and return policies impact buying decisions → incorporate as many discovery methods and innovations as possible

### Getting started in e-commerce
#### Market research
- **Market research**: the process of gathering information about consumers' needs and preferences → helps determine the e-commerce store's potential for growth and success within a specific business and industry
	- Understand your target audience (who they are, where they shop, what else they're considering) → reach them with the right message at the right time
	- Smaller company: you may do the research directly; larger company: a specific research team → either way, the results help you understand and engage customers
	- Cindy started her career in market research
- Two types:
	- **Primary research**: research done by you → surveys, interviews, direct observation, focus groups → very specific to a business, but a larger budget and more time
	- **Secondary research**: research done by others → information from published sources (consumer insights, surveys, interviews) → more budget-friendly and less time, but may not be specific enough or the market may have changed since the study
	- Analogy: sending a survey asking people which fruit they love most = primary; finding a published pie chart of the most popular fruits online = secondary
- Three main areas to focus on:
	- **Market size**: the total number of potential customers within a specific industry → Is there a large enough demand? How much competition exists already? Is there room for growth?
	- **Target audience**: the group of people most likely to purchase a company's products → Do they want to buy this type of product? Is it in their price range or budget?
	- **Competition**: the other sellers that exist already in the market → Who are your most important competitors? How can you differentiate your business and provide a better product or experience?
- Findings help you make better decisions in all areas: products you sell, prices you set, how you market to customers
#### Get started with market research
- Market research also helps tailor marketing, advertising, and business policies to the needs and desires of your **target audience**
- **Primary research**: done by you (or someone you hire) → surveys, interviews, directly observing someone interacting with a product, focus groups
- **Secondary research**: uses information someone else has put together → consumer insight reports, published surveys or interviews
	- Requires less time and money (no participants to find or reimburse; paid access to published sources is typically much cheaper) → often the **best place to start**: general overview of the industry → then know where to focus primary research for information that wasn't available
	- Primary research: more time, money, effort, but very specific to a business and the most up-to-date
- Figure out what information you need first → depends on the company's goals (launching a new brand, entering a new market, rebranding an old product, ...): market size, target audience, competition, or all three
	- If the company has done research before, you may only need to find out if the market has changed (or start from the beginning)
- Guiding questions: Is there a large enough demand? Room for growth? Does your target audience want to buy this? Within their price range or budget? How much competition exists? Who are your most important competitors? How can you differentiate your business?
- Sources for secondary research: government data, statistics, and resources; small business association resources; research agencies; industry publications; trade journals; research published by competitors; internet research for competitor information; transcripts of competitors' earnings calls; **Google Trends**
	- Pro tip: check the **publication date** first to make sure the data is recent enough
- Sources for primary research: interviews, surveys, analytics data, focus groups, direct observation of consumers interacting with a product, a visit to a competitor's brick-and-mortar or online store
- How often: not a one-time thing → the market changes (social or cultural behaviors, consumers' needs, new competitors, a major disruptive event) → annually or every few years, or when there's a specific need (start a new business, launch a new product, expand a product category, identify reasons for a long-term slowdown)
- Benefits: optimize marketing and advertising campaigns, boost **ROI**, focus time and budget on efforts likely to produce meaningful results, reach more customers, increase sales, improve customer relationships

#### Identify your target audience
- **Target audience**: the group of people most likely to buy your company's products → branding, marketing campaigns, and pricing strategy all benefit; spend the marketing budget effectively, build a brand customers identify with → the more specific, the better (→ [Course 2: Customer personas](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
- Example: a retailer that sells affordable **custom suits**
	- Men aged 30–45 → still broad
	- + incomes of **$100,000** or more, with an advanced degree → clearer
	- + a large majority travel frequently for their job
	- Further questions: Where do they shop? Do they make their own clothing decisions or does someone influence them? Do they need to pack multiple suits when they travel?
	- Be aware of your **biases**: other groups of customers will shop with you too (many people wear suits) → use **data** to understand who your customers are
- Study **demographics** (age, location, income level, education level, occupation) and other factors (personality, interests, hobbies, values, lifestyle, behavior) (→ [Course 7: Understand your audience with AI](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
#### Learn more about identifying your target audience
- **Use insights from market research**: information about customers' demographics and personal characteristics (gathered with surveys, interviews, analytics, ...) → choose questions most relevant to the company's goals and products
	- **Demographics**: age, location, gender identity, family, education level, income level, occupation
	- **Behavior**: What stores do they like to shop at? What motivates them to purchase? Who influences their buying decisions? Where do they go for advice or information? Which social media sites do they use? What media do they consume regularly?
	- **Interests, hobbies, lifestyle, values**: How do they spend their free time? Hobbies? Causes they support? What is important to them?
	- **Goals and barriers**: What goals do they want to achieve? What are the barriers? How would your product make it easier to reach their goals?
- **Analyze existing customers**:
	- Google Analytics: demographic information for site visitors (location, gender, interests, age, language)
	- **Customer relationship management (CRM)** system: software that helps a business manage and monitor its interactions with existing and potential customers (e.g. Salesforce)
	- Customer reviews and comments: social media comments, online chat transcripts from customer service, product reviews
	- A survey on your website, social media, or to email subscribers → an incentive may increase the response rate
- **Identify patterns**: organize, sort, and filter the data in a spreadsheet to find the main shared characteristics (example spreadsheet with name, age, location, household, education, main concern → most customers in their 20s and 30s: ...)
	- Companies commonly have **more than one target audience** (especially with a variety of products)
	- Don't exclude a group just because it doesn't match the majority → a smaller group can be an important target audience
- **Describe your target audience** – the custom suits retailer:
	- Audience 1: men aged 30–45; income $100,000+; professionals with an advanced degree; very little free time (frequent work travel); use LinkedIn and Twitter; wardrobe decisions influenced by their partner and the company's dress policy; need multiple suits; partner does most of the shopping; main motivation: impressing clients; buying online is easier than making time to visit a tailor
	- Audience 2 (smaller but significant): women and non-binary people aged 40–60; income $100,000+; executive-level professionals with an advanced degree; very little free time (long work hours); use LinkedIn and Facebook; influenced by fashion media and colleagues; need multiple suits; make their own shopping decisions; main motivation: expressing power in the workplace; buying online feels more comfortable than a tailor who traditionally measures for men's suits
- **Reaching your target audience**: product images featuring women and non-binary people in custom suits, a measuring guide for all genders, shift the ad budget to the social media sites and channels customers use frequently

#### How to do product research
- Launching a new product takes time, effort, and money → find out if there's enough **demand** first
	- **Demand**: how much consumers are willing and able to buy a certain product over a given period of time
	- Questions: Is there enough demand to make it worth selling? Will this level of demand continue?
	- Low or nonexistent demand → build up demand, or decide it's not worth selling
	- Extremely high demand → intense competition and higher advertising costs, or competitors low on inventory, or a product new to the market
- Example: the custom suits retailer wants to increase **revenue per order** by encouraging customers to add more products to their cart → research demand for belts, dress shirts, neckties
	- **Google Trends**: compare multiple products and their interest level over time; narrow to a region or state where your target audience lives, or search nationwide/worldwide
	- Set the time range to the **past five years** → is demand stable, growing, or shrinking? spikes show **seasonality**
	- Also ask: Is this the type of product your target audience would buy? Does it meet a need or solve a problem for them?
- Profitability soon after launch (a new line of custom suits sold for the past six months):
	- **Net profit**: the amount of money left over after expenses are paid → can you sell at a competitive price and still make enough money?
		1. **Total revenue** = number of products sold × average selling price
		2. **Total expenses** = cost of goods sold + packaging + shipping + fulfillment + advertising + return costs + any additional expenses
		3. **Net profit** = total revenue − total expenses
	- Example: 500 suits × $800 = **$400,000** revenue; expenses **$280,000** → net profit = **$120,000**
	- **Net profit margin** = net profit ÷ total revenue × 100 → a percentage to compare products regardless of price → example: **30%**
		- Generally higher is better, but a lower margin can still be profitable with high sales volume and relatively low advertising costs
- **Return on ad spend (ROAS)** = number of products sold × cost per unit ÷ ad spend → track over time to see if advertising dollars are well spent (→ [Course 5: ROI and ROAS calculations](/blog/assess-for-success-marketing-analytics-and-measurement/))
	- Example: 450 suits × $800 sold to customers who clicked the ads = **$360,000** ÷ ad cost **$45,000** (six months) → ROAS = **8**
- Recap: **net profit** → is the product as a whole profitable; **ROAS** → is the advertising for the product profitable (→ [Course 7: Evaluate product performance](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
#### Product sourcing
- **Product sourcing**: how a business acquires the products they sell to customers → affects packaging, shipping, and fulfillment costs (and the profit margin)
- Example: a business that wants to sell **mattresses** online → three main choices:
	1. **Design and create its own custom products** (in-house or with a manufacturer) → large time, expense, and commitment upfront, but more control over design and quality, a unique product that fills a specific need, higher profit margins (the company owns the product)
	2. **Partner with a vendor or wholesale supplier** to sell products made by other companies → brands customers already know and trust; less time-consuming and expensive than custom products
	3. **Dropshipping**: a fulfillment method in which products are shipped from the supplier directly to the consumer → the fastest way to bring a product to market (the drop shipper handles inventory, shipping, fulfillment; no buying in advance, warehousing, or shipping) (→ [Course 7: Optimize product offerings](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
		- Downsides: usually **slim profit margins**; the company's reputation depends on the drop shipper's work (a product that arrives damaged or in the wrong color)
- The sourcing model affects product availability and inventory, profit margins, shipping options, warehousing needs, and control over the brand's reputation
#### Researching and sourcing products
- **Product viability**: the sales potential for a specific product → research it before adding a product to your store; consider:
	- **Demand**: is there a high enough demand for this specific product to make it worth selling?
	- **Profit margin**: can you sell at a competitive price while also making a profit?
- Ways of **product sourcing**:
	- **Create products in house**: a lot of time, money, and other resources, but more control over quality
	- **Source from a manufacturer**: less control over quality and possibly lower profit, but products may be **dropshipped** (shipped from the supplier directly to the customer) → saves time and resources
	- **Source from a wholesaler**: buy directly at a lower price and sell at whatever price you're comfortable with → you ship the items yourself, but possibly a higher profit margin; dropshipping may also be possible
	- Weigh: **quality control** (importance for store and brand reputation), **profit margins**, **shipping and fulfillment** (fill orders yourself, or a warehouse and employees?), **time spent**
- **Forecasting**: the process of predicting the future demand for products (hypothesizing how popular an item might be) → analyze whether products are worth the financial risk, understand **seasonal trends** → how much of each product to keep in stock
	- Use analytics dashboards and sales data: top-selling products, negative reviews, quantities sold over a period, seasonal sales spikes or dips, product reviews
- **Add accessories to increase revenue**: complementary accessories increase revenue per order (e.g. an electronics store selling the newest laptop → charging cables, cases, screen protectors, external hard drives)

#### Overview of e-commerce stores
- **E-commerce store**: a store that sells its products online → search or browse, add to a virtual shopping cart, pay online
- Basic elements (best practice, recognizable on almost any e-commerce site):
	- **Homepage**: the main page of a website → shows what types of products the company sells, helps customers find information, main hub connecting to other pages
	- **Navigation bar**: a collection of links to other pages within the website → typically stays at the top on every page
		- A **menu** with product links organized by department or category (e.g. appliances → refrigerators, washing machines); other important pages (featured deals, weekly ad, gift registries, customer support)
		- **Search functionality** (search bar)
		- Link to create or sign in to an **account** (edit account and payment information, access previous orders)
		- Link to the **shopping cart** (view the current order at any time)
	- **Product detail pages (PDPs)**: information about a specific product → description, images, specifications, reviews
	- **Digital checkout system**: enter shipping and payment information and complete the purchase → a key part of the customer journey (remove **friction**, more later)
	- Other pages: customer support, contact information, return and exchange policies, shipping information
- **Responsive website**: designed to work on all types of devices (computers, mobile phones, tablets) → easier, more efficient browsing → typically better conversion rates (→ [Course 2: Make your website mobile-friendly](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
- **Web accessibility**: a website or mobile app designed and developed so that people with disabilities can use it → **71%** of people with disabilities leave a website immediately if it's not accessible; many accessibility enhancements improve everyone's experience (e.g. **video captions**)
- **Store locator** (if the business also has brick-and-mortar stores) → research products and find nearby stores; **BOPIS**: "buy online, pick up in store"
- Many elements (shopping cart, digital checkout) are part of the **e-commerce platform** → add/update products, manage orders, receive payments, handle shipping and fulfillment, all in one place; built-in tools and templates
#### How an e-commerce store works
- How customers navigate (example: a customer searching for a **kids' desk**):
	- Find products via the **search bar** (usually top of the page), the **navigation menu** (top or left-hand side), or links to specific categories or products
	- Research products in more detail (types of desks, features such as storage compartments or a keyboard tray) → on the store's website (buying guide, blog) or other sources; on the product detail page (description, images or videos, customer reviews, customer-uploaded images or videos)
	- Research can happen at multiple times: before visiting the store, after adding to the cart, or after purchase (return or exchange)
	- Add to the shopping cart (maybe multiple products while deciding, or complementary products such as a desk chair) → checkout (payment, shipping, billing information) → complete the purchase
- Basic elements of an e-commerce store:
	- **Home page**: the main page of a website; helps customers understand what the company sells, find what they need, main hub
	- **Navigation bar**: top or left-hand side; a **fixed element** (stays in place on every page); search function, navigation menu with department or category pages (e.g. furniture → kids' furniture, office furniture → desks), cart and account links, store locator
	- **Product detail pages (PDPs)**: product description, specifications or technical details, images or videos, customer reviews, other relevant information
	- **Shopping cart**: holds all products a customer plans to buy; add or remove at any time → begin checkout
	- **Checkout**: billing and shipping information via a **payment service provider**; signing in to an account gives a faster checkout (information auto-populates)
	- **Help and support**: chat function, multiple ways to contact customer service, self-help resources; links to FAQs, return and exchange policies, shipping policies, contact information, company details and values
		- Often in the **footer**: a navigation section at the bottom of the website with links to supporting pages (customer support and services, policies, company information)

#### E-commerce platforms
- Common platforms: Wix, Shopify, BigCommerce, Squarespace, WooCommerce, Magento → a basic difference: **open-source** or **SaaS**
- **Open-source**: allows the user to access and edit the original source code → customize the website in any way (WooCommerce, Magento)
	- Analogy: a brick-and-mortar store in a building fully **owned** by the retailer → can completely remodel it
	- Need to know how to make changes in the code or hire someone with web development expertise
- **SaaS (Software-as-a-Service)**: web-based software available on a **subscription** basis → some customization but no access to the source code; different plans by size, budget, level of support (Shopify, BigCommerce)
	- Analogy: a store that **leases** its retail space → the landlord maintains the building; the tenant can make changes only within the landlord's limits
- Open-source:
	- Advantage: complete control over design and **functionality** (the technical features and capabilities available on the website) → endless customization to improve the customer experience
	- Disadvantages: need coding and technical skills to create and maintain the store; pay separately for a **web hosting service** (a service that enables a company to publish its website on the internet); the platform may be free or low cost, but additional costs (web hosting, web design, web development, security, IT maintenance)
- SaaS:
	- Advantage: an **all-in-one solution** (hosting, security, customer service); built-in templates and user-friendly software → launch quickly without coding skills
	- Disadvantages: limits on customization (advanced customization may require coding); may only integrate with certain third-party apps or shopping channels; only certain payment methods; additional fees for some features
- Considerations for all platforms:
	- **Page load speed**: up to **90%** of customers abandon a site that's too slow; a **one-second delay** in mobile load times can impact conversion rates by up to **20%**
	- **SEO capabilities** of the platform (+ SEO keywords and phrases on the site) → higher in search results → more visitors (→ [Course 2: What is SEO and why is it important?](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
	- **Room for growth**: handle increased traffic, a large number of products, more flexibility for marketing needs
- In an entry-level role you probably won't choose the platform (management usually has); coding is typically not required, but basic coding knowledge may help
#### E-commerce platforms and domains
- Knowing the difference between open-source and SaaS → identify the internal and external resources needed to manage the website (at a smaller company you may need to decide)
- **Open-source** platforms:
	- Fully customizable
	- Require basic knowledge of coding, development, and design (or hire someone)
	- May take a lot of time to build
	- May be less expensive than monthly subscriptions
	- Typically no customer support
- **SaaS** platforms:
	- Typically do most of the website building, development, and design for you
	- No access to the original code → not fully customizable
	- Less time to build
	- Can be more costly than open-source
	- Typically provide customer support
	- Built-in analytics (traffic, clicks, ...)
- Examples of open-source platforms: **WooCommerce** (popular for ease of use), **Adobe Commerce** (vast capabilities but more expensive), **CubeCart** (more robust technical support)
- Examples of SaaS platforms: **Shopify** (one of the most trusted; the focus of this course), **Squarespace** (one of the most user-friendly), **Wix** (emphasizes design and customization)
- **Domain**: the URL (web address) of your website, in the browser's address bar → e.g. the example store in this course: www.bathecoshop.com; must be **unique**
	- An upfront cost + an **annual fee** to keep it active
	- Some SaaS platforms (Shopify) sell domains directly; for open-source solutions, register through a site such as **GoDaddy** (list of accredited solutions on **Google Registry**)

### Ads in e-commerce
#### Welcome to module 3

#### Advertise online
- In the US, digital marketing experts estimate a person can be exposed to **4,000–10,000 ads each day** (figures differ globally)
- **Online advertising**: a form of marketing which uses the internet to deliver promotional marketing messages to consumers → now the go-to method for most businesses (brick-and-mortar or online)
	- Before the mid-1990s, online advertising was largely prohibited → companies relied on radio, television, billboards, magazines → once the ban was lifted, online ads soared
- Four key benefits:
	1. **Cost effective**: costs calculated by **CPM** (cost per 1,000 impressions) → online ads average about **$3–$10** CPM vs. about **$15–$30** for newspapers or television (→ [Course 3: The cost of advertising on social media](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))
	2. **Global reach**: global traditional campaigns could cost millions and require extensive research → online ads target specific sections of the world, even an internet user in another country
	3. **Data for effective brand strategizing**: a billboard on a busy highway gives little insight (drivers may move too fast to read it; you never know how many paid attention or searched for you) ↔ online ads track where ads were seen, how many people clicked, how many clicks led to a purchase → where to spend ad dollars
	4. **Quick and easy to produce**: a TV ad needs actors, a script, a production crew ↔ online ads use **automated structures** (input information, generate the ad)
- The most common online ads are powered by **Google Ads** (Google = the most popular search engine globally) → search, browse, and compare products across brands and retailers
#### Platforms for e-commerce ads
- **E-commerce advertising**: any form of online or offline advertising that raises awareness of and drives traffic to an e-commerce business
- **Google Ads**: boost brand awareness or acquire new customers → **Search ads** on Google search engine results pages (**SERPs**) and **display ads** on websites via the **Google Display Network**; tremendous reach (→ [Course 2: How Google Ads works](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
- **Google Shopping ads**: image-based product ads showing a product photo, title and price, store name, and any reviews → shown every time a relevant search is made; a click leads to the product page → drive traffic and conversions

- **YouTube Ads**: more than a billion hours of video viewed daily → video ads and campaigns aligned with marketing goals → especially effective for educational or informational content (product demonstrations, reviews, explainer videos)
- **Amazon Ads**: a platform designed for shopping → bid on keywords and placements to show products higher in search results; sponsored products, sponsored brands, sponsored display ads, video ads, audio ads
- **Facebook and Instagram Ads** (parent company **Meta**): **Ads Manager** = an all-in-one tool to create, manage, and track ads
	- **Facebook Ads**: the most users of any social media platform (billions of active accounts) → e.g. **Collection ads** (videos or images from a business's catalog, seamless move from discovery to purchase); targeting by location, occupation, interests, past activity, ... (→ [Course 3: Ad formats on social media](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))

### Google Shopping campaigns
#### Advertise with Google Ads campaigns
- E.g. a famous musician's new music video in YouTube's "recommended" section or a link in Google Search ads → a Google Ads campaign at work
- **Campaign**: a tool that creates and initiates a plan for how to promote products or services on Google's network (search results, websites, videos, mobile apps, maps, shopping listings, ...) → uses a set of **ad groups** (ads, keywords, bids) that share a budget, location targets, and specific audiences (→ [Course 7: Dynamic remarketing and ShearShare](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
	- Many campaign types (e.g. **App campaigns** for app developers to find new users and increase in-app sales)
- Four campaign types you'll likely encounter in e-commerce:
	- **Shopping campaigns**: product listings on search results and the **Google Shopping tab** → photo, title, price, store name, ... → ideal for e-commerce retailers selling product inventory
		- Products appear next to competitors → customers compare appearance and price (benefits great-quality, affordable products; hurts poor-looking or too-expensive ones)
		- Promote online inventory, boost traffic to the store, visually engaging listings, boost sales or sign-ups
	- **Search campaigns**: text ads on search results while people search on Google for related products and services → reach customers who know what they're looking for and are ready to buy (→ [Course 2: Understand the benefits of SEM](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
		- Example: **Jamie**, a busy restaurant manager, forgot to order flowers for her 20th wedding anniversary → searches "flowers" → nearly seven billion results → a local florist's Search campaign would show same-day delivery at the top
	- Search and Shopping run on a **PPC (pay-per-click)** model: the company only pays when somebody clicks the ad to visit the website
	- **Display campaigns**: a Google Ads tool to place an image advertisement across various websites → any platform in the **Google Display Network** (more than **two million** websites, videos, and apps; reaches over **90%** of internet users worldwide) → "digital billboards", often artistic (art, graphic design, photography, typography)
	- **Video campaigns**: YouTube had over **2.3 billion** users worldwide as of 2021 → a Google Ads tool to place video ads before, during, or after YouTube videos and in search results
		- Get noticed on YouTube; meet customers at the start of their buying journey, spread brand awareness, remind returning customers
		- Limited time to make an impression → a lot of work to be engaging → many businesses hire ad agencies or professional video producers
- These four are **regular Google Ads campaigns** → complete control of goals, keywords, conversion metrics, and budget decisions (require knowledge to use)
#### Understand Smart Campaigns
- **Smart campaign**: an automated campaign management tool within Google Ads that helps you promote your business → originally **Google AdWords Express**, designed for small businesses and new advertisers; combines Search and Display campaigns, Google controls most of the management
- Advantages:
	- **Automated**: Google controls the campaign's direction and how the budget is spent → less time checking in
	- **Easier to set up**: under **15 minutes**, only a few steps (regular campaigns need more)
	- **Save money**: uses detailed analytics and data to determine the highest conversion rate → clickers more likely to buy (less paying for clicks without purchases)
- Limitations: limited campaign goals, limited keyword options, limited budget control
- **Smart Shopping campaign**: an advanced shopping campaign that uses technology to optimize for more sales and reach Google shoppers across Google's sites and networks (Google Search Network, Google Display Network, YouTube, Gmail) → the best Smart campaign for e-commerce
	- Optimizes real-time signals (user queries, time of day, devices) → shows products to customers more likely to buy
	- A study found over **20%** more conversion value than regular Shopping campaigns
	- Three benefits:
		1. **Optimize existing shopping ad products**: combine your single product feed and assets into ads across networks → Google tests them and shows the best performers
		2. **Automatic bidding**: Google predicts the value of a potential conversion for each search and adjusts bids to maximize return
		3. **Start from your e-commerce platform**: easy integration through third-party platforms (Shopify, WooCommerce)

### E-commerce trends and seasonality
#### Explore seasonality in e-commerce
- **Seasonality** (also **seasonal marketing**): the regular and predictable fluctuation of e-commerce traffic around special holidays, events, and weather on a quarterly or yearly basis → determine when a business will see a potential increase or slowdown in revenue
- **On-season**: the period where customers are much more likely to buy due to weather variables or special events → the most financial gain
	- E.g. **sandals** in warm weather seasons → a period of rapid economic expansion = a **boom**
	- Holidays and special events: e.g. florists → globally, the week leading to **Mother's Day** is the industry's busiest week (even where it's not the prime flower-growing season); also **back-to-school** events and **cyber sales week**
- **Off-season**: customers take more time to buy (especially larger-ticket items); relatively low site traffic and revenue
	- E.g. sandals in winter → a period of poor performance or inactivity = a **slump**
- **Off-season marketing**: put your business ahead of the competition by building strong brand awareness that drives customers to your site during the on-season:
	1. **Grow your database**: target new contacts and attract new audiences → conversions give customer information for on-season promotions
	2. **Increase your social media presence**: interact with customers, create engaging posts → stay connected all year (→ [Course 3: Social media engagement](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))
	3. **Create quality content**: measured by interactions and conversions → use the site's **blog** to build a community and interest in related products (e.g. a soccer apparel company posts an interview with a professional player during the off-season → draws customers to discounted jersey sales, brand remembered in the on-season)
	4. **Promote less popular products**: recover revenue lost from popular items → feature them on the homepage, dedicated campaigns, creative branding
	5. **Geographic segmentation**: grouping customers by their physical location → on- and off-seasons differ by location (e.g. a shoe seller targets sandals to warm locations and **boots** to cooler ones)
#### Use Google Ads to plan for seasonality
- **Google Ads**: an online advertising platform where advertisers bid to display brief advertisements, service offerings, product listings, or videos to web users → revisit goals and demand trends during the off-season
- **Smart Bidding**: a subset of automated bid strategies that use machine learning to optimize for conversions or conversion value in each and every auction → adjust settings manually or use **seasonality adjustments**
	- **Seasonality adjustment** tool: set a time and date when you expect a short spike in conversion rate → informs Smart Bidding when to change bidding behavior
	- Example: a small business in **Singapore** making festive party decorations (open five years, steady sales) → demand spikes for a week before **Lunar New Year** → a seasonality adjustment raising the conversion rate by up to **50%** for those days → afterwards Smart Bidding immediately returns to pre-sales performance
- Smart Bidding strategies for Shopping campaigns:
	- **Maximize conversion value**: fully automated → Google adjusts bids for the highest number of store visits; always spends the **full daily budget** → with multiple Shopping campaigns, decide on separate daily budgets or one shared budget (don't let one campaign spend it all)
	- **Target ROAS (return on ad spend)**: Google optimizes bids within the daily budget to meet your target → bids high on searches likely to generate a high-value conversion, low otherwise
		- Needs enough previous conversion data
		- Start with a realistic target a little lower than recent performance → slowly raise it over time
- **New customer acquisition**: since 2020, customers are more willing to try new brands; holidays are historically great for new customers → in campaign **Settings**, choose conversion goals and add the value of new vs. existing customers → optimizes for new-customer sales
- **Performance Planner**: forecast the impact of different spending scenarios and events in upcoming seasons; determine bid and budget settings for year-round campaigns
	- Higher **consumer intent** in certain periods (e.g. in winter people search for a new coat directly) → more conversions
	- Examines previous campaign performance and seasonal trends for your industry and region → set the conversion rate estimate to match the prior year for the period, set a custom conversion rate at the plan level, or define rates per campaign
#### Marketing for special events and holidays
- **Understanding geographic segmentation**: start with where your audience is → **target market**: a group of customers at which a business aims its marketing efforts and resources
	- E.g. **Black Friday** (the Friday after US Thanksgiving) = the single busiest shopping day for US retailers → a Black Friday campaign won't get attention in a country that doesn't participate
- Holidays and events (religious observances, cultural happenings, education events):
	- Region- or culture-specific: **Diwali** (festival of lights; dates change annually with the moon; arguably the most widely celebrated holiday in India, also by Hindus worldwide), **Black Friday**, **Singles Day** (November 11, unofficial Chinese holiday and shopping event celebrating people not in relationships)
	- **"The holiday season"**: late November to early January in many Western and other countries
	- Annual across regions and cultures: **New Year celebrations** (January 1; also Lunar New Year, Rosh Hashanah, Songkran), **back-to-school**, **Mother's Day and Father's Day** (dates differ by country)
- Planning for events:
	1. **Create a promotional calendar**: list important holidays and events → connect them to the periods before them with increased traffic (e.g. school starts in Paris September 2–9 → back-to-school sales peak **July 20–28**)
	2. **Choose additional marketing and promotional tactics** (social media, email, ...) that fit your audience → e.g. Instagram and TikTok have younger audiences than Facebook or YouTube
	3. **Assign specific promotions to each holiday or occasion** (50% off an entire purchase? free shipping on orders over $50?) → rank events by expected traffic gain and adjust the yearly advertising budget → mindful of ROI; stay competitive (the least generous promotions lose sales)
	4. **Prepare inventory** months in advance → running out of stock loses revenue and looks unfavorable; analyze sales data, stock up on popular products
		- To avoid surplus, most retailers **loosen efficiency goals** in the on-season (e.g. reduce sales efficiency goals by **20%** during the holidays → sell more sooner, avoid deep off-season discounts)
- Marketing for special events and holidays: https://www.coursera.org/learn/make-the-sale/supplement/HfsRh
#### Optimize your e-commerce marketing strategies
- Techniques to boost traffic and get customers to buy:
	1. **Research the industry's booms and slumps** with **Google Trends** (a Google tool to explore what people around the world search for on Google) → enter words or topics related to your products → search interest relative to the highest point on the chart for the region and time
		- Example: a florist searches "flowers" → largest spike around **May** (Mother's Day) and a second spike around **mid-February** (Valentine's Day)
		- Create promotional events matching the spikes: limited-time deals, discounts, free shipping
	2. **Simplify the buying process**: a **shopping cart abandoner** starts checkout but never completes the purchase (hard-to-find checkout button, unnecessary steps, frustration)
		- **Pain point**: a specific problem faced by current or prospective customers while interacting with the site → resolving pain points improves the buyer's experience and can bring customers back
		- Companies may hire **user experience (UX) designers**; at entry level you'll likely identify where shoppers left (**shopping cart abandonment**); small businesses use customer surveys or focus groups, then the platform's resources to simplify the purchase
	3. **Reconsider pricing**: bring in more money than you spend (ROI), but high prices can turn customers away → reasonable prices for new and returning buyers (don't undervalue)
		- **Competitive pricing**: match or go below competitors' prices → stands out on the Google Shopping tab; calculate reasonable ROI data first
- Optimization is ongoing → check in on results regularly, try a different approach if needed
#### Plan for seasonal events
- During special events and holidays, stores want higher conversion rates (people clicking your ads) → prepare with the **Performance Planner**
- **Performance Planner**: a Google Ads tool to forecast your Search, Display, and Shopping campaigns' performance based on your budget → predict holiday and special-event outcomes; addresses seasonality by business type and location; review how campaign changes affect conversions, conversion value, clicks
	- Helps: explore outcomes by adjusting campaign settings, understand opportunities in seasonal periods, manage budgets across accounts and campaigns
	- How it works: uses your campaign history and billions of search queries to create **forecasts** → plans for your advertising budget
	- Unlike Smart Bidding adjustments, it relies on **existing campaigns**; with enough clicks but too few conversions, manually enter a conversion rate
- Requirements (standard or Smart Shopping campaign):
	- At least **100 impressions** in the last seven days
	- At least **10 conversions** and/or conversion values within the last 10 days
	- Active each day with a minimum spend of **$10 USD** or more in the last 10 days
- Resources: "Get your campaigns ready for the holidays", "About Performance Planner"
- Plan for seasonal events: https://www.coursera.org/learn/make-the-sale/supplement/iMmmD
#### Seasonal budgeting and bidding
- **Set budgets based on business objectives** (e.g. maximum profit during the holiday season) → Performance Planner's data visualizations support budget recommendations
- **Law of diminishing returns**: an economic principle stating that if investment in a particular area increases, the rate of profit from that investment will eventually decrease if other variables remain constant → your first $1,000 is probably spent more efficiently than the next $1,000
	- To maximize profit, find where **revenue − ad spend** is highest (the **point of diminishing returns**)

| Ad Spend | Revenue | ROAS | Profit |
| -------- | ------- | ---- | ------ |
| $0 | $0 | N/A | $0 |
| $5,000 | $10,000 | 200% | $5,000 |
| $10,000 | $19,000 | 190% | $9,000 |
| $15,000 | $27,000 | 180% | $12,000 |
| $20,000 | $34,000 | 170% | $14,000 |
| $25,000 | $40,000 | 160% | $15,000 |
| $30,000 | $44,000 | 147% | $14,000 |
| $35,000 | $46,000 | 131% | $11,000 |
| $40,000 | $47,000 | 118% | $7,000 |

- → $25K spend gives $15K profit vs. $14K at $30K → **$25K** is the recommended budget
- **Set bids based on seasonal trends** (without automated bidding, base bids on recent performance):
	- **Revenue-per-click**: the average revenue for each click on all your pay-per-click keywords and ads; **ROAS**: revenue gained for each dollar spent
	- **Target bid = revenue-per-click ÷ ROAS target** → e.g. $2 ÷ 160% = $1.25
	- Holidays bring swings in performance → compare **year-over-year (YoY)** performance on holiday days to the average (e.g. Black Friday revenue-per-click **565%** higher than average → bid that much higher for a similar ROAS); **seasonality adjustments** in Smart Bidding can do this automatically
	- **Bid calendar**: colors for revenue-per-click (lower than average = red, average = yellow, somewhat above = light blue, a lot above = blue, substantially above = dark blue) (calendar: ...)
	- **Bid increase percentage = (projected revenue-per-click − average revenue-per-click) ÷ average revenue-per-click × 100** → e.g. ($5 − $2) ÷ $2 × 100 = **150%**
	- With automated bidding you may not need the formula all season, but still know when to expect increases and decreases in bids, ad spend, and revenue; "seasonality adjustments" often require similar calculations for expected conversion-rate changes

### Designing your e-commerce store for success
#### Welcome to module 4
- After building a store and attracting customers: how to build a relationship online and provide an experience that makes them come back
- In this module: engage customers → a personalized experience for each customer → product reviews, questions, and feedback → optimize the checkout process → getting products to customers (shipping and fulfillment)
#### Create an engaging online experience for customers
- **Customer engagement**: the interactions and emotional connection between a customer and a brand → in multiple ways on multiple channels throughout the customer journey
- According to HubSpot, **93%** of consumers are more likely to become repeat customers at companies with excellent customer service; **77%** share positive experiences with friends on social media or review sites
- **Brand evangelists**: customers who are so passionate about a product or service that they enthusiastically promote it to others (brag to friends and family, post on social media)
- **Personalization**: the practice of delivering a customized experience for each customer → the same personal attention as in a store, but online
	- E.g. custom suits: in store, staff help choose and take measurements ↔ online, a **questionnaire** to find a suit + a **video** guiding customers to take their own measurements
	- **Personalized recommendations** from data: new visitors → best-selling or highest-rated products; signed-in returning customers → based on browsing history or recent purchases; both → related products when browsing or adding to cart (similar products, products bought by other customers, accessories) on product pages and in the shopping cart
- **Rewards program**: a marketing strategy designed to build customer loyalty by providing incentives for customers to continue shopping with the brand → personalization (discount code for signing up, free birthday gift, choose a reward from a list); rewards for **referrals** attract more customers
- More engagement → lasting customer relationships → a more successful e-commerce business
#### Responsive and mobile-first design for e-commerce stores
- **Responsive design**: a web design approach that allows websites to automatically adapt to changes in device display sizes (images, fonts, other HTML elements scale to any screen) (→ [Course 7: Portfolio on Google Sites](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
- **Mobile-first design**: a web design approach that aims to create better experiences by designing specifically for mobile devices → a stripped-down version of the larger website tailored to smartphone screens
- Responsive design:
	- Pros: ideal for **content-heavy websites** (shopping platforms with a wide range of goods; assumes mostly full-size displays → long forms, complex CTAs); cost-effective development and maintenance (one flexible layout); one site for all devices; **one URL** (one server, no extra apps); no **redirects** needed (faster access)
	- Cons: **loss of features** (graphic elements, functions) on smaller screens; **slow loading** on mobile (designed large to small with high-resolution graphics)
- Mobile-first design:
	- Pros: aligns with current trends (Statista projects that by 2025 more than **10%** of US retail sales will come from mobile commerce; more than half of worldwide web traffic is on mobile phones); better user experience (app-like interface, less text, larger font, faster download); improved **accessibility** (readability for people with visual impairments); streamlined, well-organized content (simpler customer journey); **fewer bugs** (simpler code)
	- Cons: **cost** (different versions for different operating systems, higher development and design cost); **different content** (limited mobile content vs. broader responsive versions can confuse users)
- Choosing: gather information about users, create **customer personas**, outline the website's purpose (who, how they access the site, what actions you want) → **80–20 rule**: if 80% of users are on desktops/laptops → responsive; if 80% shop on mobile → mobile-first (→ [Course 2: Customer personas](/blog/attract-and-engage-customers-with-digital-marketing/))
	- Sources: **Google Analytics** (how people access your site), industry statistics, user testing, customer surveys
- Responsive and mobile-first design for e-commerce stores: https://www.coursera.org/learn/make-the-sale/supplement/xJZNc
#### Tips for increasing customer engagement
- Getting customers to the store isn't enough → keep them engaged (repeat customers, word of mouth; 77% share positive experiences = **brand evangelists**)
- **Tip #1: Enable product ratings and reviews**
	- **89%** of consumers worldwide read reviews before buying; **79%** trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations
	- Conversion rates for product pages with reviews are up to **3.5 times** higher
	- Reduce **returns** (more information → better decisions)
	- Types: **star ratings** (easiest to receive, one to five stars), **written reviews** (why a customer likes or dislikes the product), **customer-uploaded photos and videos** (product in use, e.g. a dress photo influences customers of similar size)
- **Tip #2: Ensure a good user experience**: **user experience** = how a person, the user, feels about interacting with, or experiencing, a product (here, your website)
	- Easy search and menus; easy access to FAQ, return and exchanges policy, customer service contact
	- Optimize for mobile and tablets: a **responsive website**, maybe an app
	- High-quality images and detailed product information; **virtual experiences** (try on jewelry, picture how a TV fits in the living room)
	- Simple, intuitive navigation, including checkout; more on UX in the **Google UX Design certificate**
- **Tip #3: Personalize the customer experience**: Accenture: **91%** of consumers are more likely to shop with brands that provide relevant offers and recommendations
	- **Product recommendation engine**: software that uses **artificial intelligence** to analyze customers' data, learn which products might interest them, and display them (browsing history, recent purchases, saved preferences; e.g. a laptop buyer → keyboard, mouse, accessories)
	- Tailored offers: an automatic email campaign → after buying a laptop, an email for **10% off** a keyboard and mouse (→ [Course 4: Retention emails](/blog/think-outside-the-inbox-email-marketing/))
	- Personalized **rewards program** (a special offer or gift on the customer's birthday)
	- A questionnaire to guide to the right product, online chat, monograms, gift messages → unique personalization sets you apart from competitors

### Creating a successful path to purchase for your e-commerce store
#### Introduction to the checkout process
- Around **70%** of customers abandon their online shopping carts without completing their purchase → issues during checkout or not trusting that personal information is secure → they leave (maybe to a competitor)
- The checkout process = the final step of the path to purchase, where the customer either completes the purchase or leaves → must be **fast**, **easy to understand**, **secure**, and **mobile friendly**
- Basic steps (vary by store):
	1. **Enter the checkout process**: click the call-to-action button in the shopping cart (any time after adding an item)
		- Sign in to a store account (auto-fills name, address, email, ...), sign in with a **social account** (Google, Apple, Facebook), **check out as a guest**, or create an account
	2. **Contact information for shipping and billing**: name, address, phone number, email; a separate shipping address if different
	3. **Shipping and delivery option** (if multiple); store pickup may be an option
	4. **Payment method and payment information** → captured by a **point-of-sale (POS)** system: an e-commerce POS system is software that allows a business to process payment transactions from customers online (typically built into the platform, e.g. Shopify's); some process both digital and in-person transactions (one POS for click-and-mortar stores)
		- Possibly a promo code field, email updates sign-up, other options
	5. **Review the order**: total cost and breakdown (subtotal, taxes, shipping and handling, additional fees)
	6. **Confirm the order** (call-to-action button) → an **order confirmation** message
- The customer can abandon at any point → make checkout as smooth, easy, and secure as possible
#### Common reasons for cart abandonment
- Often customers are just browsing or not ready to buy; excluding them:
	- **Extra costs** (shipping, taxes, fees) too high → the biggest reason (around **half** of customers); also not knowing the total order cost before checkout → free shipping and no unexpected fees help
	- **Required account creation**: an extra step; wary of marketing emails or of personal information saved on the site; extra time and effort
	- **Checkout too complicated or too long**; also must be easy to navigate on mobile (a growing trend)
	- **Not trusting the store** with credit card information (security concerns)
	- **Preferred payment method not available**
	- **Slow delivery times** (even with free shipping)
	- **Issues during checkout**: a promo code not working, error messages, the website crashing → doubt and lack of trust
	- **Unsatisfactory return policy** → a generous return policy instills confidence
- → optimizing checkout improves the customer experience and the store's success
#### Tips for reducing cart abandonment
- **Cart abandonment**: when a customer adds an item to their cart but doesn't complete the purchase (around 70%) (→ [Course 7: Cart abandonment rate](/blog/satisfaction-guaranteed-develop-customer-loyalty-online/))
- Reasons and solutions:
	1. **Just browsing or not ready to buy** → **recapture the customer's attention**:
		- **Dynamic remarketing**: show previous visitors ads containing products and services they viewed on your site (→ [Course 3: Remarketing on social media](/blog/from-likes-to-leads-interact-with-customers-online/))
		- **Abandoned cart email**: a follow-up email sent to customers who added an item to their cart but didn't complete the purchase (+ incentive: free shipping or a discount) → about **11%** click through and complete the purchase; can be automated
	2. **Extra costs for shipping, taxes, or fees** → **offer free shipping** (or over a threshold, e.g. orders of **$25** or more); be transparent about the total cost before checkout (estimate shipping by zip code on the product page or in the cart)
	3. **Requires an account** → **enable guest checkout** (no username/password needed); sign in with a social account (Google, Apple, Facebook) for auto-filled information
	4. **Long or complicated checkout** → **simplify**: remove unnecessary fields, only ask for necessary information
	5. **Difficult to navigate on a mobile device** → **design a responsive website**
	6. **Security concerns** → **secure your website**:
		- **HTTPS (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure)**: a method of transferring information over the internet that protects the integrity and confidentiality of data between the user's computer and the site (recommended by Google)
		- Requires a **Transport Layer Security (TLS) certificate** (also **Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) certificate**): a digital certificate that authenticates a website's identity and enables an encrypted connection → display the TLS/SSL symbol during checkout; the **padlock** symbol in the address bar shows the site is secure
	7. **Preferred payment method not available** → **flexible payment options**: debit cards, credit cards, PayPal, mobile payments (Google Pay), installments
	8. **Slow delivery times** → **enable faster shipping**: a faster delivery provider or multiple providers (customer chooses by estimated delivery time); option to pay for faster shipping
	9. **Errors during checkout** → **test for errors** regularly (don't assume customers will report broken things)
	10. **Unsatisfactory return policy** (no returns, too short a period, customer pays return shipping) → **adopt a generous return policy** (the company backs the quality of its products)

#### Get products to customers
- **Order fulfillment**: all the steps between receiving an order and delivering it to the customer → processing the order, picking products from the warehouse or store shelves, packing, shipping
- Three options:
	1. **In-house fulfillment**: store your own inventory, package and label orders, ship them, handle returns and exchanges → the most control over quality, **custom packaging** that fits the brand, but significant time and effort, storage space, and staff
	2. **Dropshipping**: products shipped from the supplier directly to the customer → the drop shipper owns, manages, and stores all inventory and handles the entire fulfillment process (maybe returns too) → outsource a lot of work, launch faster, avoid the risk of buying and storing inventory that may not sell
	3. **Fulfillment service**: a third-party company that prepares and ships orders from their **fulfillment centers** → like dropshipping it handles the whole process, but the **e-commerce store owns the inventory** (delivered to the fulfillment center)
		- Frees the business to focus on selling and engaging customers; usually handles returns; multiple locations → ships from the closest center → faster delivery
- Be flexible with changes in the **supply chain**: the flow of goods from the beginning stage of sourcing raw materials all the way to the finished product that is delivered to the customer → e.g. a computer chip shortage affecting new laptop production → adapt to meet customers' needs
