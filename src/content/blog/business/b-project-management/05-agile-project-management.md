### Introduction to Agile
#### A brief history of Agile
- Review: **Waterfall** = sequential or linear ordering of phases; complete one phase before moving to the next (→ [Course 1: Waterfall and Agile](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/))
- **Agile**: able to move quickly and easily; flexibility, willingness and ability to change and adapt
	- **Iterative approach**: project processes are repeated, often many times, during the life cycle
	- **Iterations**: shorter blocks of time the team works in; may be repeated based on feedback; each iteration completes all the work for a subset of the project's activities → like "a lot of mini waterfalls"
- **Agile project management**: an approach to project and team management based on the **Agile Manifesto** (**4 values** and **12 principles** defining the mindset Agile teams strive for)
	- Waterfall: linear, sequential, does not encourage changing the process once started; Agile: iterative, flexible, incorporates necessary changes throughout
- History:
	- Agile methodologies emerged organically in the **1990s** during the software boom (startups like Google, tech giants experimenting with faster ways to build software) → companies needed to innovate their processes, not just products
	- **2001**: thought leaders and creators of these methodologies met to find common ground → problem: companies focused so much on planning and documenting that they lost sight of **pleasing their customers** → wrote the **Agile Manifesto**: keep the process flexible, focus on people (team and users) over end products or deliverables
- Agile is used beyond software: draws heavily on **Lean manufacturing** principles from **Toyota's** car factories (1930s); adopted in aeronautics, healthcare, education, finance, ...
#### Distinguishing Agile from Waterfall
- Agile was created in response to Waterfall's strict linear process: Waterfall aims for **predictability** and avoids change; Agile embraces that the world, markets, and users are **uncertain and unpredictable** (e.g. customer asks for feature A but actually wanted feature B) → get customer feedback more quickly
- **Agile mindset**: always seek to work more efficiently, streamline processes without reducing quality or value → **reduce waste**
	- Forms of waste: unnecessary documentation; spending weeks or months on a feature customers don't like
	- Remedy: more team and stakeholder **collaboration** → less documentation, earlier feedback
- Three aspects that differ:
	- **Requirements**: conditions that must be met or tasks that must be finished to ensure successful completion of the project
		- Waterfall: product requirements document, several formally approved project plans, possibly a **change control board** (formal process to manage changes) → prevents building something unwanted, minimizes scope creep; works well when the end product is known (e.g. clear requirements from mandated regulation)
		- Agile: requirements are **dynamic**; an initial list grows and changes; prioritized with stakeholders (most urgent/valuable on top); worked on in iterations, with feedback and adjustments at the end of each iteration
	- **Documentation**:
		- Waterfall: lots of documentation because of handoffs between phases and teams, and work done in bigger chunks
		- Agile: emphasis on **real-time, person-to-person conversations**; shorter documents with just enough detail, written only as needed
	- **Deliverables** (a tangible outcome from a project):
		- Waterfall: released at the very end, one big event
		- Agile: **smaller, more frequent releases** → feedback and learning as you go, valuable when there's lots of uncertainty (new industry or market)
#### The four values of the Agile Manifesto
- **Agile Manifesto** (2001, agilemanifesto.org): "We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work, we have come to value":
	1. **Individuals and interactions** over processes and tools
	2. **Working software** over comprehensive documentation
	3. **Customer collaboration** over contract negotiation
	4. **Responding to change** over following a plan
	- "While there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more" → think about both sides, but place more emphasis on the left
- From the four values came **12 principles**; together they inform the why, how, and what of Agile planning and processes
- **Individuals and interactions over processes and tools**: people communicating with each other (e.g. a brief conversation instead of a long email back-and-forth); value individual perspectives and creativity; processes and tools should facilitate collaboration, never be a barrier (not chaos)
- **Working software over comprehensive documentation**: spend time on things that create value, not on debating, writing, and reviewing documents; replace "working software" with whatever the project delivers (a legal brief, an office layout, a sales presentation)
- **Customer collaboration over contract negotiation**: **customer satisfaction** is the highest priority; "contracts" = official documents requiring sign-off (huge requirement documents, formal change requests); collaborate with customers early and often (present early prototypes, ask questions, initial product testing); contracts still exist but focus on what's really needed
- **Responding to change over following a plan**: change is inevitable; larger, longer, more complex projects → more uncertainty; a fixed plan may deliver on time and budget but miss customer needs or maximum value → the most successful projects **smoothly integrate change**; Agile PMs still create and value plans but adapt them
- Find the right balance between the two sides for your industry needs, team dynamics, and organizational goals
#### The 12 principles of the Agile Manifesto
- The 12 principles reinforce the four values; grouped in this course into **four themes** (different from the four values):
	- **Value delivery** (5 principles): how do Agile teams deliver highly valuable products to their customers?
		- Deliver work as quickly as possible → get feedback, mitigate the risk of building the wrong thing; no one gets value (or revenue) until you deliver, and competition may get ahead
		- Replace "software" with "deliverables" or "solutions" (e.g. "deliver working solutions frequently")
		- **Simplicity**: focus on what matters most (extra buttons and features confuse users)
		- Examples: getting feedback on a prototype to learn which features matter; working only on approved features; reserving **10%** of the team's time for bug fixing or polishing a process
	- **Business collaboration** (2 principles): how do Agile teams collaborate with business partners and stakeholders to create business value?
		- "Business people" = sales, marketing, customer support, account management; "developers" = those who make and create products
		- Enable easy access between business people and developers: same office or virtual space, co-locate a day a week, instant messaging, blocked calendar time
		- Instead of keeping customers away from developers (fear of scope creep), hold a **weekly huddle** to explore feedback and new ideas (e.g. discover a valuable feature is easy to build)
	- **Team dynamics and culture** (4 principles): how does a team create and maintain the right interpersonal and team dynamics? → reflects the first value
		- Inclusive, supportive, empowering culture; team is motivated, trusted, has resources and space to work closely, works at a **sustainable pace**
		- Examples: ask the team what equipment they need and provide it; let teams write their own processes and templates instead of forcing ones from headquarters
	- **Retrospectives and continuous learning** (1 principle): "At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly"
		- Set time aside after each iteration: How is the team doing? Are customers happy? Processes to optimize? Are tools working? Following the values? Accumulating **debt** (processes or technology that slows us down)? (→ [Course 4: retrospectives](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
#### Adopting an Agile mindset: The Agile Manifesto
- History: introduced **2001** in the US, at a ski resort in the **Wasatch mountains of Utah**; **17** self-proclaimed "organizational anarchists" combined several lightweight processes → Agile Manifesto; intended for software development but quickly adopted across all fields
- Agile = a highly collaborative approach suited for very complex and competitive projects
- The **12 principles**:
	1. "Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through **early and continuous delivery** of valuable software" → steady value stream, trust and confidence through continuous feedback, early business value realization
	2. "**Welcome changing requirements**, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage" → constantly scan your environment, factor changes into plans
	3. "**Deliver working software frequently**, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale" → regular opportunities for stakeholder feedback, never too long down the wrong path
	4. "**Business people and developers must work together daily** throughout the project" → removes barriers, builds trust, keeps builders in tune with user needs
	5. "Build projects around **motivated individuals**. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done" → trusted by each other and by sponsors and executives
	6. "The most efficient and effective method of conveying information to and within a development team is **face-to-face conversation**" → cues, body language, facial expressions; when not possible, establish effective communication norms
	7. "**Working software is the primary measure of progress**" → show a working, demonstrable piece of the solution (vs. completed documents in Waterfall); "80% done" means nothing without an artifact to review
	8. "Agile processes promote **sustainable development**. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely" → avoid overtime and burnout, but also underutilization (boredom, loss of creativity)
	9. "Continuous attention to **technical excellence and good design** enhances agility" → working fast doesn't mean sacrificing quality; low quality makes changes complex and slow
	10. "**Simplicity**—the art of maximizing the amount of work not done—is essential" → no extra features not requested by the user or product owner, remove unnecessary procedures and documentation
	11. "The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from **self-organizing teams**" → teams design their own processes, feel empowered to speak up
	12. "At regular intervals, the team **reflects** on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly" → learning from successes and failures is continuous
- Adopting an Agile mindset: The Agile Manifesto: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/DQPYq
#### Adopting an Agile mindset
- Agile = delivering value in a world with high degrees of **uncertainty, risk, and competition**; react quickly to new information, market opportunities, technologies → works best in industries or projects susceptible to or encouraging change
	- Changing industries besides software: biotechnology (emerging vaccines, treatments), media, food industry, fashion
	- "Stable" industries (agriculture, aerospace, manufacturing, mining) still must adapt to new laws and regulations, natural disasters, ...; 2020 showed no industry is immune to change
- **VUCA**: developed by the **US military War College**; an acronym defining the conditions that affect organizations in a changing and complex world → helps decide which project management approach is best
	- **Volatility**: the rate of change and churn in a business or situation (the next disruption is always around the corner)
	- **Uncertainty**: the lack of predictability or high potential for surprise (plans rely on many assumptions that could be incorrect)
	- **Complexity**: the high number of interrelated forces, issues, organizations, and factors influencing the project (e.g. diverse and global suppliers)
	- **Ambiguity**: the possibility of misunderstanding the conditions and root causes of events or circumstances (hard to pinpoint causes of delays and design mitigation plans)
- Adopting an Agile approach increases your chances of success despite uncertainty
#### Applying Agile in a VUCA environment
- Examine the project environment before choosing an approach: **high VUCA** → consider an Agile approach; Agile doesn't remove VUCA but gives tools and systems to mitigate its risks
- **Office Green LLC** scenario: commercial landscaping company (interior plant design for offices, restaurants, hotels)
	- Market research: major shift to workers setting up **home offices** → new service **Virtual Verde** for home offices; no existing project plans and no time for prep → you are PM of a scrappy new Agile team
	- VUCA faced: **volatility** (major disruptive change to business plans), **uncertainty** (lack of predictability, hard to make concrete plans), **complexity** (interrelated factors like suppliers and the economy), **ambiguity** (can't determine or control what might cause future changes)
	- Agile → embraced the changing market and stayed flexible instead of letting business erode
### Popular Agile Frameworks
#### Introduction to Scrum
- **Scrum**: by far the most popular methodology under the Agile umbrella; not an acronym → from **rugby**: a formation where players lean forward, lock heads, and work as one unit to gain yards → a heads-down team working very closely together
- **2019 State of Agile report**: **72%** of teams using Agile methods used Scrum or a hybrid
- How it works: a team works together to quickly develop and test a deliverable, in short cycles, meeting daily to discuss tasks and clear blockers
- Key terms:
	- **Backlog**: the central artifact; captures all possible ideas, deliverables, features, or tasks; prioritized and proactively managed by the team throughout the project
	- **Sprint**: the time-boxed period where work is done, **1–4 weeks** (most around **2 weeks**); often called the "iteration"
	- **Daily Scrum** (**Stand-up**): team meets for **15 minutes or less** every day of the Sprint to inspect progress toward the goal
- Roles:
	- **Scrum Master**: ensures the team lives Agile values and principles, follows agreed processes and practices, shares information with the larger project team, helps the team focus on its best work
	- **Product Owner**: maximizes the value of the product and the work of the team; owns the inventory of work and has the final say on prioritization
	- **Development Team**: responsible for how the team will deliver the product
- Why Scrum is popular: clear roles while emphasizing the team as a whole; regular, predictable meetings and delivery schedules with predefined agendas (easy to teach); supports Agile values while adding structure; **free and open**; lots of online guidance, training, certifications
- Best for: **cross-functional** teams of about **3–9 members** ("**pizza-size team**"; too small → lacks diverse skills, too large → hard to distribute information); open-minded, adaptable team and management who value continuous learning (forcing Scrum almost always fails)
- Used beyond software: wedding planning, house moves, building rockets
#### The founding principles of Scrum
- Scrum first used to describe Agile content in **1986** in the **Harvard Business Review**: *The New New Product Development Game* by **Hirotaka Takeuchi** and **Ikujiro Nonaka**, chapter "Moving the Scrum downfield"
- Team characteristics that move the Scrum downfield:
	- **Built-in instability**: freedom to achieve important outcomes with "challenging requirements" → "an element of tension"
	- **Self-organizing teams**: operate like their own start-up, lacking true hierarchy; autonomy, continuous growth, collaboration
	- **Overlapping development phases**: individuals synchronize their pace to meet deadlines → a collective team pace
	- **Multi-learning**: relies heavily on trial and error; stay up-to-date with market conditions and respond quickly
	- **Subtle control**: checkpoints to analyze team interactions and progress → control without hindering creativity
	- **Organizational transfer of learning**: everyone is encouraged to learn new skills while supporting other team members
- "Each element, by itself, does not bring about speed and flexibility. But taken as a whole, the characteristics can produce a powerful new set of dynamics"
- The founding principles of Scrum: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/XrDcm
#### Introduction to Kanban, XP, and Lean
- Methodologies from the 1990s that share Agile values and principles but have specific practices
- **Kanban**: "kan" = sign, "ban" = board (Japanese); can be applied simply or drive the whole project (→ [Course 3: Kanban boards](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- **Kanban board**: transparent visual feedback on the status of work in progress: **To do**, **In progress**, **Done**; digital tools available
	- **Work-in-progress limit** (**WIP limit**): limits in-progress tasks to what the team can handle in a certain time; decided by the team (self-organizing, empowered, sustainable pace); new tasks are added only after finishing previous ones and when below the WIP limit → a started task becomes a priority for the whole team to get to done; less work at once → work gets done faster
	- **Flow**: the goal of maximizing efficiency, a core principle of Kanban
- **Extreme programming** (**XP**): takes traditional software development activities to an extreme level (Sue's first Agile methodology, at **Qualcomm**); can be used outside software
	- Aims to improve product quality and the ability to respond to changing customer needs
	- E.g. **test-first development**: test parts of the product before building them in full → XP tests more and smaller features for more feedback
	- Four basic activities XP enhances:
		- **Designing**: describe the pieces of the product and how they function (e.g. an ad campaign: artwork, copy, ad buy plan); stresses **simplicity** → start with a simple design for the most basic, important requirements, test it, then add features
		- **Coding**: clear and concise code so others can read and troubleshoot it (outside software: clear processes or instructions)
		- **Testing**: check for flaws; in XP "more is better" → eliminate flaws in a feature before continuing; also check features meet customer requirements
		- **Listening**: listen to the customer and integrate requirements into the product (customer collaboration, frequent communication, regular feedback)
	- Other XP practices used across Agile teams:
		- **Pair programming**: two team members work together at the same time on one task (in person or remotely)
		- **Continuous integration and continuous refactoring**: merge product changes into a shared version several times a day → quick feedback on quality
		- **Avoid big design up front**: design just enough to start, improve continuously as the product evolves
		- **Write tests, not requirements**: the test plan tells the team what to build and compares what was built to what was supposed to be built
- **Lean** (seen in Lean Six Sigma → [Course 1: Lean and Six Sigma](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/)): five principles as a recipe for improving project outcomes:
	1. **Define value**: identify and focus on what the customer wants, include the customer; a product's value = the sum of all the things the customer wants
	2. **Map value stream**: map all steps involved in producing value for the customer; challenge wasteful or unnecessary steps
	3. **Create flow**: product flows through the value stream efficiently; remove interruptions, delays, barriers
	4. **Establish pull**: the customer "pulls" on the product (features, incremental deliveries) throughout the value stream → smooth process so you can present work or add a feature request at any time
	5. **Pursue perfection**: continuously improve the first four steps
	- Agile emerged after Lean; its inventors applied Lean manufacturing principles to software; like Agile, Lean is a set of principles and a value system (differences mostly in wording)
- Many teams evolve and blend methods to create what works best for them
#### Blending project management approaches
- Recap: **Kanban** (visualization, managing flow), **XP** (product development best practices to an extreme), **Lean** (predates Agile; eliminate waste, deliver value)
- Two ways of seeing Agile: (1) a **way of thinking** about delivery through the Manifesto's values and principles; (2) delivery **frameworks and methods** (Kanban, XP, Lean, especially Scrum) → the real power of Agile is the **mindset** → you can think in an Agile way even with a Waterfall delivery approach
- Waterfall life cycle: initiation, planning, executing and completing tasks, closing (goals and scope, scheduling, budgeting, risk management) → Agile includes most of the same phases and tasks, done differently
- Reasons to blend Agile and Waterfall:
	- Stakeholders, customers, or sponsors are more comfortable with traditional approaches, but the team is established in Scrum
	- **Regulatory requirements** insisting on traditional work processes (e.g. large requirement documents for certifications)
	- A vendor in a large project follows a traditional approach → integration requires blending
	- → different parts of the project worked on in ways that meet requirements without negatively impacting the project as a whole
- Examples:
	- In a Sprint **retrospective**, a team member lacks experience with a feature → pair them with an expert for the next Sprint = blending **XP** (pair programming) and **Scrum** (retrospectives)
	- Most Scrum teams use a **Kanban board** to track progress through their Sprint
	- Warning: avoid too much change in how you do things; teams work best with consistency
- Office Green (Virtual Verde): include existing plant suppliers (used to the original **Plant Pals** office delivery team; not all want to try Agile) in discussions early to gain buy-in; use **traditional budget management controls** to watch costs
- Key points: Agile is a mindset; its values can be achieved through Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean; Agile and Waterfall both add value, and blending can add even more
#### The Spotify model
- **Spotify** (music streaming) sets a high standard for flexible Agile at scale; Agile coaches **Henrik Kniberg** and **Anders Ivarsson**: constant blending of methods and adapting over time
- **Spotify model**: encourages innovation, collaboration, productivity while maintaining autonomy, quality, communication, using:
	- **Squads**: like a Scrum Team, its own start-up within the company; self-organizing, collocated, long-term mission (e.g. improving Android app usability, the radio experience, payment solutions); no formal leader but a **Product Owner** (POs collaborate on a roadmap); access to an **Agile coach**
	- **Tribes**: collections of Squads working in a specific area, fewer than **100** people
	- **Chapters**: small groups across a Tribe with similar skills in the same competency area
	- **Guilds**: the largest group, people across the organization who share knowledge, tools, code, practices
- Be inspired by the model, but **don't emulate it**: copying an exact model rarely fits another team
- Key takeaways: adapt to your team's preferences and goals (Spotify added groups as it scaled without disrupting Squads); examine your project and organization's needs; don't fear trial and error; never consider yourself done improving

## Module 2: Scrum 101
### The pillars and values of Scrum
#### Introduction: Scrum 101
- **Scrum Guide**: the main source of truth for Scrum Teams, free at Scrumguides.org
- Scrum and Agile are sometimes used interchangeably, but: **Agile** = the foundational philosophy and mindset; **Scrum** = a framework that brings that philosophy to life; Scrum came **before** the Agile Manifesto and inspired it
- Module content: the three pillars of Scrum theory, the five Scrum values, a team mission, the roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team), Scrum artifacts and events
- In Scrum projects, project managers typically take on the **Scrum Master** role → you are the Scrum Master for Office Green's Virtual Verde
#### The three pillars of Scrum
- Scrum Guide: Scrum = a **framework for developing, delivering, and sustaining complex products** (valuable products even in hard-to-predict, risky environments)
- **Iterative and incremental** approach:
	- **Iterative**: project processes are repeated; work in time boxes or iterations
	- **Incremental**: work is divided into smaller chunks that build on each other; each instance of the product built during an iteration is an **increment**
	- → keep checking progress, become more predictable, manage uncertainty
- **Empiricism**: true knowledge comes from actual, lived experience → decisions based on real experience and hard data; each iteration and increment is a **mini-experiment**
- Three pillars of empiricism and Scrum:
	- **Transparency**: make the most significant aspects of the work visible to those responsible for the outcome (team members, senior sponsors, users)
		- Easier with small teams (Scrum Teams are **3–9** people) → avoid mixed signals, communication breakdowns
		- Outside the team, builds **trust** with customers, sponsors, management; more collaboration, fewer mistakes
	- **Inspection**: conducting timely checks toward the outcome of a Sprint goal to detect undesirable variances; stakeholder reviews are an opportunity for growth; more inspections → more improvement
	- **Adaptation**: continuously searching for ways to adjust the project, product, or processes to minimize further deviation or issues; includes immediate fixes and changes so future projects don't repeat past mistakes
#### The five values of Scrum
- Five core values: **commitment, courage, focus, openness, respect**
	- **Commitment**: personally committing to achieving the goals of the Scrum Team (e.g. a teammate familiar with a new technology sets their work aside to help a blocked member learn it)
	- **Courage**: do the right thing and work on tough problems (take on a hard task requiring a new skill; say you're stuck and need help; call out negative behavior) → strengthens resilience
	- **Focus**: everyone focuses on the necessary work within the Sprint and the team's goals (let a member focus on a difficult but necessary part of the solution); the Scrum Master helps the team focus by facilitating activities and events daily
	- **Openness**: team and stakeholders agree to be open about all the work and its challenges; share issues → someone may have a quick solution
	- **Respect**: respect the opinions, skills, and independence of teammates → more likely to listen to feedback
- Values bring the pillars to life:
	- **Transparency** ← openness to share information + focus to share the most relevant
	- **Inspection** ← courage to offer difficult feedback + mutual respect to listen
	- **Adaptation** ← courage to make changes + commitment to follow through
#### The Scrum Guide
- Scrum Guide definition: "A framework within which people can address **complex adaptive problems**, while productively and creatively delivering products of the highest possible value"
- Every Scrum team member subscribes to the **3 pillars** (transparency, inspection, adaptation) and **5 values** (courage, commitment, focus, openness, respect)
- Scrum has few rules and practices, easy to understand, but **challenging to master** → mastery = embodying, living, and promoting the pillars and values
- Further reading: Scrum Guide, Scrum.org
- The Scrum Guide: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/o8jvU
### Scrum roles and responsibilities
#### Essential Scrum roles
- Like positions in soccer (strikers, wingers, defenders, goalkeepers), each Scrum role has responsibilities toward a common objective
- Agile principle: build projects around motivated individuals → motivate with a mission and product vision they care about
	- **Mission**: a short statement that stays constant throughout the process and gives the team something to work toward → **why** we're doing the work
	- **Product vision**: makes clear what outcomes the team is responsible for and where its boundaries are → imagine what the work will be like when done
	- Virtual Verde mission (from Business Development): "Virtual Verde improves users' health and happiness by bringing their at-home workspace to life"; product vision (by the Scrum Team): "Virtual Verde is a living marketplace that transforms the home office"
- Three roles:
	- **Product Owner**: responsible for **what** the team builds and ensuring everyone understands the **why** → "build the right thing" (e.g. capture and promote the team's ideas for delivering plants to home offices)
	- **Development Team**: responsible for **how** the team delivers the product → "build the thing right" (e.g. building the website, integrating billing systems, fixing issues)
	- **Scrum Master**: responsible for **when** the team delivers value to users; roughly equivalent to the traditional project manager → "build the thing fast" (e.g. finding out why a vendor was late, helping prioritize user issues, organizing the demo for the CEO)
	- The whole team works together: the Development Team can contribute to the "what" and "when", the Product Owner to the "how"
- Skills of Scrum Teams (Scrum Guide):
	- **Cross-functional**: deliveries are the accomplishment of the entire team (e.g. software developer, marketing specialist, quality assurance, logistics expert); fresh perspectives add value
	- **Self-organizing**: no manager dictating tasks; rely on the five values; high-performing teams often have a manager **outside** the Scrum Team providing strategic leadership and career development → if a manager starts telling the team what to do, Scrum's benefits collapse
#### Traits of an effective Scrum Master
- **Scrum Master**: promotes and supports the Scrum process by helping everyone understand and implement Scrum (practices, rules, values) → responsible for **helping the team be their very best** (coach individuals to manage external forces, maximize the team's internal potential)
	- E.g. Virtual Verde keeps getting error reports on the website's shopping cart → the Scrum Master notices the pattern and helps find a better solution (a special test plan, extra solution reviews before releases) rather than fixing one error at a time
- Responsibilities:
	- Coach team members on Agile and Scrum practices, rules, values
	- Help manage the **Product Backlog** effectively (= the single authoritative source for things a team works on: all product features, requirements, and activities associated with deliverables)
	- Facilitate Scrum events (e.g. **Sprint Retrospectives** at the end of every Sprint)
	- Remove **blockers** (missing information, access to training or tools)
	- Prevent unhelpful interactions or interruptions from outside the team
- Traits:
	- **Organized**: organize project artifacts, manage Scrum events
	- **Supportive leader**: needs of others and the team first ("How can I help?"), not bossing people around
	- **Facilitator**: productivity and collaboration, every voice heard
	- **Coach**: encourages dialogue and discussion over giving answers directly
	- **Great communicator**: especially with diverse stakeholders with competing perspectives and styles
- Scrum Master vs. traditional project manager: can be quite different, but often played by the same person with a similar skill set
	- Scrum Master's first job: facilitator and coach to the team; if the company requires many traditional PM activities (budget management, risk spreadsheets, **Gantt charts** → [Course 4: tracking methods](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/)), it may hire separate project managers
	- Traditional PMs often become Scrum Masters (decision making, communicating, flexibility, organizational skills)
#### Pete: What makes an effective Scrum Master
- Pete: Technical Program Manager at Google (end-to-end execution of programs); uses Agile as a core part of deliveries → iterate on the process, deliver faster than traditional models
- Scrum = a single implementation of Agile; rapid value through iterative work over short periods; a **self-organizing team** → everyone must be clear on roles and responsibilities; a leader (Scrum Master) ensures everyone works toward the same goal
- Qualities of an effective Scrum Master:
	- A good **teacher and communicator** → instill Scrum values throughout the team
	- **Influential**: usually no managerial authority over the team → lead through influence: ensure the team operates effectively and build a **culture of motivation**
	- Give direction and keep the team working toward the **Sprint** goals (a Sprint = a focused effort toward well-defined goals, "a burst of energy")
#### Traits of an effective Product Owner
- **Product Owner**: ensures the team builds the right product or service; responsible for **continuously maximizing the value** of the product delivered; key activity: acting as the **voice of the customer**, expressed through ownership of the **Product Backlog**
- Responsibilities: help the team understand **why** their work matters; **prioritize** the Product Backlog to optimize delivery and value; keep the Backlog **visible and transparent** to all; make sure the product fulfills customer needs
- Traits:
	- **Customer-focused**: understands customer needs and the industry extremely well
	- **Decisive**: great communicator, understands both sides of an issue to defend decisions
	- **Flexible**: open to new information that can generate profitable change
	- **Optimistic and positive**: delivers the product vision, inspires the team to believe in the mission
	- **Available**: the iterative nature of Scrum needs the PO to inspect, adapt, and plan the next iteration regularly
	- **Collaborative**: works with the team and several stakeholders to meet customer needs
- Virtual Verde example: PO's order = flower arrangements, potted succulents, large potted plants, herb gardens → the Development Team's vendor specialist knows **herb gardens** (thought difficult as regulated food items) are easy because the vendor has many in stock → a flexible, customer-focused PO reprioritizes herb gardens first
#### Traits of an effective Development Team
- **Development Team**: the people who do the work to build the product; **3–9** people (nimble yet able to complete significant work each Sprint; too small → lacks skills and ideas, too large → too many opinions and communication streams)
- Traits: **cross-functional** (all required skills in-house), owns its processes and structures (**self-organizing**), operates as a team rather than individuals, **customer-oriented** (focus on the user)
- **Co-located** (same physical space) vs. **virtual**:
	- Example: a plant vendor can't make the holiday rush due to quality issues → the quality assurance specialist could fly out to help, leaving tasks undone → co-located team can quickly huddle and rearrange work; distributed or different time zones → harder (phone, email, scheduling), workarounds may delay the project
	- Virtual work is possible (dial in, Zoom, Skype) → both have benefits and drawbacks; use what's best for your team
- Recap: Product Owner = "building the right thing"; Development Team = "building the thing right"; Scrum Master = "building the thing fast" → no role is more or less important
#### Understanding Scrum Team roles
- **Scrum Master** (Scrum Guide): "accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide" by helping everyone understand Scrum theory and practice, and "accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness" by enabling the team to improve its practices
	- Makes sure important meetings (e.g. Daily Scrum) occur and stay within the **timebox** (a Scrum concept that refers to the estimated duration for an event), like a coach watching the game clock
	- Duties: coach self-management and cross-functionality; help the team create high-value Increments that meet the **Definition of Done** (an agreed upon set of items that must be completed before a project or user story can be considered complete); cause the removal of **impediments**; ensure all Scrum events take place and are positive, productive, and timeboxed
	- vs. project manager: does **not** manage changes in scope or priorities, does not maintain traditional artifacts like Gantt charts
- **Product Owner** (Scrum Guide): "responsible for maximizing the value of the product resulting from work of the Scrum Team" → represents the voice of the customer throughout the project
	- Duties: develop and explicitly communicate the **Product Goal**; create and clearly communicate **Product Backlog** items (all features, requirements, activities associated with deliverables); ensure the Backlog is transparent, visible, understood
	- vs. project manager: in Scrum, **scope** definition and management belong to the PO (in traditional PM, to the project manager); the PO is not a manager and not responsible for team performance; both share **stakeholder management**
	- In many companies (including Google), product/solution scope belongs to a separate **product manager** → when joining a company, find out how it approaches product definition, requirements, and user research
- **Development Team** (**Developers**): "the people in the Scrum Team that are committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint"
	- Responsibilities: create the **Sprint Backlog** (the set of Product Backlog items selected to be completed during the upcoming Sprint); instill quality by adhering to a Definition of Done; adapt their plan each day toward the **Sprint Goal**; hold each other accountable; design, build, and test Backlog items in increments
	- **Cross-functional**: software team (web developer, database developer, UX specialist); marketing team (writers, editors, SEO specialists, business analysts)
- Necessary traits: constant collaboration and improvement, value feedback, bring energy and fun, admit and learn from mistakes
	- **Product Owner**: confidently gives direction, requirements, objectives but lets the team decide how; promotes the vision, prioritizes the Backlog; organized, strong communicator
	- **Scrum Master**: strong leadership; efficient facilitator and negotiator who resolves conflict; coaches the Development Team, facilitates events, eliminates distractions
	- **Development Team**: focused on completing deliverables and a superior product; eager to work together, willing to compromise for the product

#### Building a Product Backlog
- **Product Backlog**: the single authoritative source for things a team works on; contains all features, requirements, and activities associated with deliverables; traditional equivalent = the **set of project requirements**
- Three key features:
	- A **living artifact**: items added at any time, evolves through the project life cycle, central guide for what to work on next
	- **Owned and adjusted by the Product Owner**
	- Always a **prioritized** list: new items added in order of importance; items at the top are specific and well-defined, vague items at the bottom
- Data to capture for each Backlog item:
	- **Description**: clear, with essential details from the customer's perspective (e.g. "As a Virtual Verde client, I plan to grow my choice of vegetables while I work from home in my New York City apartment" → an action and a location)
	- **Value**: how much business value the item delivers to customers, the team, or users; format chosen by the Scrum Team (e.g. **$** low value to **$$$$** high value)
	- **Estimate**: how much effort the team thinks an item will take (**relative effort estimation**); owned by the **Development Team**
	- **Order**: estimate and value help the PO place the item ("how important is this compared to all other items?"); ordered highest to lowest priority = **stacked rank**
- Virtual Verde example: market research shows home workers prefer easy-care plants → succulents (1) above orchids (2); a user asks Support for **bonsai trees** (also hard to care for) → more users requested orchids → orchids **$$**, bonsai **$**, bonsai last
- Include as much as you can without stressing about unknowns: e.g. bonsai cost (high-end vs. low-end market) unknown → document an **assumption** in the description, study it when higher on the list
#### Product Backlog: The Scrum Guide overview
- **Product Backlog**: an ordered list of what needs to be done to improve a product; the single authoritative source for items the Scrum Team works on
- **Product Backlog refinement**: items are broken down and further defined by adding details (description, value, order, estimate, size)
- **Product Goal**: the long-term objective for the Scrum Team, included in the Product Backlog; the rest of the Backlog defines what will fulfill it

#### Writing user stories
- **User stories**: short, simple descriptions of a feature told from the perspective of the user → solutions centered on the user and user experience; can start large and broad, then be broken down
- Three elements: the **user**, the **action**, the **benefit** → common format: "As a **[user role]**, I want **[this action]** so that I can get **[this value]**"
- **Personas**: detailed descriptions of different users, sometimes with names (Virtual Verde):
	- **Leo**: plant vendor (acquiring plants, supply chain, delivery logistics)
	- **Felicity**: gardening expert helping the support team advise customers
	- **Zach**: amateur vegetable gardener who wants to cook with the plants
	- **Nia**: management consultant working from home, wants a professional backdrop for video conferences
	- **Reena**: flower aficionado, wants a different flower arrangement each week
- **INVEST** criteria for user stories:
	- **I – Independent**: can be started and finished by itself, not dependent on another story
	- **N – Negotiable**: room for negotiation and discussion
	- **V – Valuable**: completing it delivers value
	- **E – Estimable**: the Definition of Done is clear so the team can estimate it
	- **S – Small**: fits within a planned Sprint; otherwise break it down (low-priority stories can stay big until they become a priority)
	- **T – Testable**: a test can be written to check it meets the acceptance criteria
	- The **Product Owner** mainly writes user stories; the team gives feedback on clarity and INVEST fit before investing time
- **Epic**: a group or collection of user stories (Virtual Verde: live plant delivery, office plant advice service, vendor management, client data management)
- Sample story (live plant delivery epic): "As a Virtual Verde client, I would like to acquire a bonsai tree so that I can have a beautiful plant and I can meditate as I trim the branches" (in Japan, pruning bonsai is a meditative practice)
- **Acceptance criteria**: the checklist (created by the PO) used to decide whether the user story is done; e.g. users can:
	- Browse three different types of bonsai trees
	- Compare them to know which is easiest/hardest to grow (beginner, intermediate, advanced gardener notation)
	- Purchase bonsai care packages (fertilizer, trimming shears, ...)
	- Access an online bonsai booklet and a care booklet packaged with the tree
	- Find a troubleshooting bonsai issues page in the FAQ
- Higher-priority items have more detail and fewer gray areas; leaving low-priority items vague saves time on items that may be deprioritized
#### The elements of user stories and epics
- **User stories**: short, simple descriptions of a deliverable told from the perspective of the user; **epics**: a collection of user stories (like a book series: a story is one narrative, an epic gives the overall arc)
- User stories put the customer first, provide insight into user goals, enable collaboration, inspire creative solutions, and create momentum with small wins
- Elements to include:
	- **User persona**: what the user is like, relation to the project, goals
	- **Definition of Done**: an agreed upon set of items that must be completed before a user story can be considered complete
	- **Tasks**: key activities needed to complete the story
	- **Any feedback already provided** (from customers on a past iteration)
- **INVEST** recap: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable
- Example (local library website for reading reviews before checking out books): "As an avid reader, I want to be able to read reviews before I check out a book from my local branch so that I know I am getting a book I am interested in"
- **Epic**: Mike Cohn (inventor of the term in Scrum) describes it as a "very large user story" that can't be delivered in a single iteration and may need to be split
	- Library examples: reading reviews, adding books to cart → "website creation" epic; easily finding the non-fiction section → "organization of the physical space" epic
- The Product Owner can write user stories and epics, but a **Developer** can too, as long as the PO remains accountable for the Product Backlog item
- Key takeaway: epics track large, loosely-defined ideas; user stories are smaller units of work inspired by the end user

#### Backlog refinement and effort estimation
- The Product Owner owns the Backlog data, but the whole team keeps it up to date
- **Backlog refinement**: keeping the Backlog described, estimated, and prioritized so the Scrum Team can operate effectively; the PO and some or all of the team review the Backlog to ensure:
	- It contains the appropriate items (nothing new needed, nothing to remove)
	- Items are prioritized by the PO (setting the **order** field)
	- Items at the top are ready for delivery with clear acceptance criteria
	- Items include **estimates** (an informed assessment of how much work an item will be)
- Humans tend to **underestimate** time to completion, multiplied in big projects → root cause of late, over-budget projects → Scrum uses **relative estimation** instead of **absolute estimation** (= time and effort estimation in traditional PM)
	- **Relative estimation**: compare the effort of a task to the effort of another task; values in relative units or sizes, not hours, days, weeks
- **T-shirt sizes**: pick an item of about medium workload = "medium", compare every other item to it ("if that was a medium, what size is this?")
	- Virtual Verde: adding bonsai trees to the catalog, creating a mobile app, launching a new logo (= the medium), creating the new account page
- **Story points** (Sue's favorite): pick an **anchor item**, estimate relative to it using the **Fibonacci sequence** (0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, ...; skip 0 and the first 1)
	- Numbers spread farther apart as they get higher → higher estimates carry more uncertainty and risk; one number combines **effort and risk** (no point debating 21 vs. 25, but 21 vs. 34 is a real conversation)
	- Fruit exercise (effort to completely eat a fruit: seeds, napkin, one bite, peeling, tools): mango = 5 (anchor) → orange 3, strawberry 1, banana 3, pineapple 13, cherry 2
	- Estimation exercises uncover ideas on how to get things done and surface the **riskiest** parts of the project
	- Virtual Verde: new user page = **8** (simple software update), bonsai trees = **13** (finding vendors, building a supply chain, ...) → story points show differences T-shirt sizes might hide
- When to refine: the team decides → dedicated meetings, continuously in conversations or emails, or as part of a scheduled event like **Sprint Planning**
#### Agile effort estimation techniques
- Effective relative effort estimation → successful, predictable Sprint outcomes; main steps are the same across techniques
- **Planning Poker™**: for a small number of items (**under 10**); **consensus-based**
	- Each person has a deck of **Fibonacci** cards (each number = sum of the last two: 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, ...); **?** card = doesn't understand or lacks information; **coffee cup** card = needs a break
	- Used in **Sprint Planning**: each member lays a card face down, all reveal at once, discuss especially far-apart estimates → hiding estimates first avoids bias from hearing numbers aloud
- **Dot Voting**: also for few Sprint Backlog items; dot stickers color-coded by effort (S = green, M = blue, L = orange, XL = red); items on paper around a table or on the wall; members walk around adding stickers
- **The Bucket System**: for Backlogs with many items, very quick (a couple hundred items in **one hour**)
	- A line of note cards ("buckets") marked with effort numbers; each person draws a random item card and places it on the line without discussion; can pass an item they don't understand; discuss misplaced items until consensus; no more than **120 seconds** per item
- **Large/Uncertain/Small**: quick rough estimation for Backlogs with several similar items; like the Bucket System but only three categories; place simpler, obvious stories first, then discuss complex ones
- **Ordering Method**: smaller team, many Backlog items; items randomly placed on a low-to-high scale; each member in turn moves one item one spot lower or higher, or passes, until no one wants to move items
- **Affinity Mapping**: for **more than 20** Backlog items; sticky notes on a wall/whiteboard/table; group items by similar theme, group, pattern (place one, discuss whether the next is similar → same group or new group); **3–10** groups total, each named, then prioritized by importance
- Characteristics of effective estimation:
	- **Avoids false precision**: rough relative estimates → more accuracy across the project (no lengthy debates over 7 vs. 10 days)
	- **Avoids anchoring bias**: private initial estimates (e.g. Planning Poker) → independent opinions, not compelled to match others to avoid embarrassment
	- **Promotes inclusivity**: builds trust and cohesiveness
	- **Leads to effort discovery**: uncovers strategies to get items done
- Agile effort estimation techniques: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/UEjul
#### T-shirt sizes and story points
- **Relative estimation** recap: compare the effort for one Backlog item to another → estimates more accurate relative to one another
- **T-shirt sizes** (XS, S, M, L, XL, XXL): quick, well understood, a good introduction for teams new to relative estimation; steps:
	1. Agree on the scale and metrics
	2. Identify at least one **anchor** Backlog item and assign it a size (some teams choose two: top and bottom of the range)
	3. Sort through remaining items and agree on sizes
- **Story points**: more advanced, good for experienced teams; usually the **Fibonacci sequence** (1 + 2 = 3, 2 + 3 = 5; numbers spread further apart) → more accuracy and specificity than T-shirt sizes; steps:
	1. Agree on permitted point values (some cap at e.g. **21**, some jump from 21 to **100**)
	2. Identify at least one anchor item and assign it a value
	3. Sort remaining items as a team, agree on estimates, capture them in the backlog management system
- Best practices: ask the Product Owner questions to have enough information; discuss divergent estimates; agree on and capture final values; discuss breaking down items in the larger size/points range
- Less experienced teams: start with T-shirt sizes; advanced teams can use either

### Scrum events
#### Introduction to the Sprint
- **Sprints** (iterations): provide the rhythm for the team; one of the five Scrum events; quicker feedback, collaboration, focus; amount of work planned based on the team's **historical capacity**
	- Each Sprint is like a **mini-project**: planning, execution, delivery, closing, and a retrospective
- Five Scrum events (Scrum Guide): the **Sprint**, **Sprint Planning**, **Daily Scrum**, **Sprint Review**, **Sprint Retrospective** → the other four revolve around the Sprint
- **Timeboxes** (recommended durations): create urgency that drives prioritization, a window of focus that improves productivity, a predictable rhythm
- Sprint timebox: **1–4 weeks**; three considerations:
	1. **Frequency of changes**: new requests each week → 1-week Sprints; stable needs → longer
	2. **Focus time** developers need to build a Backlog item: if most activities need at least a week → Sprints of at least **2 weeks**
	3. **Delivery overhead**: large stakeholder reviews or rigorous testing/QA taking several days → longer Sprints (**3–4 weeks**)
- No one-size-fits-all; change the length after a few Sprints if needed (Sue's team: 1-week Sprints for frequent change, but work often takes longer than a week → considering 2 weeks)
#### The Sprint: The Scrum Guide overview
- From the **2020 Scrum Guide**: "Sprints are the heartbeat of Scrum, where ideas are turned into value"
	- Fixed length events of **one month or less** for consistency; a new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends
	- All work to achieve the Product Goal (Sprint Planning, Daily Scrums, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) happens within Sprints
- During the Sprint: no changes that would endanger the **Sprint Goal**; quality does not decrease; the Product Backlog is refined as needed; scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is learned
- Sprints enable predictability through inspection and adaptation at least every calendar month; too long a horizon → Sprint Goal may become invalid, complexity and risk rise; shorter Sprints → more learning cycles, limit risk of cost and effort; each Sprint = a short project
- Forecasting practices: **burn-downs**, **burn-ups**, **cumulative flows** → useful but don't replace **empiricism** (only what has already happened can be used for forward-looking decisions)
- A Sprint can be **cancelled** if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete; only the **Product Owner** has the authority to cancel it
- The Sprint: The Scrum Guide overview: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/JUS5r
#### Sprint planning
- **Sprint Planning**: the first event within the Sprint; the entire Scrum Team confirms **capacity** (time and people) and identifies which Backlog items will be done → the **Sprint Backlog** and the **Sprint Goal**
- The Scrum Master facilitates and answers: Who is available (vacations, conflicts)? What has been our average **velocity** (points or items completed in a single Sprint in the past)? What can and should be accomplished? What is the Sprint Goal? How will the work get done? Who is responsible for what?
- **Definition of Done**: an agreed upon set of items that must be completed before a user story or Backlog item can be considered complete; e.g.:
	- Code or solution reviewed by an independent peer group
	- Passes all testing requirements (security, performance testing)
	- Documentation completed
	- All acceptance criteria specified by the PO are met
	- The PO accepts the user story
	- The team decides the list and improves it as needed
- **Sprint Backlog**: the set of Product Backlog items identified for completion during the upcoming Sprint (a subset of the Product Backlog)
	- E.g. Virtual Verde: 50 Backlog items, 4-week Sprints named by month (May Sprint, June Sprint, ...) → the top 5 items fit May's capacity and effort sizes
- **Sprint Goal**: the overarching objective the team aims to achieve → the **why** of the Sprint, a big-picture view of the Sprint Backlog; keeps the team on a broader objective instead of separate work streams
	- May Sprint Backlog: users can purchase bonsai trees; access an online discussion forum on home office decor; vendor management can add vendor audit results; users can use a coupon for home office accessories; customer support can connect products to support tickets → Sprint Goal: "provide a comprehensive experience to the user who wishes to install a bonsai tree in their home office" (e.g. coupon for bonsai, vendors audited for bonsai quality)

#### The Daily Scrum and Sprint Review
- Agile principle: face-to-face conversation is the most efficient and effective way to convey information → two face-to-face events
- **Daily Scrum** (**stand-up**): the Scrum Team synchronizes and prioritizes the day's activities; **15 minutes**, same time and place every day; each member answers:
	- What did I do yesterday that helped the Development Team meet the Sprint Goal?
	- What will I do today to help the Development Team meet the Sprint Goal?
	- Do I notice any **impediment** that prevents me or the team from meeting our goals?
	- → lets the Scrum Master quickly unblock the team; reinforces focus on the Sprint Backlog and Sprint Goal
	- Scrum Guide: every day; Sue's team (1-week Sprints) holds stand-ups two days a week → find what works for your team
- **Sprint Review**: at the close of a Sprint; a meeting with the entire Scrum Team where the product is **demonstrated** to determine which aspects are finished and which aren't → pillars of **inspection and adaptation**; values of openness, courage, respect
	- Development Team and Product Owner lead the inspection; explore which Product Backlog items are done
	- Fun and uplifting; timeboxed, **no more than 4 hours**; often the source of great product ideas
	- Example (August Sprint Review): the team's marketing specialist demos a **launch email** to existing **Plant Pals** corporate customers → live feedback (love the opening line, bigger opening image, easier to forward, shorter text)
	- Benefits of group inspection: immediate feedback; everyone has a voice → shared ownership; team learns how teammates work → trust
- **Product Increment**: what's produced after a given Sprint, considered **releasable**; releasable when the team has developed a **Minimum Viable Product** (**MVP**: a version of a product with just enough features to satisfy early customers)
	- Only items meeting the **Definition of Done** are part of the Increment; anything not done goes back to the Product Backlog
#### Sarah: The benefits of a Daily Standup
- Sarah: program manager on Google's Engineering Education Team
- A daily or weekly **Standup** with the cross-functional team centralizes and organizes work, beyond documentation: even separate roles (data analyst vs. marketer) benefit → visibility, **transparency**, camaraderie and teamwork that documentation alone doesn't create
- Keep it short (not a retrospective or town hall): Sarah schedules **15–20 minutes**; quickly share what you're working on and any **blockers** → set up separate time with the person or workstream owner to unstick them
- Across time zones and locations: send a gut check or survey for the time (e.g. 11:00 am ET vs. 4:00 pm ET) → reach consensus, then stick with it
- With a daily Standup on a tight-deadline project, avoid making attendance mandatory for everyone every day
- Stay in the timebox: create an agenda with minutes allocated per update; don't cut people off mid-sentence → at a natural break, "put a pin in that" and come back later or in the next meeting
#### Releasable Increment versus Minimum Viable Product
- Scrum Guide: "An **Increment** is a concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal. Each Increment is additive to all prior Increments and thoroughly verified... In order to provide value, the Increment must be usable"
- **Potentially releasable (shippable) Product Increment**: the goal of every Sprint = a completed, tested, ready-to-ship addition to the product ("potentially" → it may not actually ship)
	- Pet adoption app example: features = presenting available pets, rating potential matches, contacting the adoption center → none worth shipping alone, but completing **one feature in its entirety** in a Sprint beats bits of all three
	- Enables early feedback, high quality, ability to respond to change → always aim for a potentially releasable increment as the Sprint goal
- **Definition of Done**: a formal description of the state of the potentially releasable Increment when it meets the required quality measures; the team's agreed-upon requirements for any item considered "done" (software: completed, reviewed, passed testing; non-software: a document with legal review and approval, a formalized closeout report) → an explicit, shared understanding
- The **Product Owner** decides whether a solution is releasable: Is the increment complete? Will it bring value, meet quality measures, well-tested? Is it usable by the end user, and can their feedback improve future versions?
- **Minimum viable product** (**MVP**): term coined by **Eric Ries**: "that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort" → quicker user feedback than a full-featured product
	- Examples: a landing page; a "buy now" button that only registers clicks
	- An MVP may take several Sprints; every Sprint produces an increment
	- Pet adoption: the PO decides the MVP = the three features **for cats only** → release, collect feedback useful for all pet types later
- A releasable increment **can** be an MVP but doesn't have to be; the PO uses increments and business insights to decide what makes a valuable, viable release
- Releasable Increment versus Minimum Viable Product: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/6DQgb
#### The Sprint Retrospective
- **Sprint Retrospective**: the last of the five Scrum events; an essential meeting of **up to 3 hours** for the Scrum Team to step back, reflect, and identify improvements in how to work together (→ [Course 4: retrospectives](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
	- Reflect on what's working or not regarding **people, processes, tools**; which improvements to explore next Sprint; were last Sprint's improvements helpful, and why? (→ [Capstone: Sprint retrospectives](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
- Keys to success:
	- **Respect and blamelessness**: create a safe space for candor; acknowledge awkwardness; allow anonymous or private feedback if needed
	- **Participation**: input must matter; prompt ideas with "What is one thing we could try in the next Sprint?", "What slowed us down?", "What happened that we didn't expect?" (Sue's team found **dependencies on external stakeholders** slowed them → new communication channels to raise awareness of priorities)
	- **Balance negative with positive**: "Where did we notice success?" → recreate successful outcomes
	- **Act on it**: otherwise teams get discouraged; search for improvements or turn what worked into habits and norms
#### Sprint Retrospectives: Pitfalls and best practices
- **Retrospectives**: workshops or meetings that give teams time to reflect on a project and brainstorm future improvements; in Scrum, at the end of each Sprint (every 1–4 weeks) → much more often than in traditional PM; a critical moment to inspect and adapt
- Pitfalls:
	- **Too many gimmicks**: games and exercises not every team enjoys → use occasionally or when the team asks
	- **Only focusing on the negative**: also highlight where the team exceeded expectations → avoid failures and repeat successes
	- **Changing processes after each retrospective**: keep a new process for a few Sprints before judging it; note opportunities and wait before implementing
- Best practices:
	- **Ask open-ended, probing questions**: "How could we have better achieved our Sprint Goal?" instead of "Did we achieve the Sprint Goal?"
	- **Consider diverse communication and participation styles**: start with silent reflection (journaling) or pairs before the group conversation
	- **Cover many aspects of the Sprint**: productivity and efficiency, scope and understanding of the Definition of Done, communication within the team, stakeholder communication, progress toward long-range release plans
	- **Reflect periodically on Scrum theory and values**: "How could the team become more transparent?", "How did we abide by our Scrum values in this Sprint?"
- Sprint Retrospectives: Pitfalls and best practices: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/HjYXh
### Scrum tools
#### Velocity and burndown charts
- **Burndown chart**: measures time against the amount of work done and the amount of work remaining → keeps the team aware of how they're doing against goals; in Scrum, reflects completion of user stories during the Sprint; the Scrum Master reviews it (sometimes daily) (→ [Course 4: choosing a tracking method](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
	- Using **T-shirt sizes**? Map sizes to numbers (e.g. XS = 1, S = 2, M = 5, L = 8, ...) for burndown and velocity calculations
	- Virtual Verde July Sprint: 4 weeks = **20 business days**, Sprint Backlog = **200 points** → even burn = **10 points/day**
		- Day 5: 22 points (25% of Sprint) → on track
		- Day 10: 45 points vs. ~100 expected → running late → don't panic; the Scrum Master should already be helping unblock the team
		- Day 15: 140 points → back on track; Day 20: 188 points → discuss in the retrospective
- **Velocity**: a measure of how many points a team burns down during a single Sprint on average
	- Sprint Planning uses average velocity over **at least three Sprints** to decide how many items fit in the Sprint Backlog
	- No good or bad velocity: it's what the team has historically achieved in a timebox; each team calibrates its own points → **can't compare teams** (Sue's 3-person team: 70 points per 1-week Sprint; a previous 5-person team: 120 points per 2-week Sprint → just different)
- Stable velocity + refined, estimated Backlog → predict (1) approximately how long the entire Product Backlog will take and (2) how much will be done by a given time
	- E.g. average **200 points/month** for three months, **1,500 points** left → ~7–8 Sprints; in July, what's done by January 1 (5 months) → draw a line at **1,000 points** down the Backlog
	- To pull in dates: add people to increase velocity, rearrange priorities, other project decisions
- Reaching a stable velocity can take multiple Sprints, as the team gets used to estimating and working together
#### Interpreting velocity: Dos and Don'ts
- **Velocity**: "the measure of how many points the team burns down in a given Sprint on average"; **story points** = a unit expressing the estimated effort to implement a Backlog item, calibrated by the team (T-shirt sizes mapped to numbers)
- First Sprint: no historical basis → rough guess; after a few Sprints → a good measure used to fill the Sprint Backlog (requires **backlog refinement**)
- Using velocity for good (cautions from Agile experts):
	- **Sharing outside the team**: stakeholders may lack context → add supporting materials (velocity with a date range and a trend visualization; dips and spikes give insights)
	- **As a performance metric**: pressure and intimidation harm the team's culture
	- **Comparing teams**: story point weights are subjective (5 points for one team ≈ 3 for another) → unfair; velocity is **not a measure of value**
	- **Projecting delivery dates** over many Sprints: becomes a pressure point, creates false expectations and harsh competition; teams need several Sprints to know their capacity; too many dates in advance can't account for changes → don't treat estimated dates as **commitments**
- Interpreting velocity: Dos and Don'ts: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/zKGv1
#### Utilizing Kanban boards
- **Kanban board** (some teams say **Scrum board**: same basic tool; the Scrum Guide doesn't define a Scrum board, but some tools add Scrum-specific features to a Kanban board) (→ [Course 3: Kanban boards](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- Three main features for Sprint tracking:
	- **Visualization**: everything at a glance; point at work items, use colors and sizes, notice where the team's challenges are → find improvements
	- **WIP limits**: constraints on how many work items the team actively works on at any time → **focus** (a Scrum value); "no such thing as multitasking" → more work, less efficiency; each team sets its own limit, and the board shows when it's exceeded
	- **Flow of work**: physical Post-it notes or virtual boards → experience work moving through **to do → doing → done** (often during the Daily Scrum, but anytime)
- Example: **Leo** (plant vendor) finishes finalizing contracts with the three top plant vendors for the launch → moves the item from doing to done, picks the next item or helps a teammate
#### Tools for transparency and collaboration
- **Transparency** (a Scrum pillar) depends on tools that make everyone aware of progress; store the Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, documentation in one centralized place
- **Scheduling and work management tools**:
	- Traditional PM: **Microsoft Project** (powerful schedule and resource management)
	- Scrum: the most critical tool manages the **Backlog and Sprints** → **Jira** (Atlassian): customizable, supports team and backlog management, Sprint definitions, velocity, burndown charts; similar purchasable tools; some teams build Agile tooling in spreadsheets; most offer free trials
	- **Trello**: simple Kanban (Sue uses it for personal projects: planning a move, a birthday party)
	- **Asana**: Sprint Planning and Backlog management; assign tasks, automate workflows, track progress, communicate with stakeholders
- **Documentation, collaboration, productivity tools**: word processing (e.g. **Google Docs**, documentation + collaboration in one), spreadsheets (Excel, Google Sheets) for Backlogs, presentations (Google Slides or Microsoft equivalent)
- **Collaboration and communication** (Agile values individuals and interactions): video conferencing, team and one-on-one chats, email → teammates unblock themselves before the next Daily Scrum
- In Scrum, the team decides together which tools to use

## Module 4: Applying Agile in the organization
### Understanding value-driven delivery
#### Introduction: Applying Agile in the organization
- A wider view of Scrum and Agile: applying Agile thinking to job interviews and work scenarios, delivering value and achieving business goals, responding to risks and team dynamics, Agile within the broader project management discipline, using Agile in your next position
#### Maximizing value-driven delivery
- **Value**: what the end product provides to the user (financial benefits, user growth and engagement, compliance adherence); means different things to each customer
- The number one Agile principle: satisfy the customer by delivering valuable software (or product/solution) → delivering value quickly and efficiently is why Agile exists
- **Value-driven delivery**: the team focuses on delivering a product of high value (delivering a product ≠ delivering value; pre-Agile teams focused on process and evaluated usefulness only at the end)
- Three parts ("build" and "run" can be replaced by "create", "produce", "deliver"):
	- **Build the right thing**: understand what customers really want → go beyond "a website to promote their plant service" to their goals (brand recognition? more customers?) → solution-oriented conversation; "individuals and interactions" includes customers and users
	- **Build the thing right**: build only the requested or approved features → unnecessary features add complexity without value, delay or reduce value, increase risk of bugs
	- **Run it right**: think through how users interact with the product after delivery: How do users get support? How does it add value long after delivery? How do new features reach existing users?
- Virtual Verde:
	- Right thing: survey current and potential customers on plant preferences and home office design → update user stories in the Product Backlog
	- Thing right: secure a trusted plant vendor; work with designers on pots, vases, accessories; have marketing feature the desired plants and designs on the website and catalog
	- Run it right: follow-up customer satisfaction surveys (offerings, delivery times, plant quality) → evaluate vendors, offerings, marketing; add watering cans, automatic plant health systems, free monthly gardening tips
#### Case study: Forming a value-driven task force
- **Case studies**: in-depth, data-driven analyses of a business, community, or organization → industry insights, learn from competitors' successes and failures; focus on the problem, its effect, the pros and cons of solutions
- Questions to ask: What is the issue? What is the goal of the analysis? What is the context? What key facts should be considered? What alternatives does the decision-maker have? What would you recommend, and why?
- Case: **Penta**, a construction software company, created Scrum-team-based problem solving by forming an **Agile task force** (people from across the organization ideate, test, analyze) → *A Six-Month Cultural Transformation: The Penta Story*
- Case study: Forming a value-driven task force: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/zCerS
#### Camron: How Agile can drive value
- Camron: technical program manager (a program manager with a technical background who can engage in technical conversations and decisions)
- Favorite thing about Agile: **flexibility** → most decisions are made at the beginning, when you know the least; Agile embraces change and builds in opportunities to make changes
- **Incremental delivery**: deliver little bits over a long period vs. Waterfall's everything at the end (waiting days, months, years before extracting value) → Waterfall makes sense for a house or a car (can't use parts of a car; safety requirements)
- For hard design issues, don't sacrifice the whole project: if 90% is built and shippable, ship it → users get value while you figure out the last 10%
- **Change is never free**: plans communicated, estimates made, work in progress → is the change within your threshold or budget? "It's not always about it being perfect. It's about it being good"
#### Components of a value roadmap
- **Value roadmap**: an Agile way of mapping out timelines and requirements for product development, usable in all types of businesses; a guide showing where to go, how to get there, and what to accomplish along the way to maximize value → gather customer and stakeholder input and apply it to each iteration; explains the vision, identifies milestones
- Three components:
	- **Product vision**: based on user interviews and market analysis; the team's **north star**; defines what the product is, how it supports the customer's business strategy, and who will use it
	- **Product roadmap**: created and maintained by the **Product Owner**; a high-level view of the expected product, its requirements, and an estimated schedule for milestones → building the right thing
	- **Release plans**: developed by the PO and project manager; a **release** happens when the team has a basic working version of a feature or requirement; several releases over a project → only the **first release date** is set in stone, the rest are early estimates subject to change
		- A release plan contains: a **release goal** (an overall business goal for the release's features), the list of Backlog items (epics, user stories, features), an estimated release date, other relevant dates (a convention, a major holiday)
- A value roadmap only works if the team is collaborative and all stakeholders work together regularly
#### Product roadmaps: Benefits, pitfalls, and best practices
- Types of roadmaps: project, product, value, Lean, Agile roadmaps; often visual, ideally on one page to show the big picture
- Benefits: clarify the sequence of deliverables; show teams how their efforts relate to the **north-star vision**; show stakeholders **incremental value** over the project; help stakeholders understand the layout of the work
- Pitfalls:
	- Letting stakeholders think the roadmap is **set and unchangeable** → impedes adaptation, pressure to hit deadlines no matter what
	- Spending too much time fine-tuning delivery dates instead of keeping them rough and refining as they approach
	- Putting all the work into the roadmap rather than producing deliverables
- Best practices: make it highly noticeable and refer to it frequently; indicate the highest **priority** items and, if possible, the highest **value** items; make it visible to the wider stakeholder group for their planning; review it regularly with sponsors, stakeholders, and the team (→ [Course 4: roadmaps as a tracking method](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- Further reading: *Product Roadmap First Principles*
- Key takeaway: a shared roadmap connects Sprint work with the broader vision → motivation through rough patches, sense of accomplishment
- Product roadmaps: Benefits, pitfalls, and best practices: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/M2MId
#### Creating an effective value roadmap
- A value roadmap uses the basic Scrum events to define product goals and requirements → specific routes to deliver the most value
- Tips:
	- **Product roadmap**: product release dates should be only **rough estimates** (months to years away, things change) → too specific a roadmap sets the team up for failure
	- **Release plans**: the Product Owner and project manager/Scrum Master develop them together → connect the product roadmap with the team's **capacity and velocity** (the team's ability to complete work at a certain pace); otherwise unrealistic and an unsustainable pace (violates the sustainable development principle)
	- **Hard dates** (can't change): factor them into affected release plans (e.g. Office Green's **office décor convention in October** → launch the first phase of Virtual Verde there); communicate with stakeholders for a clear understanding of **must-have features** → focus on them if the deadline is at risk
	- A release plan is a **living artifact** (doesn't contradict "responding to change over following a plan"); common reasons to change it:
		- A change in **team velocity** (adding or losing members, efficiency gains)
		- A change to the **product scope** (approved by the PO)
		- Better understanding of the **effort** needed for features (a user story or epic is harder or easier than thought)
	- The Scrum Master/PM reviews the release plan **before each Sprint Planning** to check the team is on track → if off track, an open conversation with the PO and business people (**transparency**)
#### Responding to change over following a plan
- Changing a release plan in three stages:
- **Step 1: Identifying a needed change** using the **triple constraint** (→ [Course 2: triple constraint](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/)):
	- **Scope** ("what"): contents of the product roadmap, Product Backlog items, intended deliverables, intended users or customers
	- **Time** ("when"): product roadmap timeline, release schedule, Sprint duration
	- **Costs or resources** ("how"): makeup of the Development Team, PMs, POs, other business people, and equipment
	- Any stakeholder can identify a change; e.g. customer feedback on early prototypes → new/deleted features (scope); a Sprint Retrospective finds understaffing (cost/resource); shifted dependencies or dates → roadmap change (time)
- **Step 2: Deciding to make the change** (basic steps of most decision-making models):
	1. Identify the "**decider**": a single person (usually the Product Owner or a senior stakeholder) for consistency and accountability
	2. Develop and share the important factors, gather supporting data
	3. Openly discuss benefits and costs; identify uncertainty, capture assumptions
	4. Document the decision
- **Step 3: Implementing the change**:
	- **Document** the change and decision-making process (meeting notes, pros/cons lists, assumptions, data)
	- **Capture the change in affected artifacts** (roadmaps, Product Backlogs, staffing plans, integration dates) with a reference to its source; revision labels like "version 1.2" or "updated on December 20th"
	- **Share** with all affected stakeholders (meetings, documentation, email announcements)
	- **Monitor** the change for a certain time
	- If not approved: still document the information and logic; consider putting the change **on hold** pending more information
- Responding to change over following a plan: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/WcLGw
### Leading through change and challenges
#### Facilitating organizational change
- Organizations you apply to may already be Agile, be switching, or be ready to transition; entry-level PMs usually support (not lead) the change in large organizations, but a smaller organization may want you to lead it
- Review (→ [Course 1: organizational culture and change management](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/)):
	- **Organizational culture**: based on shared workplace values; shows in behaviors, activities, communication, how people work together → a change out of sync with culture is much harder; research: companies that ignore the cultural aspects of Agile are more likely to fail
	- **Change management**: the process of getting folks to adopt a new product, process, or (for Agile) a new **value system**
- Cultural change takes time, sometimes years; implementing a few changes still adds value → "**Change takes patient persistence**"; small changes add up
- Bringing Agile or Scrum to a new team:
	- Create a **sense of ownership**: find an **executive sponsor** who feels ownership of the change; connect changes to the company's stated mission or values → buy-in from the top, support, resources
	- Create a **sense of urgency**: ask the team, organization, stakeholders what's working and what's not, and tie changes to those opportunities:
		- What is preventing us from providing the best possible product to our customers?
		- What is allowing our competitors to outperform us in this market?
		- How can we help our teams become more productive and supported in their work?
		- → reuse these questions to collect feedback and demonstrate **incremental improvements** (the true spirit of Agile)
- Virtual Verde: the Office Green CEO wanted to capture the home office trend → urgency: home office decorating was a hot online trend; the team from the Plant Pals project was motivated to try a new approach
- Sue's example: transforming a global Google team of about **200 developers** into an Agile organization took about **two years** (tools, processes, coaching across work sites)
#### The influencer change framework
- From *INFLUENCER: The New Science of Leading Change* (Joseph Grenny, Kerry Patterson, David Maxfield, Ron McMillan, Al Switzler)
- **Influencer**: someone able to lead and influence others to change their behaviors, hearts, and minds for meaningful, sustainable results; **influence ≠ persuasion** (persuasion is short-term, influence is lasting; requires trust, authority, confidence in your decisions) → difference between temporary behavior change and deep change in culture and values
- Three keys to influence:
	- **Clarify measurable results**: know what you want, why, and when; **SMART** (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound); keep measures visible and transparent to the team
	- **Find vital behaviors**: a **vital behavior** = the action an individual takes at a pivotal moment in the context of the change they seek
		- E.g. to increase Product Owner involvement, a developer who just mocked up a feature emails the PO for review and feedback instead of moving on
		- Find them by consulting experts, scanning the best-cited articles and research, or a culture assessment (norms and customs); note those who succeed where most fail
	- **Use the six sources of influence** (correlated with successful change; use all, prioritize by audience, e.g. financial incentives vs. social justice impacts), with the PO involvement example:
		- **Personal motivation**: are they internally motivated? "love what they hate" → the PO gives timely, appreciative, effective feedback
		- **Personal ability**: do they have the ability, knowledge, skills to "do what they can't"? → the developer knows how to use demo tools and send a quick video
		- **Social motivation**: do social contacts encourage or discourage the behavior? → team members remind each other in the Daily Scrum to email the PO
		- **Social ability**: are there resources in their social network? → a tool to track all demos to the PO during the Sprint
		- **Structural motivation**: rewards or incentives? → a coffee gift card Sprint award given by the PO
		- **Structural ability**: environmental factors? make the incorrect behavior harder than the correct one → the content management system pre-populates the PO in the reviewer list
- Further reading: the book and *The Influencer Change Framework–The Power to Change Anything*
- The influencer change framework: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/DLIDF
#### Coaching an Agile team
- The PM/Scrum Master is the designated **Agile coach**: help the team recognize areas for improvement and implement solutions; three steps (like a sports coach):
	1. **Design the "plays" with the team**: the Scrum Master owns the playbook but creates it with the whole team (how the team runs a Sprint Review, works day-to-day, publishes plans to stakeholders); involve the team in updates (e.g. Sue's brainstorm with sticky notes on what wasn't working, then prioritized improvements)
	2. **Provide feedback**: as early as possible, day-to-day (like directions from the sidelines); also a **big-picture view** (like reviewing game video for patterns); not only fixing broken things but encouraging what works well
	3. **Celebrate and learn**: congratulate often (a job well done, a happy customer, a big launch); treat a "loss" as critical data and a learning opportunity → Thomas Edison: "I have not failed—I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work"
#### Coaching versus managing in Agile
- The difference is in **communication**: management = **giving direction** (one-way); coaching = **teaching** (two-way)
- **Managing**: overseeing the work of others → onboarding and orienting new employees, conducting meetings, delegating tasks, monitoring progress and performance, making high-level decisions
	- Agile teams are **self-managing** (autonomy to choose how to accomplish work, empowered to problem-solve)
	- Still needed for decisive action: an emergency, being behind on a deadline, a client with specific needs you know best, a results-driven project with little room for error
- **Coaching**: a two-way communication style aimed at influencing and developing team members' skills, motivation, and judgment → teaches critical thinking and decision-making; offer guidance, then get out of the way; ask questions so members reach conclusions themselves; trust → higher satisfaction and quality
	- Use when a member already has experience on similar projects and is growing new competencies or trying a new approach
	- Principles: **Motivate** (point out the value of their work, instill pride), **Support** (be an accessible resource), **Encourage and appreciate** (validate heavy workloads, assure them they can handle challenges)
	- Helpful when: branching into a new technology or discipline; behavior with unintended, not readily visible effects on team dynamics; recovering from a setback
- Scenario: a Scrum Team repeatedly fails to meet customer needs, reworking the same three features, showing **burnout** → coaching working session:
	- Motivation: brainstorm positive reasons for the customer's feedback and why an excellent end product matters
	- Support: ideas to streamline customer feedback (e.g. a **design Sprint** with the customer attending)
	- Encourage and appreciate: a fun, inclusive event celebrating work accomplished
- In Agile and Scrum, **coaching is usually the best initial option** (builds capabilities → more agility); choose by asking: What is the desired outcome? What is the team member's skill level? What does the situation need now?
- Further reading: Scrum.org blog post on coaching with Scrum values
- Coaching versus managing in Agile: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/RT1G1
#### Agile team challenges
- The PM/Scrum Master anticipates common challenges; organized by three of the four themes of the Agile principles (value delivery, business collaboration, team dynamics and culture; the fourth is retrospectives)
- **Value delivery** issues (delivering working solutions frequently):
	- Signs: missing delivery dates, tasks taking much longer; **burnout** (long hours, exhaustion); too many items in progress, nothing getting to done
	- Remedies: **more demos** (big-picture view of the working product vs. the value roadmap); ask in **retrospectives** what slows the team (dependencies, communication); review what "**done**" means; focus on **only a few user stories per Sprint** → better to deliver fewer items in one Sprint than many items over more Sprints
- **Business collaboration** issues (developers and business people building the right product together):
	- Signs: team overwhelmed by critical feedback or change requests after reviews; avoiding feedback, complaining about PO/business changes; an "**us versus them**" mentality (e.g. "Don't give a demo to the salesperson... they'll just point out what's wrong")
	- Remedies: **more demos** (steady feedback, shared understanding of done); a **Solution Design Sprint** (an entire Sprint working solely on the solution design, with the team and business people sitting together); introduce Backlog changes **only between Sprints**
	- Example: an engineering director kept asking engineers for quick **dashboards** (a web page that shows data) → disrupted focus and velocity → asked to go straight to the Scrum Master so requests could be planned
- **Team dynamics and culture** issues:
	- Signs: **low morale** (grumpy, irritated); **lots of conflict** left unresolved, resentment and grudges; also **low conflict** → people may not feel safe to disagree (openness and courage are Scrum values)
	- Remedies:
		- A team brainstorm on working better together (e.g. share stories of the worst and best teams ever → list of do's and don'ts)
		- Change up workflows: pair people on a hard task, change how a regular meeting runs
		- Take a training class or watch a team dynamics video together and discuss
		- Try a retrospective technique, e.g. **Six Hats Thinking Technique**: each member chooses a different hat with a different objective (positives, negatives, emotive statements, ...) → a well-rounded retrospective
#### Common Agile coaching challenges
- **Managing a stable product roadmap**: change is a core Agile value, but too much change → an unstable roadmap; two main causes:
	- **Product ambition**: product leadership overly ambitious about what the team can deliver; the PO wants to please stakeholders and over-promises (e.g. the Office Green CEO asks to launch Virtual Verde in **Asia in four months** and the PO says "sure" before consulting the team)
		- Remedies: agree upfront how to handle new opportunities (when reviewed and estimated, how commitments are made); regular roadmap reviews with the whole team, **at least quarterly**; promote knowledge sharing between the PO and Development Team
	- **Product assumptions**: too many assumptions under uncertainty jeopardize success (Virtual Verde: which plants sell best, stay healthy in varied climates, which vendors)
		- Remedies: **document assumptions** and make them transparent → agree they're safe or double-check them with **unbiased user research** (gathers information about what users really want: surveys, focus groups, other objective data)
- **Incomplete implementation of Scrum**: practices only partially implemented or without proper support and coaching → roles, artifacts, activities are designed to work as a set
	- Problems: loss of clear roles (e.g. a developer also acting as Scrum Master lacks bandwidth for both); skipping or blending events (Sprint Review, Retrospective, Planning) → less transparency, inspection, adaptation; no Scrum coaching
	- Solution: **implement Scrum completely**; reinforce connections between activities and Scrum/Agile values (e.g. stand-ups exist to gain feedback, unblock work, ask for help, stay focused on Sprint goals); define and fill roles clearly (PO builds the right thing, Development Team builds it right, Scrum Master builds it fast)
- **Lack of team stability**: people leaving and joining frequently → unpredictability, disrupted flow
	- Remedies: a quick **onboarding** process; **pair programming** style (new member learns on the job with a colleague; a partner can pick up work if someone leaves); **shorter Sprints** if members keep leaving (wrap up their last Sprint before leaving)
- A huge community of Agilists is happy to help; even experienced Agilists ask for help

#### The evolution of Agile
- Agile's popularity has grown fast since 2001: one industry report → **85%** of organizations adopted a **product-centric model** (associated with Agile); State of Agile report → **30%** of those apply a **hybrid** of methodologies → blending methods is a useful skill
- Driver: a **VUCA** world; the Manifesto (mindset) hasn't changed much, but its frameworks keep evolving
- **DevOps**: combines software **development** and IT **operations**; Google Cloud Platform: "an organizational and cultural movement that aims to increase software delivery velocity, improve service reliability, and build shared ownership among software stakeholders"
	- Like all Agile frameworks: shorten the product life cycle, deliver continuously with very high quality
	- Emerged from the challenge of running software reliably for billions of people 24/7 → launching fast and reliably to a global market = significant **competitive advantage**; building and evolving secure, reliable large-scale systems at a rapid pace
- **Business agility**: incorporating Agile principles into the wide sphere of management to thrive in high-VUCA environments → rethinking financial planning, governance and reporting structures, hiring and HR practices; larger organizations may use **Scrum of Scrums** or the **Scaled Agile Framework** (**SAFe**)
- Beyond tech: Agile training for the **Google sales team in Latin America** (reduce risk at all stages of the sales cycle via early feedback, frequent discussions); **construction** (PMI article: translating the Manifesto into construction terms, e.g. "silos are minimized and close cooperation is encouraged" to deal with delays and budget overruns)

#### Scaling Agile
- For teams larger than 9 or products needing multiple teams → five scaling frameworks:
- **Scaled Agile Framework** (**SAFe**): the most popular; a Lean-Agile scaling framework drawing on Kanban, Scrum, XP, DevOps, Design Thinking; first principle: "**take an economic view**" (value above all); organizes work and teams into **Agile Release Trains** based on value streams (e.g. sales); mature, detailed guidance (check back to the Manifesto to preserve agility)
	- Core values: **Alignment** (synchronize planning and execution at all levels), **Built-in Quality**, **Transparency** (visible execution to build trust), **Program Execution** (working systems and business outcomes), **Leadership** (model SAFe's values and principles)
- **Scrum of Scrums**: integrates the work of multiple smaller Scrum teams on the same project
	- At least **12** people divided into Scrum Teams of **5–10**
	- Scrum of Scrums meetings (weekly, twice a week, or daily), Daily Scrum format focused on the team: What did the team do yesterday? What problems are affecting your team? What does your team want to accomplish before we meet again? Is your team blocked?
	- A Scrum Master or "**ambassador**" per team + a **Scrum of Scrums Master** for the overall process; Sprint Planning, Review, Retrospective
	- No official framework beyond this; assumes teams understand Scrum well and design their own coordination
- **Large-Scale Scrum** (**LeSS**): maximize value and reduce waste in larger organizations; grew out of **600+** experiments; ten principles: Large-scale Scrum is Scrum; empirical process control; transparency; more with less; whole-product focus; customer-centric; continuous improvement towards perfection; systems thinking; Lean thinking; queuing theory ("flow", manage queue size, minimize multitasking)
	- **Basic LeSS** (up to ~50 people) and **LeSS Huge** (50–6,000+ people); less.works
- **Disciplined Agile Delivery** (**DAD**): a hybrid combining Kanban, LeSS, Lean Development, XP, Agile Modeling, ...; guides process decisions SAFe and Scrum of Scrums leave open; strategy based on context and desired outcomes; four layers:
	- **Foundations**: principles, guidelines, concepts, roles, team structures, **Way of Working** (**WoW**)
	- **Disciplined DevOps**: deliver effectively and safely, data and security management first
	- **Value Streams**: align with business strategy (customers, sales, portfolio management)
	- **Disciplined Agile Enterprise** (**DAE**): connects the marketplace with corporate governance and enterprise activities
- **Spotify Model**: not a true Agile framework (no standard guide; describes how Spotify scaled through culture, autonomy, communication, accountability, quality) (→ [The Spotify model](#the-spotify-model))
	- **Squads**: autonomous teams of **6–12** with a coach (like a Scrum Master) and a PO; **Tribes**: Squads in the same feature area, **40–150** people, with a Tribe Lead; **Chapters**: specialists aligned across the organization (e.g. JavaScript developers) setting best practices; **Guilds**: communities around shared interests
	- Its value is not the names but how practices served Spotify's culture of trust, transparency, autonomy; video by Henrik Kniberg
- Best practices: treat scaling models as general frameworks, not instruction manuals; mix and match while applying the Manifesto; don't scale without prior Agile experience (Waterfall → scaled Agile is risky without a guide); **don't scale if it isn't necessary** (larger team → more complexity)
- Scaling Agile: https://www.coursera.org/learn/agile-project-management/supplement/Pe7s8

#### Agile project management opportunities
- Job titles: **Agile Project Manager**, **Scrum Master**, **IT Agile Project Manager**, **DevOps Project Manager**; look for a role that suits your experience, complements your industry domain expertise, offers growth, has a culture that fits, and an employer who supports your goals
- Sue's interview questions as a Google hiring manager:
	- "What's the difference between Agile and Waterfall project management?" → does the candidate know Agile is more than Scrum, Sprints, Standups (founding values: customer collaboration, value delivery, self-organizing teams)? Do they avoid making Waterfall the worst solution (all projects benefit from clear requirements, risk management, stakeholder awareness)?
	- "How do you know when to use an Agile approach or framework on your project?" → understanding of which challenges Agile/Scrum help with
	- "If you are facing resistance with your team following a Scrum or Agile practice, how do you convince them to give it a try?" → communication and influence skills, belief in self-organizing teams (Google teams resist being told what to do; it can diminish innovation)
- Questions to ask interviewers (culture is crucial): How supportive is management toward blending project management approaches? What's the first thing I should know about the culture here? How often will I hear about the needs of our users or customers? What would a typical day look like in this position?
- Bringing Agile to your current team:
	1. **Start small**: bite-sized pieces (a Kanban board for one workstream, a retrospective after a major milestone)
	2. **Listen to feedback**: meet the team where they are, include their ideas → small changes amplify into big results
	3. **Be strategic**: target today's biggest challenges (unpredictable estimates and crunch mode → relative estimation; too many voices on the product → a single **Product Owner**)
	4. **Find allies**: Agile allies in your organization or network (Google has a network of about **60 volunteer Agile coaches**)
