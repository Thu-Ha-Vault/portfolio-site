### Creating a project charter
#### Project charters: Purpose and components
- This set of videos: apply **strategic thinking** and effective business writing to initiate a project → analyze project documents and supporting materials to identify requirements, evaluate stakeholders, problem solve; use a project charter to align scope and goals; negotiate with stakeholders to prioritize goals
	- **Strategic thinking**: analyzing documentation and talking with stakeholders to inform decisions based on the information available to you
- **Project charter**: a formal document that clearly defines the project and outlines the necessary details to reach the project's goals (→ [Course 2: Project charters](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
	- Created by the project manager during the **initiation phase** (first phase of the project life cycle)
	- Organizes vital project information, creates a framework for the work, communicates details to the necessary people; a reference throughout the life cycle to help stakeholders realign on scope, goals, and costs
- Main components:
	- **Summary**: overview of the project and the goals you hope to accomplish; brief (a few sentences at most), to the point
	- **Project goals**: the desired results of the project; the overall result stakeholders aim to achieve, determined by input from stakeholders and the project manager
	- **Deliverables**: specific tasks and tangible outcomes that enable the team to meet project goals
		- E.g. launching the tablet rollout is part of Sauce & Spoon's annual growth and expansion goals; one deliverable = successful installation of working tablets at two restaurant locations
	- **Scope**: the boundaries of a project; the charter also lists what's **out-of-scope** (details that don't contribute to the project's goals)
	- Other possible sections: budget and cost, success metrics
- Activity: document the project name, summary, goals, and deliverables of the Sauce & Spoon tablet pilot in a project charter
#### Project charters: Stakeholder alignment
- Any time you communicate project details, think about your **audience** and what information is important to them → for a project charter, the audience = all key stakeholders
	- Questions: Who are your stakeholders? What details are most important to them? Are there project details they might disagree with?
	- Then review the charter to ensure those details are included → present it and confirm everyone agrees
- The project charter is also an **alignment tool**; **alignment** = reaching agreement between two or more parties
	- A common cause of project failure: **misalignment** among stakeholders about project details (or between you and stakeholders with differing visions) → align before the work begins
	- Stakeholders generally aren't involved in day-to-day execution → take time in initiation to lay out the details clearly, so the team works toward outcomes expected by **all** stakeholders
	- The **initiation phase** is usually the most ideal time to make changes; later, substantive changes may require undoing completed work → don't be afraid to make adjustments early
- Example of misalignment: one stakeholder wants to **fully automate the ordering experience** with the tablets over time; another wants the rollout to **improve order accuracy** → the project manager facilitates a discussion to reach agreement on the goals
- Collect feedback when presenting the charter, identify misalignments, make changes
- Record feedback and document misalignments and their resolutions → reference later
	- E.g. an **appendix** (a section of additional content at the end of a document) with a **time stamp** (the date, and sometimes the time, when new content was created or added)
- Activity: observe how Peta guides a conversation among disagreeing stakeholders, then revise the charter summary, goals, and deliverables
#### Project charters: Drafting SMART goals
- In initiation some goals might be broad because not all details are determined → add specificity where possible to avoid misalignment and understand scope, budget, and timeline
	- You must be able to accurately identify whether a goal has been met; clear goals → determine the **deliverables** (specific tasks or outcomes that contribute to the completion of a goal)
- Stakeholders often describe outcomes vaguely ("more customers to use a service", "sell more units") → the project manager ensures goals are well-defined → clear roadmap, focus, less wasted time and miscommunication
- **SMART** goals: **specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, time-bound** (→ [Course 2: SMART goals and OKRs](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
	- **Specific**: answers "What am I aiming to accomplish?" and "What are the requirements and constraints of this goal?"
		- Tip: look for **subjective** words (bigger, better, faster) → agree with stakeholders on what they mean in practice (faster by how much?)
	- **Measurable**: add numbers and figures (increase profits by 5%? by 30%?)
		- **Benchmarking**: evaluating success against the standard → research how others in your industry quantify success (e.g. search "How do restaurants measure success?", "How do you evaluate employee training sessions?")
		- Restaurant metrics: **table turnover rate** (average length of time a guest spends at their table), **prime cost** (labor cost + total cost of goods like food and drink), **average check amount** (average amount guests spend on a given meal)
		- Every industry (hospitality, entertainment, construction, tech) has its own success metrics; metrics are a big part of how Google measures success
	- **Attainable**: challenging but not impossible → "Can it be done?" Do you have the time, resources, and people to complete it on time and within budget?
	- **Relevant**: "Does it make sense for us as a company or project team to pursue this goal?" → check how closely project goals align with wider organizational goals
		- Google uses **OKRs** (objectives and key results) for organization-wide goal setting: combine a goal and a metric to determine a measurable outcome
		- E.g. Sauce & Spoon's broader objective = fresh, quick food for working families in its community → relevant project goal: **decrease customer checkout time by 10%, on average, in the first six weeks after implementation**
	- **Time-bound**: add a deadline
- Activity: use supporting materials to turn the drafted goals into SMART goals and add any additional goals
#### Project charters: Defining scope, benefits, and costs
- The primary audience of the charter = stakeholders → emphasize benefits of particular interest to a key stakeholder; stakeholders might not see more detailed plans later → get **consensus** on critical elements now
- **Scope**: the boundaries of the project (e.g. the number of restaurants in the pilot); details that don't contribute to goals are **out-of-scope** → think about what's needed to achieve the goal
	- Questions: Which details are stakeholders aligned on that are in-scope? Do stakeholders disagree on any elements? Any details to designate as out-of-scope?
- **Benefits**: the expected gains of a project → direct monetary gains, or indirect benefits (improvement in customer engagement or satisfaction)
	- Usually found in the **business case** or **project proposal**
	- Tablet rollout examples: speed up service and increase sales by an estimated percent; clear data points on customer ordering and an integrated **point-of-sale system** to guide future decisions
	- A list of benefits can reveal project goals you might have missed
- **Costs**: money spent on project tasks and the prices of time, resources, and labor; evaluated and managed with a **budget** (an estimate of the amount of money allocated to complete the project) (→ [Course 3: Project budgeting](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- Build the list with stakeholders: estimates on labor, materials, other factors
	- The benefits of a project should outweigh the costs (→ [Course 2: Cost-benefit analysis](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/)); this section helps stakeholders agree the project is worth the money
- Activity: add a list of benefits and a list of costs to the charter
#### Afsheen: Initiating a project effectively
- Common problems in initiation: getting **alignment between key stakeholders**; securing the right people → "attraction is much more effective than promotion": make talent want to join the project rather than just assigning work → good foundation, best velocity
- Refining success criteria: ask many questions (a list of about 20), e.g. understand the environment, pitfalls or risks in the ecosystem; survey interviews with people in the environment ("What do you see as a problem?"); quantitative questions about measurables
- Why projects fail: (1) really **poor project definition**, (2) **disorganization** (the project lead doesn't establish a framework to manage the project), (3) **too many people** → no accountability
- Stakeholders have different perspectives; if two key sponsors are "apples and oranges" → meet with them a lot, broker compromise, work through the initial project formation problem
### Negotiating effectively with stakeholders
#### Completing a stakeholder analysis
- Almost every part of a project requires input and agreement from multiple stakeholders, who don't all value the same things → a successful project manager must be a skilled **negotiator**
- Common aspects negotiated during a project: **scope**, **costs and benefits**, **timeline**, **success criteria**, **roles and responsibilities**, **resources** → good early alignment can prevent some of these negotiations
	- Early on, agree on the scope (note how Peta focuses on securing stakeholder buy-in for scope); the same skills apply to negotiating budget, timeline, or product requirements later
- **Stakeholder analysis**: a visual representation of all of the stakeholders → who they are, their priorities, how they prefer to communicate; how strongly to prioritize a stakeholder's needs or goals (→ [Course 2: Completing a stakeholder analysis](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
	- Stakeholders have a vested interest in the project's success → helps you include the right people in important conversations and prioritize different needs
	- Answer as many questions as you can about each stakeholder, then identify who directly influences the project and their level of interest
	- **Influence**: how much power a stakeholder has and how much their actions affect the project outcome
	- **Interest**: how much the stakeholder's needs will be affected by project operations and outcomes
	- High interest + high influence → keep them informed, make their goals top priority; low interest + low influence → you might deprioritize one of their personal preferences
	- Map them on a **power-interest grid** → clarity on whom to prioritize and how to approach negotiations with each
- One of the most important negotiations: **defining and maintaining scope** → stakeholders might push to add more; the project manager negotiates what's realistic within budget and timeline expectations
#### Stakeholder management: Tips and takeaways
- **Stakeholder management**: the process of maintaining good relationships with the people who have the most influence on your work → good communication, teamwork, trust
- During project initiation:
	- **Identify all stakeholders at the beginning** → set clear expectations, responsibilities, boundaries; gives them time to voice concerns; a sense of ownership → embrace roles, give input, help remove barriers
	- **Keep the project vision clear**: the **project vision** describes the need the project is fulfilling; stakeholders may pressure to increase requirements, shorten the timeline, or cut resources → agree on the vision and what "done" looks like; include highly influential stakeholders in strategic planning
	- **Equip stakeholders with user-friendly resources**: a **one-pager** (a one-page overview of the project) or weekly status report with the latest information and links to main project artifacts; access to necessary documentation
- Throughout the life cycle:
	- Find out what stakeholders care about and why → "What are your most important priorities and goals? What role would you like to play? How will this project support you?"
	- Adjust communication frequency and approach to roles and preferences → "Here's how I plan to keep you informed—does that work for you?"
	- Enlist senior stakeholders when necessary → "Who else do you recommend I reach out to?"
	- Once stakeholders have a vested interest, bring project problems to them → "How would you handle this situation? What solutions come to mind?"
- Key takeaway: stakeholders can be your biggest allies
- Stakeholder management: Tips and takeaways: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/in44Y
#### Finding mutually beneficial solutions
- You'll need negotiating techniques to move a disagreement (sensitive goals or scope, disagreement among stakeholders, or you disagreeing with stakeholders) **from stalemate to solution**
- **Persuasion** and **negotiation**: constructive tools to enhance communication, clarify wants and needs, achieve workable solutions for everyone → also boost your influence
	- View the people you negotiate with as **colleagues and peers, not opponents** → that's why you do a stakeholder analysis (know them as people and as partners who want the project to succeed)
	- Address conflict, build consensus among stakeholder groups, de-escalate conflict
- **Mutual benefit**: all parties involved gain some kind of benefit or advantage
	- E.g. you want to hire five people, a stakeholder wants to limit head count to three → install automated software to pick up some of the work, or adjust the timeline or expectations so five people aren't needed
	- Goal: a solution that **maximizes benefits, minimizes losses, and is fair for all** → brainstorm all possible options, present multiple alternatives
- Know what **tradeoffs** you're willing to make → clear understanding of priorities among scope, timeline, budget
	- A fixed deadline → negotiate scope changes that would push past it; required look or function → requirements are top priority, negotiate budget or timeline instead
	- Tool: the **triple constraint** model (**iron triangle**) → decide if a request is acceptable and what impact it'll have (→ [Course 2: Triple Constraint](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
#### Negotiating scope with stakeholders
- Stakeholders may want to adjust scope after it's established (too much work with too few resources, unrealistic timeline, additional tasks needed) → meet with them so they can raise concerns
- Tips:
	- **Understand motivations**: budgetary (reduce costs), interpersonal (more time to complete tasks), personal career goals (keep a position, strive for a promotion)
	- **Set the scene**: reflect on why you are meeting; remind them of the shared common goal
	- **Listen first**: hear them before presenting your views → easier for them to accept your suggestions
	- **Ask questions to define goals**: get specific, measurable details (not subjective language) → e.g. "We want to cut the amount of time it takes customers to sign up for our newsletter by at least 30 percent."
	- **Explain the "why" before the "what"**: reasons and value first, then the request
	- **Do not oversell**: state your case, then withdraw slightly; silence as a sign of respect; if they're quiet, you've stimulated thought
	- **Be creative**: alternative solutions turn a heavy negotiation into a team effort; there may be more than one solution
	- **Do not make it personal**: focus on what's good for the project; reframe with objective facts
	- **Seek a win-win outcome**: a **mutually beneficial agreement** (benefits all parties) should always be the goal, even if one party compromises more
- Negotiating scope with stakeholders: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/rywMz
#### Achieving a win-win outcome
- **Win-win outcome** = mutually beneficial agreement; applies not only to internal stakeholders but also to vendors, contractors, suppliers
- Best practices:
	- **Share information**: open lines of communication rather than withholding information (e.g. tell a new supplier the last one provided low-quality products → clear quality expectations)
	- **Ask questions and listen actively** to clarify the other party's concerns and expectations
	- **Propose multiple options**: one option can set you up for failure; if all are rejected, ask which one they like best → direction toward a solution
- Achieving a win-win outcome: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/0Kk4z
#### Stanton: Managing scope changes with stakeholders
- Stanton: Program Manager at **YouTube** (combines many projects into a program: development, product requirements, testing)
- Scope changes can happen at any time, often right before a launch
- Story (startup, an app about body weight percentages): the CEO, focused on launch PR and marketing, wanted the graphs changed at the last minute
	- Got an estimate from developers → far beyond the timeline; launch date was fixed → several proposals → negotiated: launch as is, ship the change in a second release about **two weeks** later
- Stakeholders want some things more than others → **stack rank** last-minute requests within the priority level
- "You can't get anything for free": a scope change means checking priorities to see **what gets pushed out** by the new item
- Stakeholders come to you because you understand everything happening on the ground → that's your power in this role
#### Applying influence in negotiations
- **Dr. Jay Conger**'s four steps to effective influencing: **establish credibility**, **frame for common ground**, **provide evidence**, **connect emotionally** (→ [Course 4: Steps to effective influencing](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- **Coalition**: a temporary alliance or partnering of individuals or groups to achieve a common purpose or engage in a joint activity → two or more people advocating together exert more influence than alone
	- Applies all four steps: boosts credibility, members help find common ground and provide evidence, include someone with a positive relationship with the stakeholder to connect emotionally
	- E.g. which Sauce & Spoon stakeholders could you form a coalition with to negotiate an out-of-scope request?
- An effective coalition: the right mix of **power and interest** → people with vested interest and expertise + people with a high level of power in the organization; guided by the **stakeholder analysis**
- Asking for support (well-crafted email, in person, or phone call):
	- State the issue and which aspect of the project is being negotiated
	- Ask if they would consider supporting your position or solution, and state it
	- Refer to their sources of power or interest → e.g. "The timing of the launch will affect business hours, and your experience managing restaurant projects year-round could be helpful in explaining why this factor needs to be reconsidered."
- Activity: write a persuasive email to influence stakeholders to join the coalition

## Module 2: Building out a project plan
### Identifying tasks and milestones
#### Introduction: Building out a project plan
- The confirmed project charter is used to start the **planning phase**
- **Project plan**: the central artifact a project manager builds during the planning phase; most projects capture requirements in it
	- The Sauce & Spoon project plan is a critical part of your portfolio → shows you can break a large project into a set of achievable, smaller tasks
- Scenario recap: Sauce & Spoon, a small but growing chain with five locations; Peta, first in-house project manager, launches the pilot of tabletop tablet menus at two locations in-scope, on time, within budget
- Next activities: analyze conversations, emails, and other documentation to build the plan; identify tasks and milestones; ask the right questions and practice **empathy** to make accurate time estimates
#### Identifying project tasks: Analyzing documentation
- Project documentation to analyze: project charters, emails, old project plans (available when you join a new organization or switch projects); build the plan in a spreadsheet (project plan template) or project management software
- **Project plan**: documents the scope, tasks, milestones, budget, and overall activities to keep the project on track; useful for any project, big or small
	- At its center: the **project schedule** → guide for time estimates, determining milestones, monitoring overall progress
	- Main jobs of a project manager: identify all project tasks, estimate how much time each will take, track each task's progress
- Adding tasks and milestones for the first time: review the **goals and deliverables** in the charter → list all items with tasks or milestones associated
	- **Milestones**: important points within the schedule that indicate progress; usually signify the completion of a deliverable or phase (→ [Course 3: Tasks and milestones](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- **Project tasks**: activities that need to be accomplished within a set period of time; assigned according to each person's role and skills
	- E.g. deliverable: promoting the new tablet menus with **table signs and email blasts** → milestone = completion of this deliverable (sign-offs on final marketing materials, confirmed email blast dates); tasks: write multiple drafts of the materials, generate an email list, program the emails for the correct dates
	- For each deliverable, ask: "What steps do we need to take in order to achieve this?" → steps become tasks
	- E.g. deliverable: a **post-dining survey** to assess customer satisfaction → assign someone to develop the survey, determine how to deliver it, create a process for carrying it out
- Other documentation:
	- **Emails** related to the project → ask to have relevant ones forwarded; uncover tasks and team members to follow up with
	- **Older project plans** for a similar initiative (e.g. a colleague's product launch plan; unrelated projects that also had construction components) → inspiration, possible task durations, subject matter experts, suppliers
- While reviewing, ask: Is there a large task worked on by many people that could be broken into smaller tasks for individuals? Are there signals that prior tasks must be completed first? (e.g. "install tablets" implies selecting a tablet vendor first)
#### Tips for defining project tasks
- Breaking tasks down takes practice: decide which tasks need subtasks (e.g. a cross-country move: no need to break down which box to unload first, but the movers' tasks may need smaller steps)
- Guidelines:
	- **Define tasks in one or two sentences**: a longer description signals a complex task to break down or clarify
	- **Look at task dependencies**: what must be completed or handed off before work on a task can begin (e.g. setting up the stage for an awards ceremony depends on estimates from an **AV contractor**, procuring equipment, constructing the stage backdrop)
	- **Enlist help from team members**: discuss each broad goal or major task as a team → varying perspectives (someone with experience may split a task into three)
	- **Define tasks by the time they will take**: reveals lengthy tasks (possible hidden subtasks); helps schedule around long tasks and determine milestones (often the culmination of a series of tasks); celebrate completion of large tasks
	- **Identify tasks by their "done" factors**: begin with the end in mind → work backwards to find missed steps and checkpoints
- Tips for defining project tasks: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/uzEHQ
#### Identifying project tasks: Conducting online research
- **Domain knowledge**: knowledge of a specific industry, topic, or activity → online research is a helpful strategy to gather it when a project or industry is unfamiliar
	- E.g. private banking: how clients open accounts, back office operations, how trade confirmations are verified; Sauce & Spoon: guest averages, table turn times
	- You don't need to be an expert, but familiarity with different industries shows **versatility** and saves time on future projects; no one expects all the answers right away
- Online research → industry terms, techniques, processes; how other organizations managed similar projects (inspiration from successes, lessons from mistakes)
- Tips:
	- Search **news coverage of similar projects** at other companies (e.g. "menu tablet news", "restaurant tablet news") → note surprising outcomes and unanticipated roadblocks → add tasks to achieve similar results or avoid similar roadblocks
	- Search **research** on related topics (e.g. "restaurant tablet research", "digital menu ordering"; add tags like "best practices" or "key takeaways")
	- Research **similar projects in other industries** (e.g. tablet installation in retail stores or coffee shops)
	- Review your task list and research the specifics of executing that work (e.g. choosing the tablet model → smaller subtasks?)
#### Identifying project tasks: Analyzing key conversations
- A populated plan shows employers you can identify key areas of work from documentation, research, and conversations and **synthesize** them into a single organized document
- Discussions with stakeholders and team members uncover missing tasks or clarify subtasks:
	- **Group brainstorm** with team members likely to work on the tasks (e.g. Peta's team brainstorms challenges waitstaff and guests might have with the tablets)
	- **One-on-one conversations** about tasks they'll own (e.g. a vendor who specializes in training restaurant employees; a graphic designer for new marketing materials) → tasks may be more complex than assumed, or missing a key step
	- **Experts in your organization** not involved in the project → processes, filling gaps
	- **Key stakeholders** for remaining gaps → be strategic: those with high or medium interest or influence (subject matter experts, those directly affected like team leadership); refer to the stakeholder analysis
		- Prepare: gather information, outline clear outstanding questions; present your research and current task list and explain how they can help → respects their limited time
- Conversations contain more detail than you need → note extra information that may be useful later
- Right level of detail: enough to check on progress and identify problems early, not so much that you endlessly revise the plan and burden the team with constant updates → varies by project and team
#### Ordering tasks and identifying milestones
- Finalize the list: break any remaining larger tasks into subtasks
- **Order tasks** in the sequence they must be completed → helps assign start and end dates
	- Consider the natural **order of operations**, **dependencies**, and **prerequisites** (e.g. can't train staff on tablets before they're installed and tested)
	- Ask each task owner what needs to happen before they can start; search e.g. "prerequisites for launching new hardware"
	- Rearrange spreadsheet rows (e.g. research menu tablet models before signing a contract with the supplier)
- **Identify milestones** (→ [Course 3: How to set milestones](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/)):
	- Points where you and your team can **evaluate the work completed so far** (e.g. the first internal test run of the tablet's ordering capabilities); may match deliverables
	- Tasks **stakeholders have a particular interest in** (review notes from conversations; e.g. when a tablet supplier has been selected, since it impacts the budget)
	- Tasks that carry a **high risk** or signal the **completion of a phase or major task** (e.g. the first successful test run of ordering capabilities)
### Making accurate time estimates
#### Time estimation: Asking the right questions
- **Time estimation**: a prediction of the total amount of time required to complete a task (→ [Course 3: Making realistic time estimates](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- Estimates for each task → overall timeline in relation to individual deadlines and milestones; easy to track progress and recognize if a task will go over → adjust quickly
- You won't be an expert initially → need your team and subject matter experts; asking the right questions helps them reach the most accurate estimates
- Strategies with task experts:
	- **Check their understanding of the task**: ask them to explain all the detailed steps → makes them think through the work before estimating
	- **Ask for estimates of the sub-steps**, add them up, and compare the total with their estimate for the whole task
	- **Discuss assumptions**: equipment, supplies, number of people, skill and experience level of people working alongside them → how likely is it that some assumptions won't work out, and how would that impact the estimate?
	- **Effort estimate** vs **total duration estimate**: effort = only the actual time it takes to complete a task; total duration = effort + other factors like approvals, prep work, testing
		- E.g. designing and launching the tablet **checkout page**: effort for designing = 8 hours (mock up and implement), but total duration includes testing, feedback, and approvals → more than 8 hours
	- **Compare against actual time spent on similar tasks** in previous work: what was different/the same, how long it took, does that change the estimate?
#### Time estimation: Three-point estimating
- **Three-point estimating**: determines the most realistic time estimate for a task using optimistic and pessimistic calculations (best-case and worst-case scenarios); also accounts for potential risks to budget and resources
- Each task receives three estimates:
	- **Optimistic**: best-case scenario, issues will not occur (e.g. supplies come in early, everything works as it's supposed to)
	- **Most likely**: some issues might occur; how long the task usually takes under normal circumstances (supplies arrive on time, slight adjustments)
	- **Pessimistic**: issues will definitely occur; everything that could go wrong does (supplies late or incorrect, nothing works)
- Ask task experts or research to understand best- and worst-case scenarios → add notes to the plan for each task
- Example: **training the staff to use the tablets**
	- Optimistic: well-qualified vendor with all materials, on time; all staff on time and finish within the scheduled time; all equipment works → **4 hours** (2 hours training + 1 hour each for setup and post-training review) on the originally scheduled date
	- Most likely: vendor qualified but missing some materials or new and needs extra time; a couple of staff absent or late (extra training time); minor equipment glitches; may be rescheduled later in the week → closer to **6 hours**, date likely 2–3 days later
	- Pessimistic: original vendor quits and a new one must be hired; several staff no-shows or turnover; equipment not delivered on time or doesn't work → training still about 6 hours but rescheduled up to a **full week** later
- Don't take a quoted estimate without understanding its context: an optimistic 2 days that takes a week throws off the schedule; a pessimistic 1 month that takes a week wastes time that could go to other tasks or an earlier release
	- Always using the worst case is **wasteful** → compare best and worst cases with the most likely, then build in a **buffer** for likely risks while keeping the project efficient
#### The three-point estimating technique
- Estimation techniques (costs, scope, time) → better forecasts for stakeholders and clients, more accurate budgeting of funds and resources
- Process: for each task add a duration estimate for optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic (from research or a task expert) + notes about the conditions of each
	- Staff training example: optimistic **4 hours**, most likely **6 hours**, pessimistic **6 days**
- **Determining a final estimate**: compare optimistic and pessimistic with the most likely, consider likely conditions
	- Team has never done the task, or dependencies are unknown → closer to **pessimistic**
	- Team is familiar with the task and optimistic conditions can be confirmed → closer to **optimistic**
	- Or simply use the **most likely** estimate, especially if the difference between optimistic and pessimistic is minimal (a few hours, or no more than one or two days)
	- Build in a **buffer** for likely risks
- Formulas (E = final estimate, o = optimistic, p = pessimistic, m = most likely):
	- **Triangular Distribution**: E = (o + m + p) / 3 → each estimate has identical weight
	- **Beta (PERT) Distribution**: E = (o + 4m + p) / 6 → weighted average; the most likely case gets more weight → more accurate in most cases; often used for both cost and time estimates
- The three-point estimating technique: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/wyUlK
#### Time estimation: Applying confidence level ratings
- **Confidence level rating**: indicates how confident you are in an estimate's accuracy → tells stakeholders how likely a task will be completed in the estimated time; addresses uncertainty (estimating is not a perfect science)
	- Ranges from **high** (very confident) to **low** (not very confident)
	- Add notes about risks or issues affecting the estimate → whether to ask the team for input, which tasks to track more closely
	- If confidence is low across a large percentage of estimates → communicate uncertainty about the timeline to stakeholders
- Ways to determine a rating:
	- **Three-point technique**: having considered best- and worst-case conditions → high confidence
	- **Poll your team** on their assigned tasks and calculate the average confidence as a percentage (e.g. **90%** → high; **60%** → medium)
	- **Define categories**: never done a project like this, done it once, done it a handful of times, done it a ton of times → never/once → low confidence
- A system that accounts for unknowns gives an accurate picture of the timeline → everyone is better off long term
#### Time estimation: Effective time estimate negotiation
- Peta negotiates with team members about tasks with low confidence ratings or longer-than-hoped estimates
- Negotiating with a **task expert**, not a stakeholder → goal: arrive at an **objectively accurate estimate together**, not persuade them to agree to an outcome
- People over- or underestimate time, costs, or resources unintentionally: being optimistic, trying to please you, or overly cautious
- Techniques for negotiating time estimates:
	- **Say no without saying no**: avoid "That won't work", "That's not going to happen", "I can't do that", "There's no way" (make people defensive) → ask open-ended questions: "How would you like me to proceed?", "How can we solve this problem?", "What can I do to help?" → invites collaboration
	- **Focus on interests, not positions**: the goal is not to win; identify their **interests** (basic needs, wants, motivations) → e.g. an expert who cares deeply about quality vs your deadline concern → ask which areas of quality they'd compromise on to shorten the estimate while keeping an acceptable degree
	- **Present mutually beneficial options**: maybe the expert is missing information, or you could find and supply a resource to lower the estimate
	- **Insist on objective criteria**: neutral information like **market value, research findings, previously documented experience, laws and regulations** → agree in advance which criteria to consult; ask an instinct-driven expert for clear, objective data supporting their instincts
#### Time estimation: Negotiating with empathy
- **Empathy**: the ability to understand and feel what others are feeling; imagining yourself in the other person's position (→ [Course 4: Team effectiveness](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- Questions about how long a task will take can make people feel insecure (not trusted, not competent) → without empathy you seem to be **micromanaging**
	- **Micromanaging**: a manager too closely observes, controls, or continuously reminds the people they manage of their assigned work → shows lack of trust and confidence
- Tips:
	- **Listen with curiosity**: start with a question instead of assumptions or suggestions (e.g. ask how long a task took on a previous project rather than suggesting a time frame)
	- **Repeat what you think you heard**: restate their message in your own words → they confirm intent; may see issues from another perspective
	- **Connect with their experience**: estimating is hard for anyone; share a time you misestimated; you know they want to do their best and you want to support them
	- **Recognize judgments**: acknowledge internal judgments (e.g. doubts about their work quality) and view the situation more compassionately; people read body language, facial expressions, and tone
	- **Recognize buffering**: ask up front whether they've included a buffer for holidays, sickness, childcare, or emergencies; assure them you want an honest answer
	- **Avoid distractions**: phone on silent, no notifications or texting, close your laptop → undivided attention
#### Torie: Practicing empathy as a program manager
- Torie: Education Program Manager at Google, works on the digital literacy curriculum **Applied Digital Skills**
- Empathy matters because team members and stakeholders have different work and communication styles → tailor messaging to different audiences to communicate goals and impact effectively
- Example: a project with about five people on the program team had **missed deadlines** → talking with a team member revealed personal issues → shifted resources and got help from other teammates; "meet people where they are", adjust timelines as needed
- Negotiating task estimates: ask a lot of questions from the beginning, talk to people who have been on the team longer, look for similar past projects (and different ones with different timelines), collect as much data as you can

## Module 3: Maintaining quality
### Developing a quality management plan
#### Introduction: Maintaining quality
- Moving from planning to the **execution phase** → first establish the project's **quality management plan** so you deliver a project that meets stakeholders' expectations
- This module: define quality standards, evaluate how successfully they are met, present findings to stakeholders, write evaluation and survey questions, create a **retrospective document** (used in discussions about project progress and process improvements) → add to your portfolio
#### Key quality management concepts
- **Quality planning**: the process the project manager or team establishes and follows for identifying which standards of quality are relevant to the project as a whole and how to satisfy them
	- Can't just launch and assume everything will be okay → clearly defined standards, processes, and methods of evaluation keep you focused on progress and alert you to needed adjustments
	- Works together with general project planning; for Sauce & Spoon, goals and success criteria focus the plan on **quality standards, evaluation questions, and feedback surveys**
- Benefits of quality management: **delivering a quality product**, **decreasing overhead** (**overhead** = costs; fewer errors that cost money to fix), **increasing collaboration and ongoing reviews** (team always learning and giving feedback)
- Main concepts (→ [Course 4: Key quality management concepts](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/)):
	- **Quality planning** (above)
	- **Quality standards**: requirements, specifications, or guidelines used to ensure materials, products, processes, and services are fit for achieving a desired outcome
	- **Quality assurance (QA)**: a review process that evaluates whether your project is moving toward delivering a high-quality service or product
	- **Quality control (QC)**: techniques used to ensure quality standards are maintained when a problem is identified
- **Quality management plan**: documents all the information needed to manage quality throughout the life cycle → policies, processes, and criteria for project quality + roles and responsibilities for carrying them out
#### Defining quality standards
- **Quality**: making sure you deliver what you say you will, as efficiently as you can → on time and under budget doesn't necessarily mean goals are met; tracked throughout the life cycle
- **Quality standards**: the requirements and specifications your product or service must meet to be considered successful by your organization and the customer → defined for major tasks, milestones, deliverables
	- Help identify ways of testing and evaluating quality during planning, execution, and after launch; if standards aren't met → adjust the project plan
- Example deliverable: **training of management, front of house staff, and back of house staff** → What must staff be able to do or demonstrate after training? Same training for each group? Different requirements for front and back of house? Specific time frame, budget, or location? → answers start the list of standards
- Resources to determine standards:
	- **Project documents**: business case and project charter (goals, scope, budget)
	- **Conversations with experts and stakeholders** (e.g. ask the training vendor or tablet sales representative how long the tablets take to recharge; ask funding stakeholders how long they expect the tablets to last before replacement)
	- **Industry research** for established standards (software and construction: functionality, design, safety; many industries: ease of use, productivity, effectiveness, customer satisfaction)
- Standards must be **objective and measurable**
	- Stakeholders may say "ease of use" without specifics → ask "What would be a sign that the tablets are easy to use or hard to use?" → e.g. "It shouldn't take longer than **20 seconds** to place an order", "Returning customers report that it's faster to use the tablet versus placing an order with a server"
	- Productivity and effectiveness: Should the tablets change how front of house staff work? Make them faster or serve more tables at once?
	- Customer satisfaction: How would the tablets ideally impact the customer's experience? What would you want the customer to do or say as a result?
- Still use **critical thinking** to determine and adjust the right standards for your project
### Evaluating quality standards
#### Creating evaluation questions
- With quality standards defined, you can measure against them and begin implementing → Peta's team has begun the rollout: both pilot restaurants have tablets installed, staff trained, guests ordering with the tablets
- **Quality assurance**: reviewing processes to evaluate whether the project is delivering an acceptable level of quality; examples: **beta testing**, **internal checklists**, **feedback surveys**
- **Evaluation**: observing, measuring, and comparing findings to a set of agreed-upon criteria (quality standards or overall goals); a form of research designed to **promote learning and inform decisions**; provides **accountability**
	- Lets you **improve**, **judge**, and **learn** about aspects of the project:
		- Improve: e.g. implement the staff training process more efficiently
		- Judge: whether something works as intended or you should continue (e.g. staff struggled because touch screens kept malfunctioning → continue with the original launch date?)
		- Also check for **unintended problems** (e.g. installing tablets while the restaurant is open → negative dining experience)
		- Learn: what made the project run as intended, how to replicate it, how to overcome challenges in the future
- Evaluation process:
	- First articulate **why** you're evaluating (improving, judging, learning) → shapes the questions; narrow the focus by reviewing project and organizational goals
	- E.g. Sauce & Spoon reached a milestone: three deliverables required for launch testing are complete → **installing the tablets**, **integrating the tablets with the POS system**, **training the staff**; Peta's evaluation informs future phases, including a rollout at **two more restaurant locations** → her why: judge the quality and performance of the tablets and identify ways to improve the training process
- **Evaluation question**: a key question about the outcomes, impact, and/or effectiveness of your project or program; two categories:
	- **Questions to improve**: How can we improve? What is working and what's not? Which goals are being met? Who is benefiting? What are the most common participant reactions?
	- **Questions to measure and compare** (judge whether to proceed): What were the results? Were there unintended outcomes? What were the costs and benefits? Any lessons to be learned? Should we continue?
	- E.g. "To what extent do tablets improve the staff's work performance?"
- Effective evaluation questions: address stakeholder or user **values, interests, and concerns**; relate to the **purpose** of the project and the evaluation; are **worth answering** and important for the project and beyond; are **practical and feasible** with available resources
#### Determining evaluation indicators
- **Evaluation indicators**: reveal the specific type of data that needs to be collected to answer your evaluation questions → state what you want to measure (the number of something, level of satisfaction, preferences, demographic information like age, gender, experience)
	- Like quality standards add specificity to goals, indicators determine the specific type of response you're aiming for
	- "Indicate" = point out or show → pathways for answering evaluation questions; measurable evidence of meeting a goal; visible signs like test scores, attendance rates, observed behavior
- E.g. "To what extent do tablets improve the staff's work performance?" → indicators: faster **table turnover rates**, higher **tip averages**, higher quality ratings from customers
	- Observed behavior: fewer staff congregating by the beverage station or arriving late → increased productivity; **over 90% staff compliance** with the tablet-ordering process → order placement accuracy
- Indicators are clues, signs, or markers that measure one aspect and show how close it is to meeting the established quality standards
#### Developing a survey
- **Surveys**: each respondent answers a set of clearly defined questions; the data are collected and analyzed → a popular method to answer evaluation questions and demonstrate examples of the evaluation indicators; Peta creates **customer surveys** for the tablet rollout
	- Evaluate and measure the quality of a process, goal, or deliverable; show what's working and what's not; whether you met quality standards and where to adjust
- Survey development process: (1) develop evaluation questions, (2) define evaluation indicators, (3) determine what type of survey to design and questions to ask
- **Survey question** vs **evaluation question**: a survey question is designed to **collect data** that helps answer evaluation questions → a more direct interpretation designed to get data points
	- E.g. evaluation question "To what extent do tablets increase work performance?" → indicator: how much **side work** staff complete during a shift → survey questions: Are the tablets easy to use? Was there enough time during the training to practice and ask questions? On average, how many side work tasks do you complete during a shift? Since using the tablets, how often have you sent back an incorrect order?
- Types of survey questions:
	- **Open-ended**: require more than one-word answers; respondents answer in their own words (e.g. "What went well during the presentation?", "What did you find most useful or interesting?")
	- **Closed-ended**: a single response:
		- **Yes/no or true/false** (e.g. "Did you order an appetizer?", "Have you eaten at this restaurant before?")
		- **Multiple choice**: select one or all that apply (e.g. "How often do you dine at this location each month?" → 0–1, 2–3, 4–5, 5 or more)
		- **Scaled**: rate on a scale (frequency, like/dislike, importance) (e.g. "On a scale of one to five, how do you rate your overall dining experience, with one being poor and five being excellent?")
- Tips: ask what you mean to ask; each question specific, addressing **only one measurable aspect**; don't make **assumptions** about respondents (knowledge, preferences, life experiences) → answer options that allow accurate answers; don't give too much detail → may influence answers and create **bias**
### Presenting data
#### Delivering an evaluation presentation
- The Sauce & Spoon surveys have been administered and data collected → analyze the data and report how well the project measures up to its quality standards; the presentation slides show you can synthesize and summarize data into recommendations for a particular audience
- **Consider your audience**: what's most meaningful to them and how much time they have; mixed audiences (team members, managers, stakeholders, executives) → you may present the same data in different ways
	- Your team → a **detailed report** to address the aspects they're responsible for
	- Senior stakeholders and executives → a **summary** of the most important information and its impact on their investment
- Create a **detailed evaluation report** addressing the evaluation questions, then summarize it into the right format; common styles besides a full report:
	- **Summary sheet**: a one- or two-page write-up with only the most relevant information (a flyer or snapshot of findings)
	- **Slide-based presentation**: digital slides to visually present information
- An evaluation is **not a raw data report** → reflect what the data means and how it answers the evaluation questions → **filter and analyze** the data (the most important part)
	- E.g. **36%** of respondents reported a negative dining experience with the tablets → could mean tablets weren't installed correctly (glitches), tablets were poor quality, staff weren't trained well enough (delayed or incorrect orders), or respondents simply prefer a standard dining experience → additional analysis needed
	- Look for **trends, patterns, and anomalies**; share the process with team members to check understanding and uncover more through varied perspectives
- Shape the story into one cohesive narrative: **storytelling** = turning facts into narrative to communicate something to your audience (→ [Course 4: Presenting data to tell a project's story](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
	- For the tablet project: remind stakeholders of the overall goal and purpose → identify the milestone evaluated and how it's expected to meet goals → what the data revealed (not every data point) → major issues + summary of the rest; if going well, a few highlights; if major failings, possible solutions or specific questions
#### Evaluation presentations: Components and tips
- **Evaluation presentation**: examines how well your project is working according to an identified set of standards
- Components:
	- **Introduction**: summary of the presentation → overview of goals and desired outcomes, summary of findings, lessons learned, recommendations; no more than a paragraph
	- **What is being evaluated and why**: purpose = measure how well the project meets the established quality standards → state the goal, milestone, or deliverable evaluated and its quality standards; include the evaluation questions and indicators used
		- Pro tip: stakeholders have limited time → select only the most important standards and questions
	- **Evaluation findings**: one section per standard, summarizing what the findings mean; make a clear judgment (What did you learn? What's the takeaway?) and tell the story of what the data means for the project and stakeholders
		- E.g. "The data reveals that 36% of users felt their dining experience was negatively impacted by the tablets" → synthesize why (software not installed properly, ineffective staff training, customers prefer waitstaff) so stakeholders can make decisions or declare this aspect successful or not
		- Pro tip: visualize the data with graphs or charts
	- **Conclusion with recommendations**: restate findings, propose a couple of recommendations for the next phase; how findings may be used in future projects
- Format: slide-based presentations in **Google Slides** or **Microsoft PowerPoint** → images and succinct bullet points, summarized information; great for high-level presentations
- Additional tips: **tailor communications to stakeholders**, **start with an interesting hook** (ice breaker, joke, interesting visual aid), **use visuals throughout**
- Evaluation presentations: Components and tips: https://www.coursera.org/learn/applying-project-management/supplement/ySCch
### Facilitating retrospectives
#### The value of retrospectives
- Retrospectives are an example of **quality control**: they help teams adjust and improve processes as needed
- **Retrospective** (**retro**): a workshop or meeting that gives project teams time to reflect on a project → discuss successes and setbacks, learn from mistakes (→ [Course 4: The purpose of a retrospective](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/); → [Course 5: The Sprint Retrospective](/blog/agile-project-management/))
	- Often at the very end, but also a process improvement tool throughout the life cycle, especially after a **milestone** (e.g. right after implementing the tablets and testing them with **beta users**)
- Benefits (three main purposes): **encourage team building** (understand different perspectives), **facilitate improved collaboration** on future projects, **promote positive changes** in future procedures and processes
- Have an **agenda** to guide the discussion, organize the meeting, and document learnings
- The project manager manages the tone, makes every team member feel included, identifies details to record in the **retrospective document** → facilitate a respectful and productive discussion recognizing successes and areas for improvement
#### Retrospectives: Encouraging participation
- Low participation prevents meaningful process improvements; retrospectives can be intimidating (they draw attention to challenges) → before one, ask: does your team seem likely to contribute?
- Techniques used at Google:
	- **Create a safe environment**: open with the policy "what's said here stays here, what's learned here leaves"; remind the team the meeting is free from stakeholders or customers
	- **Model the participation you want**: start with your own successes and challenges; prepare examples you could have handled better (e.g. a paperwork error that slowed tablet delivery by **two business days**) → admitting your mistakes makes it okay for others
	- **Pose a group question and ask for individual responses**: e.g. each person thinks of one success and one challenge, then shares
		- If "What went well?" / "What went wrong?" don't work, rephrase: "What about this project should we **start, stop, and continue**?"
	- **Review the project timeline**: if the team only mentions recent successes and challenges → refresh memories of the entire project
#### Retrospectives: Encouraging accountability
- **Accountability**: being responsible for decisions associated with a project or task → participants must be honest about how the team could have performed better
- **Accountability ≠ blame**: only accountability belongs in a retrospective; blame shuts people down; accountability encourages thinking **holistically** about mistakes and challenges and finding solutions
	- Also encourages **ownership** → more motivation to meet quality standards
- Techniques:
	- **Come prepared with specific challenges** to discuss as a group (if the team focuses only on successes) → e.g. Sauce & Spoon **kitchen managers** felt left out of decisions made by the **general managers** → ask the team what problems led to this feedback
	- **Turn team complaints into SMART action items** (specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, time-bound) → e.g. invite the kitchen manager to weekly staff check-in meetings, add a **five-minute agenda item** for them to discuss issues and get feedback, check in **two months** later → team becomes solution-oriented
	- **Push the team to identify its role** in creating a challenge (even ones that seem outside their control, like a late delivery from the tablet supplier) → trace the series of events and find a missed opportunity (e.g. someone assigned early to manage the tablet vendor could have set up **weekly check-in calls** → foresight to work around the missed delivery)
	- Keep criticism **constructive**: **constructive criticism** = a respectful form of feedback intended to help the recipient improve a piece of work; if it gets unhelpful or harsh → redirect and **detach the challenge from any specific person**, steering toward process improvements the whole team can learn from
#### Retrospectives: Addressing negativity
- Retrospectives build trust, honesty, direct communication, but without **psychological safety** they easily turn negative → before one, ask: is this conversation likely to feel stressful for the team?
- Techniques:
	- **Set a positive tone at the start**: highlight project successes (positive feedback from a stakeholder, thank the team for reaching a major milestone)
	- **Determine how you'll set the tone**: meeting props → e.g. an even number of **green and red index cards** (successes on green, challenges on red) → subtly encourages thinking of successes too
	- **Anticipate negativity** by meeting one-on-one before the retrospective; understand the root (Does this person feel insecure about the value they add? Did they receive negative feedback on their work?) → e.g. reassure them of their value
	- **Ask team members individually to share their thoughts** (when one negative voice dominates) → gives everyone room, models solution-oriented thinking, discourages the negative member from responding to every question
	- **Call a meeting break**: a timeout helps de-escalate
- No single technique fits every scenario
#### Dana: Leading positive retrospectives
- Dana: Site Reliability Manager at Google
- Retrospective at the end of every project: what went well, what went poorly, and **where we got lucky** → reuse the lessons in the next project; sometimes done mid-project when decisions must be made
- Retrospectives must be **positive** and **blameless**; goal: continuously improve ourselves, our team, and our processes; ask every participant for their take on their part and the larger project
- Failure mode: people don't speak up (common at **Sprint Retrospectives**) → (1) they don't care much about improving and tolerate things, or (2) more concerning: lack of **psychological safety**
- Make the team feel they have a stake in what happens next and can make a difference through what they say in **postmortems**

## Module 4: Effective stakeholder communication
### Communicating with senior stakeholders
#### Introduction: Effective stakeholder communication
- Continue the execution phase, then transition to the **closing phase**: write an email about a problem to a senior stakeholder; create a **closeout report** to present the project impact to executive stakeholders; write an **executive summary**
- At the end: create your own **personal impact report** to reflect on your experience with the program
#### Communicating project problems
- **Synthesizing** information from multiple sources (conversations, emails, documents) is a skill project managers use constantly (conversations, meetings, documentation, emails); employers may want proof you can do it
- Every project has problems; most are handled within the immediate team, but sometimes you must **escalate** problems and propose solutions to a senior stakeholder for input and guidance (→ [Course 4: Escalating issues](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
	- Stakeholders shouldn't have to read several documents or email threads → synthesize relevant information into a coherent summary (e.g. don't just send a link to the project plan)
	- **Synthesizing**: gathering information from multiple sources and using those points to form your own analysis
- Ask: "How can I communicate a decision in a way that makes it easy for them to decide?" → a one- to two-sentence overview
	- E.g. five tasks overdue because of supplier delays, may affect the final deliverable → "A number of tasks have run past their due date because of supplier issues, so we recommend hiring a **second supplier** to hit the deliverable date. Otherwise, we'll need to push the launch date back."
	- Stakeholders can agree, disagree, or provide their own solution → either way you've communicated the problem and proposed a solution
#### Chris: The art of problem-solving
- Chris: Program Manager at Google on **Search** (features for millions of users across many surfaces, languages, information needs)
- "Problem solving is quintessentially the most important thing we do as program managers. It is the job." → scope problems, budget problems (too few or too many funds), people problems (not enough, too many, wrong skill sets), timeline problems
	- Identify the problem → figure out a framework from tools, processes, methodologies → propose a solution → get **buy-in**
- Find the **root cause**: what you experience is usually a red herring or outcome of the underlying problem → debug the systemic, process, tooling, or technical issue first → then put a plan together objectively → reach a decision
- Charters, scope docs, meeting notes, trackers are not the job → just mechanisms to do the real job: running scope and programs, convincing people, driving organizational change, solving strategic initiatives
- New to the field? Build problem-solving expertise through hobbies, building software for yourself or a friend, or passions in other industries
#### Connecting project problems to goals
- Identifying a problem's impact on the organization's **OKRs** helps determine and communicate its level of **risk and urgency** (→ [Course 2: Introduction to OKRs](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
- **Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)**: a tool for organization-wide goal setting; combine a goal and a metric to determine a measurable outcome
	- **Objectives**: what needs to be achieved (similar to a goal)
	- **Key results**: how an organization, team, or individual will tangibly measure success in meeting the objective
	- E.g. objective: prioritize customer needs and wants → key result: address feedback from customer reviews **within 24 hours**
- OKRs check project relevance: a project contributing to larger OKRs is worth the time and money; OKRs are a shared language (at Google every project aims to contribute tangibly to organization-wide OKRs; struggling to connect → re-evaluate the project)
- When communicating problems: identify the specific ways the problem might impact wider OKRs → explains why it needs to be addressed and catches a busy senior stakeholder's limited attention
	- Sauce & Spoon objectives: "We seek to run an efficient, profitable business model" / "We seek to prioritize our customer needs" → add a sentence to the problem summary explaining how the issue jeopardizes them
#### Writing emails to escalate a problem
- Email is an effective tool for quickly escalating a problem and asking a stakeholder for a decision on how to proceed (→ [Course 4: Writing an effective escalation email](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- Best practices:
	- **Think about what's most important to your stakeholder**: senior stakeholders care more about impact on the organization than on a single project → connect to OKRs and state the impact **within the first two sentences**
	- **Write a clear subject line** stating what the email is about + the action you want: "please review" (attached document), "urgent", "timely", "decision needed"
	- **Keep the body brief**: outline the problem (one or two sentences), explain the impact on the organization's OKRs (one sentence), clearly state the decision you need; include hyperlinks or attachments for supporting information
	- **Proofread** for misspellings, grammatical errors, inaccurate hyperlinks (spellcheck, grammar check)
#### Laura: Stakeholder communication best practices
- Laura: Executive Productivity Advisor at Google (coaches executives one-on-one on time management, meeting management, effective email, communication, organization)
- With executives, share only the absolutely important information and keep it **concise** (more detail is fine with coworkers)
- Do work beforehand: ask their **executive assistant** or someone who worked with them about their preferred communication style, presentation types, and the information they need to make decisions
- Each stakeholder communicates differently (e.g. one manager loved to brainstorm and wanted frequent meetings with all the details; the other was the opposite) → tailor the same information and decision process to each person
- Anticipate the **five questions** they'll probably ask → have the additional detail ready in the **appendix**
- Bring a problem **with proposed solutions**: "I think maybe we should do A, but we could also do B and C. What do you think?" → a starting point; shows you did the background work
- Email updates:
	- **TL;DR** ("too long; didn't read") at the top: the one sentence they need to know (update on project A, decision needed, action requested, deadline by)
	- Be concise: bullets, highlighting or bolding what must pop out, restate your ask at the end, include deadlines, links, attachments → easy to scan and reply
### Demonstrating impact
#### Completing a closeout report
- Sauce & Spoon has launched the tablets after passing quality standards → most of the project manager's work is done; one of the last tasks: the **closeout report** (→ [Course 4: The importance of project closure](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- Compiles all links and documentation into one place → "**good project hygiene**"; a time to reflect on team performance and ensure every task was completed
- **Closeout report**: confirms the project is done, summarizes deliverables, success metrics, feedback, lessons learned, and next steps; a reference document for the organization
	- Helps follow-up or similar projects run smoothly; sets future project managers up for success with meticulous information
	- Ensures everyone is satisfied with the work, finalizes the team's efforts and lets people move on, increases the impact of the team's work through communication with less-involved people
	- Created by project managers for project managers, future project managers, and anyone interested → someone unfamiliar should understand what the project was, why it was done, how well it went; your Sauce & Spoon closeout report should stand alone as a **work sample** for employers
- Sections:
	- **Project summary**: the objectives (what was the desired result?)
	- **Methodology or approach**: Waterfall, Agile, Lean, a combination, or something else
	- **Performance baseline** (one of the most important): actual results vs objectives from planning and execution → actual vs planned schedule, actual vs planned cost, planned vs delivered scope; a notes column for discrepancies or issues
	- **Key accomplishments and outcomes**, **Lessons learned**, **Next steps**, **Project documentation archive** → be thorough; purpose = compile and archive the most important aspects
#### Project impact report
- **Impact report**: shows others the value added by your project; usually a presentation guided by a deck or slideshow; for **senior stakeholders or project sponsors** not involved in the day-to-day details (unlike the detailed closeout report for future project managers) (→ [Course 4: Demonstrating project impact to stakeholders](/blog/project-execution-running-the-project/))
- Why report impact: **analyze results** to adapt and improve services; **motivate** staff and senior stakeholders by celebrating achievements; **build trust and credibility** with supporters, sponsors, funders, and beneficiaries; **share lessons** with similar organizations
- **Executive summary**: a few sentences to a paragraph describing the project's purpose and outcome; an overview of the main points of a larger report for stakeholders without time to read it all → "a highlight reel for your project"
	- Concise and strong description of positive outcomes, not too much information, not too vague
	- Test: if an executive only read this, would they understand the highlights? → How effectively was the project delivered and what did we learn?
	- Review your SMART goals, business case, and project charter
	- Tip: draft the rest of the impact report first (slides for **results**, **what worked**, **next steps**, with graphs and images) → easier to pull out highlights
- Executive summary elements: **project vision** (purpose; what need the project fulfills), **key accomplishments** (activities, tasks, milestones that led to success; main highlights; profitability; value added), **lessons learned** (potential improvements to future processes)
- Example: a money-saving app that automatically moves money from users' checking accounts into designated savings accounts **bi-monthly** (amount based on checking balance at withdrawal + variables like deposit frequency)
	- Vision: "Our goal is to help users get ahead financially by creating an algorithm-based automatic deposit system that pulls money into a savings account."
	- Key accomplishments: helped **1,000 beta users** save over **$300,000** in six months (proved the use case); financial highlight: charging **$3 per month** with 1,000 users in six months → netted **$18,000**
	- Lessons learned: beta users wanted more frequent updates about when their money was transferred → added in the update

#### Boost your project management skills with AI
- Ben: Staff Program Manager on the **Google Cloud** team (cloud networking features, training for the thousands of program managers at Google) → gen AI to write a project charter, run effective meetings, and more
- **Prompt**: the input you provide to the gen AI model to elicit a specific response
- Prompting framework **T-C-R-E-I** ("**T**houghtfully **C**reate **R**eally **E**xcellent **I**nputs"):
	- **Task**: what you want the model to do
		- **Persona**: the expertise to draw from (e.g. a professional speech writer, a marketing executive with 15 years of experience) or a target audience (a customer, your manager)
		- **Format**: how the output should appear (bulleted list, short sentences, table)
	- **Context**: necessary details → "Give me some ideas for a birthday present under $30" vs "Give me five ideas for a birthday present. My budget is $30. The gift is for a 29-year-old who loves winter sports and has recently switched from snowboarding to skiing."
	- **References**: examples for the tool to use (e.g. past birthday presents); not always available for abstract work
	- **Evaluate**: did the input give you the output you needed?
	- **Iterate**: add more information or tweak the prompt
	- Be clear and specific; use natural language and complete thoughts; a simple prompt is sometimes enough
- **Human-in-the-loop** approach: combines machine and human intelligence to train, use, verify, and refine results → be mindful of what you put in, always evaluate and verify what comes out
	- Consider whether confidential or sensitive information is needed; consult your organization's rules or policies; outside work, avoid personal or confidential information and check how data might be used
- Demonstrated with **Gemini**, but techniques apply to ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude
- Gen AI reduces time spent on administrative tasks, documentation, chasing updates → more time for strategic decision-making
#### Execute your meetings more efficiently with AI
- What happens after a meeting is often the most important part → gen AI makes meetings more productive and organizes the follow-through
- Scenario: generate a meeting summary and capture action items → prompt: "Summarize the following meeting notes in a succinct, two-paragraph email that identifies the key points and action items. Include a friendly opening line and warm sign-off." + paste the notes → clear summary with action items (action items are often lost among notes)
- Tips:
	- Gen AI can't read your mind → meeting notes must be complete enough; some video conferencing software offers meeting **transcription**
	- Specify the **format** to match your communication objective (short email for attendees vs a longer recap for people who missed the meeting)
#### Identify potential project risks with gen AI
- **Risks**: potential events that could occur and impact the project's outcome, with a positive or negative effect on objectives like scope, schedule, cost, or quality → can't eliminate all; be clear about them and prioritize to mitigate (→ [Course 3: Risk management](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- The project charter might list only a few risks → Gemini can generate a more detailed set
- Example: a **dog wellness app** → prompt: "I'm a project manager overseeing the development of a dog wellness app. We are a mid-size company offering dog walking, training, and pet supplies. Please review the following project details and identify only major risks that could affect the project timeline or budget." + paste project charter details (some tools like **Gemini Advanced** allow uploading documents directly)
	- Output: a large list, some risks general → the more information in the prompt, the better (add risks from previous projects or already identified risks)
- Tips: gen AI is great at **brainstorming** (what could go wrong, not just what helps); ask it to reformat output in different ways; review the list carefully with your own **judgment** (may be irrelevant or miss key areas); come back with new risks to brainstorm **mitigation plans**
#### Plan sprint retrospectives using AI
- Retrospectives give honest, timely feedback to guide the next sprint and future work (→ [Course 5: The Sprint Retrospective](/blog/agile-project-management/))
- Pro tip: Gemini's **microphone feature** → add information, start a new prompt, or iterate by voice
- Example prompt: "I am a project manager preparing to lead a one-hour retrospective for my team about the project we just completed. The project involved a community fundraising event for a local school with businesses and nonprofits from the area. Share a robust question bank that will spark participation in the meeting and guide our discussion to uncover what went well and what could be improved next time."
	- With limited time and a large group, select the most relevant questions → consider **relevance, impact, engagement, time allotted**
- Add cultural and personal aspects (thank the team, adapt the icebreaker to team culture); briefly explain the purpose of the meeting up front
- Challenge: commit to experimenting with gen AI on something time-consuming or where you need ideas
#### Use AI to create a project charter
- Like planning before building a house → the project charter (a formal document that clearly defines the project and outlines the details to reach its goals)
- Prompt: "Act as a project manager and build a project charter that includes the information below. Add placeholders for any missing information." + paste the project description, business need, deliverables, stakeholders, success criteria → a good starting point (bullets to expand, details to add)
- Tips:
	- Gen AI creates a lot of content quickly in different formats
	- Share an existing charter **template** or specify the format (number of pages or sections)
	- Read responses closely: "Gen AI may be doing the heavy lifting, but you still need to do the heavy thinking" → **Evaluate**
	- **Iterate**: refining and improving prompts and outputs through repeated cycles of testing and adjustment
- Iteration tips: give more specific guidance where output didn't meet expectations; provide **references** (examples of the format or analysis you want); check your phrasing (shorter sentences); explain what you liked (e.g. 2 of 5 brainstorm ideas) and didn't like → ask for a new list
	- For the charter: ask for less technical language, shorten or lengthen sections, or ask whether important information is missing
#### Use AI to improve project communications
- A project won't succeed without **good communication** → gen AI can generate a list of communications needed (status reports, stakeholder updates, ...)
- Prompt: "Given the project charter, which includes the following information, please share a summary in a table form of the most important communications I should prepare during the project, and indicate both the type of communication and stakeholder." + paste charter information → a detailed table to organize communications and engage the right stakeholders
- Tips:
	- Specify the output **format** (table, bullets)
	- Your company may have standard templates, methods, and norms about who receives what → you determine how to manage communication; gen AI gives a starting point
	- Review company policy before including sensitive or confidential information (e.g. names of individuals); avoid it whenever possible
- After the **stakeholder communications plan**, use gen AI to draft communications (e.g. the first draft of a status email to senior sponsors)
