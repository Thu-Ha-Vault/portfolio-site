### Understanding the basics of project management
#### What is project management?
- **Project**: a unique endeavor, usually with a set of unique deliverables; a **temporary** pursuit with a defined beginning and end → a series of tasks that need to be completed to reach a desired outcome
- **Project management**: the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to meet the project requirements and achieve the desired outcome
	- Valuable because it helps ensure a project delivers the expected outcomes **on time** and **within budget**
- Example: a school district signs a deal with a **telehealth** company (students see a doctor via tablet, smartphone, or computer at school; completed by the end of the school year) → tasks: training sessions for faculty, budgeting for tablets/computers/equipment, communication with doctors, the telehealth company, and school staff
- Poor project management → trillions of wasted dollars every year; **48%** of projects miss delivery dates, **43%** miss budget targets, **31%** fail to meet the organization's goals
- At Google, many project managers are called **program managers** (manage multiple projects for specific products, teams, or programs)
	- Emilio's team (Responsible Innovation) ensures technology, research, and publications follow Google's **AI principles**
- Core aspects: planning and organizing, managing tasks, budgeting and controlling costs
#### What does a project manager do?
- Project managers make sure the project is completed **on time and on budget** and that the outcome **brings value** to the company (e.g. creating a new service for customers or tailoring an old one to customer needs)
- Responsibilities vary by project, industry, and company (constructing a building, a renewable energy product in a startup, your own event management company)
- Typical daily responsibilities:
	- **Planning and organizing**: gathering requirements from teammates or customers (kickoff meeting, survey) → creating **project plans** (set the tone, keep everyone on pace and aligned, move tasks along)
	- **Managing tasks**: manage tasks for team members, communicate key **milestones** to the larger team or customers
	- **Budgeting and controlling costs** and other factors: a full-time job, since plans may change and cause unexpected costs
- You'll use different tools, techniques, and methodologies every day
#### Transferable project management skills
- Examples of everyday projects that use project management skills:
	- **Planning a surprise birthday party**: venue, invitations, budget based on the number of guests → planning and organizing, managing tasks, budgeting, controlling costs
	- **Retail or service industry**: managing employees' schedules → plan and organize complementary work styles, cover all duties per shift, track availability
	- **Relocating for a new job**: coordinate friends and family, budget for movers/boxes/expenses, pack and label one room at a time → managing tasks simultaneously
- These past projects are a selling point → bring them up in job interviews (e.g. "Tell me about a time when you had to juggle several tasks at once" or "...influence a customer or a teammate") to show time management and leadership

## Module 2: Becoming an effective project manager
### Exploring how a project manager adds value
#### Introduction: Becoming an effective project manager
- Module 2 goals: explain the unique value a project manager brings to their team, describe a project manager's roles and responsibilities, list their core skills → recognize skills you already have and identify new ones to learn
#### The value of a project manager
- **Project managers**: shepherd projects from start to finish and serve as guides for their team, using organizational and interpersonal skills every step of the way
- Add value through **prioritization**, **delegation**, and **effective communication** (example: renting a house and repainting the rooms)
	- **Prioritization**: help team members break large tasks into smaller steps; connect with the team and stakeholders to decide which tasks are most critical
		- **Stakeholders**: people who are interested in and affected by the project's completion and success (e.g. the leader of an organization)
		- E.g. lay out drop cloths and apply blue tape before painting; new face plates for light switches can come later or be cut if out of time or money
	- **Delegation**: match tasks to individuals who can best complete the work (e.g. a friend with professional painting experience paints the ceiling and detailed molding, scheduled before another friend paints the walls)
	- **Effective communication**: with the team and key stakeholders; being **transparent** (up front with plans and ideas, information readily available)
		- Regular check-ins with the team (enough paint and supplies?) and with people outside the team (ask the landlord for permission and share the work days)

#### How project managers impact organizations
- Main ways project managers add value: **focusing on the customer**, **building a great team**, **fostering relationships and communication**, **managing the project**, **breaking down barriers**
- **Customer**: a person or an organization that defines the requirements of the project and sets important guidelines (budget, deadlines)
	- **Internal customers**: stakeholders within your organization (management, project team members, resource managers, other departments)
	- **External customers**: outside your organization (clients, contractors, suppliers, consumers)
	- The customer is at the center of a successful project → understand their expectations
- Questions to ask the customer (with example responses):
	- What is the problem you would like us to help solve? → a new process to make the company more efficient
	- How is the problem impacting your organization? → losing clients because orders arrive late
	- What prompted you to ask for help now? → may lose department funding without improving efficiency
	- What is your hope for the outcome of this project? → fill orders faster without sacrificing quality
- **Building a great team**: the team is a project's biggest asset → understand each person's motivations, strengths, weaknesses; bring on people with the right skills (e.g. medical expertise for a medical project); show the team they are valued, trusted, appreciated (input, questions, addressing needs quickly)
- **Relationships and communication**: check in daily with the team; communicate the big picture so each person understands how their task impacts others (e.g. a graphic designer who doesn't know the customer's goal to appeal to educators can't fully capture the vision)
- **Breaking down barriers**: challenge "because we've always done it this way" → let the team innovate and share ideas, support new approaches, advocate for additional resources, reach out to other organizations yourself to keep the team on schedule

### Learning more about project manager roles and responsibilities
#### Key project manager roles and responsibilities
- Project managers follow a process of planning and organizing, managing tasks, budgeting and controlling costs and other factors → complete the project within the approved budget and timeframe
- Responsibilities you might find in a job listing:
	- **Planning and organizing**: use productivity tools and create processes to improve information sharing; create and maintain plans, timelines, schedules, and other documentation throughout the project
	- **Budgeting and controlling costs and other factors**: monitor and manage the budget, track issues and risks, manage quality by mitigating them → remove unforeseen **barriers** (things that get in the way of project progress), e.g. teammates lack resources → identify, escalate to a stakeholder, secure the resource
	- **Managing tasks**: a **project task** is an activity that needs to be accomplished within a set period of time by you, your team, or your stakeholders → manage workload, demonstrate progress to stakeholders
- Example (Emilio, program manager in Student Development at Google): pathways for students from communities underrepresented in tech → worked with two engineering teams on a technical curriculum → separate **project trackers** for each team (timeline for delivery, categories and subcategories of work, assignees) + stakeholder updates every step
#### Responsibilities that utilize interpersonal skills
- **Interpersonal skills**: the behaviors you use to interact with others, such as communication, active listening, and leadership
- Responsibilities:
	- **Teaching and mentoring**: fully explain expectations (eliminate rework, confusion, frustration); share lessons learned; support each individual to meet expectations and exceed their own potential
	- **Building relationships**: with team members, customers, clients, vendors, other stakeholders → check in, notice work styles, ask about their lives beyond the project and follow up
	- **Controlling change**: "The only thing that never changes is that everything changes" (Louis L'Amour) → stay flexible to stakeholders' needs but protect the team from constant change and rework: document initial expectations, identify requested changes, understand and communicate their budget and schedule impact
	- **Empowering your team**: let them work directly with stakeholders, delegate responsibilities, let them make some decisions, use their input → fresh ideas, passionate employees (effective mentoring often leads to more empowered teams)
	- **Communicating status and concerns**: the project manager sets the tone → open door policy, trust within the team and among stakeholders, stay positive
- These interpersonal responsibilities can be just as important as concrete ones like scheduling and budgeting
- Responsibilities that utilize interpersonal skills: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/H0hSG
#### A project manager's role within a team
- A project manager is often **not the direct manager** of the people on the project team → manages the **tasks** of a project
- Each person is an expert on their portion, but no one (including you) is an expert on every aspect (a graphic designer isn't a copywriting expert; you may not be a marketing expert)
	- Example: organizing a camping trip without camping experience → assign your partner (who camped every summer) to pick the right number and size of tents
- Your job: guide the team and make sure they have the support they need. Responsibilities from job listings:
	1. Hold all team members **accountable** for their assigned tasks (ownership of specific pieces)
	2. Ensure issues and risks are tracked and visible; establish **escalation paths** (how you will communicate risks to the right people at the right time)
	3. Help teammates adopt the right **workflows** and project management styles
	4. **Collaborate with other teams** (e.g. marketing, finance) to meet requirements based on scope, schedule, and budget
#### Working with cross-functional teams
- **Cross-functional team**: team members with different backgrounds, types of expertise, and job functions, all working towards a common goal (the successful completion of the project)
	- **T-shaped professionals**: skilled at collaborating and innovating across job functions and departments while contributing their own specific expertise
- **Clarify goals**: each member understands their role, how they support each other, and the common goals; be direct and concise; define budget, deadlines, quality requirements, important resources; encourage questions; check in continuously (members may be in different departments, locations, leadership; competing priorities)
- **Get team members with the right skills**: e.g. building an airplane with five engineers but no mechanics → missing a key element
- **Measure progress**: key milestones, completed tasks, goals met on time and within budget → document completion, communicate successes, delays, or issues
- **Recognize efforts**: some skills get more recognition (a mechanic praised for a solution vs the finance member who sourced the funding) → make sure everyone's value is recognized; learn what makes members feel supported, give and take feedback, be mindful of background, personal identifiers, and work style
- Working with cross-functional teams: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/jrw3c

### Acquiring the core skills of a successful project manager
#### The core skills of a project manager
- Four core skill sets:
	- **Enabling decision-making**: gather information and insights from teammates (or decisions from the appropriate leader) to help the team make informed decisions and keep the project on schedule; communicate decisions to the immediate team or company leaders (e.g. provide relevant data or feedback to choose between choice A and choice B)
	- **Communicating and escalating**: documenting plans, sending status emails, holding meetings to escalate risks or issues to stakeholders
	- **Flexibility**: plans will change (company goals change, a team member takes a job elsewhere) → "The only constant is change"; stay cool under pressure and help the team stay calm
	- **Strong organizational skills**: organize processes and the core elements of a project so nothing gets lost or overlooked (e.g. track daily tasks in a spreadsheet, frequent status updates or reminders)
- Build on these with industry knowledge: tools and templates, popular styles like **Waterfall** and **Agile**
#### Key competencies: Flexibility and handling ambiguity
- **Enabling decision-making**: make the decision-making process collaborative (state the goals of deliverables, elicit input on how to achieve them) → responsibility, accountability, team closeness
- **Communicating and escalating**: when escalating, approach management with both the problem and potential solutions → shows initiative
- **Strong organizational skills**: organizational tools
	- Planning and scheduling software (templates, workflows, calendars)
	- Collaboration tools (email, collaboration software, dashboards)
	- Documentation (files, plans, spreadsheets)
	- Quality assurance tools (evaluations, productivity trackers, reports)
- **Flexible planning** strategies:
	- **Assess external constraints**: national holidays, team member vacations and sick leave → leave extra time in the schedule
	- **Plan for risks and challenges**: e.g. someone gets sick or quits → replace within the company or hire an independent contractor; list of possible backups; review **historical data** from past projects
	- **Calculate "float" in your schedule**: **float** (or **slack**) is the amount of time you can wait to begin a task before it impacts the project schedule and threatens the project outcome (covered later with the critical path)
- **Handling ambiguity** (in goals, requirements, schedules, vision):
	- **Keep calm**: grace and poise inspire the team
	- **Express empathy**: understand what the team thinks and feels, show support
	- **Communicate what you know clearly**: define what is confirmed and will not change
	- **Make decisions and stick to them**: don't second-guess in front of the team; explain why if you change course
	- **Trust the expertise of your team**: discuss what each knows or believes to be true, then what is unknown and how to gather more information

## Module 3: The project management life cycle and methodologies
### Understanding the project life cycle
#### Introduction: The project management life cycle and methodologies
- Module 3 goals: explain and follow the **life cycle** of a project, define a project's phases and each phase's tasks, compare methodologies (the two most popular: **Waterfall** and **Agile**) to choose the most effective one, organize how a project is run according to different methodologies
#### Exploring the phases of the project life cycle
- No two projects are the same (e.g. a political campaign for a local candidate: resources, people, election date, location) → understand the basic structure: the **project life cycle**
- Four major phases:
	1. **Initiate the project**: the launchpad → define goals and deliverables, identify budget, resources, people involved, other details → document it in one place to showcase the project's value and get approval
	2. **Make a plan**: 100% essential for every project (you can't hire a contractor to build a house without a plan) → budget, breakdown of tasks, team roles and responsibilities, schedule, resources, what to do if problems or changes arise
	3. **Execute and complete tasks**: the project team completes the tasks; the project manager **monitors progress**, keeps the team motivated, removes obstacles
	4. **Close the project**: celebrate the team's work, evaluate what worked and what didn't, share results with people outside the team who were interested
- Different finish lines:
	- Some have a firm end date (the campaign)
	- Others end with a **handoff** (a new restaurant ordering system → hand over to a support group once set up and employees are trained; Emilio's stakeholder **dashboard** → updates transitioned to the respective teams) → like turning over the keys of a newly built house to its owner
- Phase names may vary by project type or organization, but the general idea stays the same
#### Case study: The significance of each project phase
- **The consequences of rushing**: Jason (project manager at a virtual reality software company) gets an urgent Friday-night call from his manager Mateo → a cost and timeline for a VR training program for **Flight Simulators, Inc.** (aircraft maintenance) by the end of the weekend
	- He quickly sends a standard proposal: **$200,000**, **six weeks**
	- Monday: Mateo is reprimanded for not following the company's proposal process or including the engineers → the client's software won't work with their platform: six months to develop + six months to test, over **a million dollars** → the project failed before it started
- **What should have happened**: Jason asks for more time to get the proposal right → the client appreciates the communication and gives him a week → involve all key players in estimating cost, schedule, resources
	- **Initiating**: clarify project goals with the client, gather stakeholders and team members, identify skill sets, timeline, cost, document the project's value → leadership approves → client accepts
	- **Making a plan**: important deadlines and tasks, a schedule for all resources, materials, and tasks
	- **Executing and completing tasks**: the team executes; Jason monitors, breaks down barriers, communicates schedule and quality expectations, updates the client and gathers feedback
	- **Closing**: deliver the training, document **lessons learned** (what worked well, what could work better), a lunch gathering to celebrate the team
- Make sure leadership understands the risk of not properly preparing; incorrect assumptions put the company at risk

### Analyzing the different project phases
#### Phases in action: Initiating and planning
- Phase names or tasks may differ by project type or organization (Google uses a mix of methods), but projects share many of the same tasks
- **Initiate the project**: organize all available information to prepare for planning
	- Define **project goals** and **deliverables** (specific tasks or outcomes), e.g. a political campaign → raise **$5,000** or get **500 signatures**
	- Research ideas to meet the goals; find out what **resources** are available: people, equipment, software programs, vendors, physical space or locations (anything you need to complete the project)
	- Record the details in a **project proposal** → approval by a decision maker or group of decision makers (sometimes you are the decision maker)
- **Make a plan**: create a budget, set the schedule, establish the team and each person's roles and responsibilities
	- Deliberate planning is critical; plan for **risk and change**: scheduling delays, budget changes, technology and software requirements, legal issues, quality control, access to resources
	- Communicate the plan to the team (who owns which tasks, what to do with questions or problems) and to others interested in the project's success
#### Phases in action: Executing and closing
- **Execute and complete tasks**: your job is to manage the progress of the project as a whole, not do all the tasks
	- Make sure everyone knows what's expected, which tasks to do, how and when; remove obstacles; alert the right people about possible delays
	- Communicate through meetings, written communications (memos, emails, internal chat tools), working documents (task reports) → **if in doubt, err on the side of overcommunication**
	- Adjust the schedule, budget, and allocation of resources as the project progresses
- **Close the project** (often overlooked):
	1. Check all tasks are completed (incl. work added along the way); outstanding invoices paid, resources returned and accounted for, documentation submitted
	2. Get **confirmation** that the final outcome is acceptable to the people you're delivering it to
	3. **Retrospective**: reflect on what went well and what didn't → best practices, manage more effectively next time; also valuable to the people receiving the result
	4. Collect all project documentation and share final results with **stakeholders** (people interested in and affected by the project's completion and success: management, clients or customers, users of a new tool or process, the community at large) → "the VIPs of your project"
	5. **Celebrate** the team's effort (a team-wide email thanking individuals; a company party for big projects)
	6. Formally move on to pursue new projects
- Like a coach of a sports team: not playing directly, but guidance, communication, and team-building make a high-performing, successful team
#### Summary of the project phases
- The **project life cycle**: the path for your project from start to finish; each phase builds toward the next
- **Initiate the project** – questions to set the foundation:
	- Who are the stakeholders? What are the client's or customer's goals?
	- What is the purpose and mission? What are the measurable objectives for the team? What is the project trying to improve?
	- When does it need to be completed? What skills and resources will it require? What will it cost, and what are the benefits?
- **Make a plan**: a detailed project plan (major **milestones** and the tasks or deliverables in each) + a schedule to manage resources, budget, materials, timeline + an **itemized budget**
- **Execute the project**: monitor the team, break down barriers, keep the team aware of schedule and deliverable expectations, address process weaknesses or training needs, adapt to changes
- **Close the project**: confirm all requested outcomes are complete, release the team to other projects, celebrate, pass off remaining deliverables and get stakeholder approval, document lessons learned, reflect on improvements
- Following the life cycle ensures you capture customer expectations, set up for success with a plan, execute and address issues, and capture lessons learned

### Comparing project management methodologies and approaches
#### Introduction to project management methodologies
- **Project management methodology**: a set of guiding principles and processes for owning a project through its life cycle → steps to take, tasks to complete, principles for managing the project
- Two types:
	- **Linear**: the previous phase or task has to be completed before the next can start
		- E.g. building a house: blueprint → foundation → walls → roof → a bungalow-style home; a clear goal, changes in the middle are unlikely (and too late once the foundation is laid)
		- No need for many changes during development, a clear sequential process → stick to the plan to finish on schedule
	- **Iterative**: more flexible; some phases and tasks overlap or happen at the same time
		- E.g. producing a new TV show: film a pilot → test in different locations and time slots → adjust based on feedback while hiring permanent actors, starting production, and advertising; the show may change (a one-hour show becomes a half-hour show, a popular supporting character becomes a main character)
		- Anticipates changes; test parts before the final result; deliver parts as they are completed
- Google takes a **hybrid** approach: mix and match methods by project type; project managers adapt their own style to the project and team
#### Overview of Waterfall and Agile
- **Waterfall**: created in the 1970s; the sequential ordering of phases, completed one at a time like a waterfall (a **linear** approach)
	- First used in physical engineering (manufacturing, construction), then software engineering; still used for product feature design and app design; adapted by event planning and retail
	- Many styles, all following an ordered set of steps linked to clearly defined expectations, resources, and goals that are not likely to change
	- Phases follow the standard life cycle: initiating, planning, executing (managing and completing tasks), closing
	- Use when phases are clearly defined, tasks must be completed before others can begin, or changes are very expensive once started
	- E.g. catering an event on a tight budget: confirm the number of guests → define the menu → get approval on items and costs → order unreturnable ingredients → reserve tables, chairs, dishes; no menu changes once ordered
	- Has risk management practices to deal with changes, but plans rarely go according to plan
- **Agile**: able to move quickly and easily; **flexibility** (willing and able to change and adapt)
	- Many tasks worked on at the same time or in various stages → an **iterative** approach
	- Concepts emerged in the 1990s (demand for faster delivery of software), officially named Agile in **2001**
	- Phases follow the life cycle generally, but overlap; tasks are completed in iterations, called **sprints** in **Scrum** (a form of Agile): short chunks of time, usually **one to four weeks**, focused on completing specific tasks
	- Agile is more of a **mindset** than a series of steps: an effective, collaborative team seeking regular client feedback to deliver the best value quickly and adjust as changes emerge
	- Best for: the client has an idea but no concrete picture, or cares about qualities more than exact appearance; high uncertainty and risk
	- E.g. building a website: build and deliver parts in sprints (launch the homepage while the blog or online appointment booking are built over time) → early feedback, adjustments, less wasted effort; Waterfall would require the whole website to be complete before launch
- Waterfall and Agile are common, but not the only or the best methodologies; Google selects from many
#### Comparing Waterfall and Agile approaches
- Waterfall is also commonly called **traditional**; projects may use aspects of each, and in practice the differences are not always clearly defined

| | Waterfall | Agile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Project manager's role | Active leader: prioritizes and assigns tasks to team members | **Scrum Master** acts primarily as a facilitator, removing barriers; the team shares more responsibility in managing its own work |
| Scope | Deliverables and plans well-established and documented early (initiating, planning); changes go through a formal **change request** process | Planning in shorter iterations, focused on delivering value quickly; later iterations adjusted in response to feedback or unforeseen issues |
| Schedule | A mostly linear path through initiating, planning, executing, closing | Time organized into **Sprints**, each with a defined duration and a set list of deliverables planned at the start |
| Cost | Kept under control by careful estimation up front and close monitoring | Costs and schedule could change with each iteration |
| Quality | Project manager defines criteria to measure quality at the beginning | Ongoing stakeholder input and user feedback by testing products in the field and regularly implementing improvements |
| Communication | Project manager continually communicates progress toward milestones and key indicators to stakeholders | Customer-focused team, consistent communication between users and the project team |
| Stakeholders | Project manager continually manages and monitors stakeholder engagement | Team frequently provides deliverables to stakeholders; progress depends on stakeholder feedback |

- Comparing Waterfall and Agile approaches: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/qNbkq
#### Introduction to Lean and Six Sigma
- **Lean Six Sigma**: a combination of two parent methodologies, **Lean** and **Six Sigma** → common in projects that aim to save money, improve quality, and move through processes quickly; also focuses on team collaboration (a valued team is more motivated and productive)
- Five phases: **DMAIC** (**define, measure, analyze, improve, control**) → a strategy for **process improvement**; can be used to solve any business problem
	- Example: a large travel company with customer service wait times surging after a sales promotion
	1. **Define**: the project goal and what it will take to meet it (similar to the initiation phase) → bring average wait times down from **30 minutes** to **less than 10 minutes**; talk to stakeholders about expectations
	2. **Measure**: how the current process is performing (DMAIC focuses on **data**) → map the current process, locate problems; company data like average wait times, customers per day, seasonal variations; plan how and how often to measure (weekly, monthly, quarterly reports; surveys; inventory, shipping, tracking records)
	3. **Analyze**: identify gaps and issues → e.g. inadequate staffing on the busiest days
	4. **Improve**: only after careful analysis; present findings and make improvements → e.g. modify staffing
	5. **Control**: put new processes and documentation in place and keep monitoring so the company doesn't revert to the old way
	- Remember: defining tells you what to measure, measuring tells you what to analyze, analyzing tells you what to improve, improving tells you what to control
- Ideal when the goal is improving the current process to fix complex or high-risk problems (improving sales, conversions, eliminating a **bottleneck**: when things get backed up during a process)
- At Google, approaches are combined:
	- An engineering team uses **Agile** to create a customer-focused product, with aspects of **Waterfall** for planning and documentation
	- A customer service team uses **Lean Six Sigma** to improve an experience (new features based on analysis) but develops and rolls out code with Agile iterations and sprints
	- An internal education team uses only **Waterfall** for annual compliance training (fixed requirements, deadline, and goal)
#### Lean and Six Sigma methodologies
- **Lean** (also **Lean Manufacturing**): originated in manufacturing; main principle = **removal of waste** within an operation → only value is added at each phase
	- Eight types of waste: **defects, excess processing, overproduction, waiting, inventory, transportation, motion, non-utilized talent**
	- Often caused by: lack of proper documentation, lack of process standards, not understanding customers' needs, lack of effective communication, lack of process control, inefficient process design, failures of management
	- Use Lean when you want to use limited resources, reduce waste, and streamline processes
	- **5S** quality tool (five pillars of good housekeeping):
		1. **Sort**: remove all items not needed for current operations, leave only the bare essentials
		2. **Set in order**: arrange needed items so they're easy to use; label them
		3. **Shine**: keep everything in the correct place; clean your workspace every day
		4. **Standardize**: perform the process the same way every time
		5. **Sustain**: make a habit of maintaining correct procedures, instill the discipline in your team
	- **Kanban** scheduling system (**Kanban board**): a visualization tool to optimize the flow of the team's work; cards moved from left to right to show progress
- **Six Sigma**: reduce **variations** by ensuring quality processes are followed every time; from statistics → items or processes should have **99.9996%** quality
	- Seven key principles: always focus on the customer; understand how work really gets done; make processes flow smoothly; reduce waste and concentrate on value; stop defects by removing variation; involve and collaborate with your team; approach improvement systematically
	- Find measurable aspects (time, cost, quantity) → inspect → reject products that don't meet the standard → improve the process that created them
- **Lean Six Sigma**: Lean tools (Kanban boards, 5S) build quality into processes from the beginning; products are then inspected or tested against Six Sigma standards
	- Difference: Lean **streamlines processes**; Six Sigma **reduces variation** in products by building in quality and inspecting against standards
- Lean and Six Sigma methodologies: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/djsGX
#### Common project management approaches and how to select one
- **Waterfall**: traditional, linear and sequential; each stage completed before the next; the project manager prioritizes and assigns tasks; quality criteria defined at the beginning
- **Agile**: short phases of collaborative, iterative work with frequent testing and regular improvements; some phases overlap; teams share responsibility for managing their own work
	- **Agile frameworks**: specific development approaches based on the Agile philosophy (e.g. Scrum, Kanban)
- **Scrum**: an Agile framework for developing, delivering, and sustaining complex projects and products through collaboration, accountability, and an iterative process → small, cross-functional teams led by a **Scrum Master**; work divided into short **Sprints** with a set list of deliverables
- **Kanban**: a tool used in both Agile and Lean → visual feedback on work in progress with Kanban boards or charts (sticky notes or cards in categories like "To do", "In progress", "Done")
- **Lean**: uses the **5S** quality tool to eliminate eight areas of waste, save money, improve quality, streamline processes ("do more with less"); a Kanban scheduling system to manage production
- **Six Sigma**: reduce variations so quality processes are followed every time → follows **DMAIC**
- **Lean Six Sigma**: save money, improve quality, move through processes quickly; ideal for complex or high-risk problems → 5S, DMAIC, Kanban boards
- All methodologies require communication and collaboration and aim to deliver on time and within budget
- Selecting an approach: varies by project and organization; Google often uses a **hybrid** of approaches and frameworks; choosing takes time and practice
- Common project management approaches and how to select one: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/MCfxO

- Waterfall, Agile, and Lean Six Sigma are solid foundations, each with its own rules, values, and processes → no right or wrong methodology, no one-size-fits-all; blend approaches to fit the project; goal: maximize the use of resources and time
## Module 4: Organizational structure and culture
### Understanding organizational structure
#### Introduction: Organizational structure and culture
- Module 4: organizational structure and culture and how they impact setting up and executing a project; common types of structures
- **Change management**: the way you present the final project to the organization and get them to accept and implement your project outcome → ensures the project is adopted and lives on
#### Overview of Classic and Matrix structures
- **Organizational structure**: the way a company or organization is arranged → how job tasks are divided and coordinated, how members relate to one another (who reports to whom) → a map of where you fit in, who to communicate with, and how often
- **Reporting chart** / **org chart** (organizational chart): shows relationships between people and groups and who reports to whom
- **Classic** structure (usually called **functional** or **top-down**): a typical chain of command → CEO and executives at the top → directors or managers → their direct reports; each director or manager oversees teams within a function (marketing, sales, human resources)
	- E.g. the Army: private → sergeant → lieutenant → ...
	- As a project manager: communicate regularly with your manager and peers working on the same types of projects
- **Matrix** structure: project teams sit across different functions → a grid with people above you plus people in adjacent departments who expect updates on your work (not your direct bosses, but they may inform changes to your work)
	- E.g. Google: major functions (marketing, sales) with traditional reporting chains + programs for products like **Google Search** with program managers, engineers, UX designers, each reporting to their own management chain
	- E.g. Emilio's organization, **Global Affairs**: a direct manager (core role, performance reviews) + informally working with lead program managers in other organizations (updates, approval, feedback)
- In an interview, ask about the organization's structure and where your role fits in
#### A project manager's role within different organizational structures
- Understanding Classic vs Matrix structures helps you ask the right questions in a job interview and know which skills matter most for the role
- Once hired: identify key points of communication and key **stakeholders**, navigate the organization for support, know who has authority
- **Classic organizational structures**: a top-down hierarchy → CEO has direct authority over department managers → managers over sections of employees
	- Communication goes both up and down the ladder; authority comes from the top and filters down; frequent status updates may need to pass up through management levels
	- Also called **functional organizations**: divided into departments by function, each led by a **functional manager**; employees grouped by the function of their role and usually have a specialty
	- E.g. **Friendly Skies Airlines**: main function is flying airplanes; departments such as Marketing, Human Resources, Strategy
- **Managing a project in a Classic organization**
	- E.g. the Board approves retrofitting airplanes to carry more passengers → CEO sponsors a project team → led by a project manager from Engineering and Innovation, with representatives from Finance, Marketing, Strategy, Operations and Design
	- The project team stays in their reporting lines but acts as its own assembled team → the project builds from existing departments
	- As project manager: consult functional managers about resources and each teammate's capacity, learn each function's internal processes and approval structure
	- Authority may be slightly limited (competing priorities, approval chains) → set expectations up front
- **Matrix organizational structures**: employees have **two or more managers**; you still have people above you plus people in adjacent departments you update on progress
	- Functional areas cross paths more often; depending on the work, the responsible manager for each area has the most authority
	- A team has at least two chains of command → the project manager is like a **temporary manager** while assigned; the functional manager stays the same regardless of the project
- **Managing a project in a Matrix organization**
	- E.g. Friendly Skies' **Product Excellence** team develops a new amenity kit for long-haul flights → the project manager works on their behalf, in partnership with the Marketing team, to create marketing materials presenting research data
	- Further reading: PMI article on Matrix organizations
- **Key takeaway**: in both structures project managers must clearly define **roles and responsibilities**; in most Matrix organizations some project managers or department leads may have the same authority as functional managers and operate more directly
- A project manager's role within different organizational structures: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/kj5om
#### How organizational structure impacts project management
- An organization's structure provides the framework for **accountability** and **communication** → know who you report to on each project and who your team members report to; how and where to get resources
- Two ways structure impacts project management:
	- **Authority**: your ability to make decisions for the project that impact the organization (e.g. selecting vendors vs having vendors selected for you); varies from project to project
	- **Resource availability**: access to the people, equipment, and budget you need
- **Classic structure**: less authority and a tighter scope
	- Rely on approval from managers, directors, department heads, who are likely in charge of your team members and resources
	- The number of people and the budget allocated are decided by the leaders of your department or function → go through a **chain of approvals** and advocate for more resources
	- E.g. need a budget increase → report to your manager → manager escalates up their management chain for approval
- **Matrix structure**: employees often have two or more managers (their functional manager + you, the project manager; more if working on multiple projects)
	- Affects your authority → cooperate with more than one leader, share resources, negotiate priorities
	- Chain of command isn't always clearly defined → identify stakeholders and who controls what, communicate with anyone you need to report to or get approval from **before the project begins**
	- Once established the project runs efficiently; strong project focus; the project manager generally has **more autonomy** to make decisions and gather resources
#### The role of a Project Management Office
- **Project Management Office (PMO)**: a group within an organization that defines, sets, and helps maintain project management standards and processes throughout the organization → often a coordinated center for all of the organization's projects
	- Project managers may operate within the PMO or within other departments (e.g. at Google: a PMO focused on operational excellence + many project and program managers in other departments)
- PMOs offer guidance and support: share best practices, project statuses, direction; often take on strategic projects themselves
- Main functions of a PMO:
	- **Strategic planning and governance** (the most important): define project criteria, select projects according to business goals, provide a **business case** to management
	- **Best practices**: implement best practices and processes, share lessons learned; ensure consistency through guidance about processes, tools, metrics
	- **Common project culture**: train employees about optimal approaches and best practices → consistent and efficient practices across the organization
	- **Resource management**: manage and allocate resources (people, equipment) across projects based on budget, priorities, schedules; define roles and responsibilities; provide training, mentoring, coaching (especially to project managers)
	- **Creation of project documentation, archives, and tools**: templates, tools, software; maintain project history → archive all documents when a project closes for future reference and lessons learned
- Key takeaway: PMOs help manage large numbers of projects and keep all employees working toward the organization's goals
- The role of a Project Management Office: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/9yNqM
#### Lan: Working in a Project Management Office
- Lan: director of program management at Google, on the PMO team for **Geo** (geospatial products like Google Maps and Google Earth; her team works on the data and imagery powering them)
- A PMO is a team of project managers that coordinates all parts of a project (product, engineers, business functions) to bring ideas to life
	- Her role: make sure all the pieces of a project that need to connect actually connect → project managers get a **bird's eye view** of everything happening in a project
- Career path: a piece of a project → full end-to-end smaller projects → large complex projects
	- Smaller scope: go deep into technologies, build deep relationships with teams; large projects: see all the gears turning in a large ecosystem
- Benefits of working in a PMO: share best practices, challenges, templates and tools with other project managers; a community that thinks about problems in a methodical, task-, action- and goal-oriented way
### Understanding the impact of organizational culture
#### Introduction to organizational culture
- Culture: languages, food, clothing, plus less obvious but just as impactful parts → beliefs, traditions, customs
- An organization's culture provides context and guides what its people value, how they operate daily, relate to one another, and are expected to perform
- **Organizational culture**: in part the values employees share + the organization's values, mission, history, ... → the **company's personality**
	- Helps you navigate your team toward the project's goal, impacts how you plan the project, minimizes conflict
- **Mission and values** provide clues to culture → show how the project supports the mission or aligns with values → more support from executives and stakeholders for approvals and resources
	- Watch what leaders value: speed over perfection? How are decisions made? Do they examine every option?
	- Stuck on a decision or unsure how to communicate → review the mission and values
	- E.g. values stability and user feedback → expand the timeline for testing and decide based on results; values innovation and revenue growth → shorter timeline, take some risks with new ideas
- Get a sense of the culture before the first phase of the project; ask about culture in interviews (also shows you know its impact on projects)
- Questions to gauge culture:
	- How do people prefer to communicate? (scheduled meetings, email, phone)
	- How are decisions made? (majority vote or top-down approvals)
	- What **rituals** are in place for newcomers? (lunch, building tour, introductions)
	- How are projects typically run? (Classic, Matrix, other)
	- What practices, behaviors, values are reflected? (overtime or weekend work expected? company-sanctioned social events?)
- Navigating company culture once the project starts:
	- **Ask questions**: ask peers what is going well and what they would change → assess risk, adjust the project, prepare for future projects
	- **Make observations**: how things work, what people like and respect; in different geographies be aware of customs (bowing, shaking hands, wearing head coverings)
	- **Understand your impact**: be aware of your role as a **change agent** → someone who helps the organization transform by focusing on improving organizational effectiveness and development
		- The project (or even the presence of a project manager) may change the environment or dynamics → for major changes, be mindful of how extreme they are, seek feedback and approval early; recognize the limits of changes that don't fit the mission, vision, or culture
- Structure usually influences culture → consider both when planning and carrying out the project
#### Learning about an organization's culture
- Peter Drucker: "Culture eats strategy for breakfast" → culture always influences a company's success regardless of its business model
- The importance of organizational culture:
	- **Identity**: the way the company conducts business internally and externally; values are part of identity (culture ≈ personality) → learn the **mission and value statements** (why the company exists, what it believes in, how it behaves)
	- **People**: a strong, positive culture retains and attracts the best employees (valued, engaged, challenged); a toxic culture has the opposite effect → find a culture that fits your personality; talk to employees, note their attire, expressions, behavior
	- **Processes**: culture directly impacts processes and productivity; e.g. a company valuing feedback and employee involvement includes many opportunities for employees to comment
- Aspects of culture directly related to managing projects: communication, decision-making, rituals, previous management styles, values
- How to learn about a company's culture:
	- **Ask questions** (of management and peers, ideally in the interview phase):
		- **Atmosphere**: dress code; how credit is shared; is risk-taking encouraged and what happens when people fail; how managers support and motivate; how this role interacts with customers and users; when and how team members give feedback; workplace traditions; how success is celebrated
		- **Policies**: sick days and vacation; employee flexibility (working from home, flexible hours); policies supporting employees sharing their identity in the workplace
		- **Processes**: onboarding process; how employees measure the impact of their work
		- **Values**: mission and value statements; how this role contributes to the mission; support for professional development and career growth
	- **Listen to people's stories**: experiences with similar past projects; what they can tell you about key stakeholders and customers
	- **Take note of company rituals**: powerful drivers of culture, instill shared purpose (how birthdays and holidays are celebrated, lunch at the same time and place?)
		- Watch employee interactions → formal or informal? ideas solicited from employees in different roles? → tailor your interaction style to the company norm
	- **Understand your impact**: as a project manager you become a **change agent** → sit down with management to understand what is expected of you
	- **Sharpen your communication skills**: how a company communicates is tied to its culture → learn to navigate different channels; ask about practices (sign emails from the team or individually? presentations with team members or only the project manager?)
- Approaching projects differently from the past may meet resistance → showing appreciation of the culture helps team members accept improvements
- Key takeaway: navigating departmental interactions, communicating effectively, planning in line with the culture → success in your project management career
- Learning about an organization's culture: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/dNW9X
#### Amar: Project management in life and in the organization
- Amar: Senior Engineering Program Manager at Google Shopping; drives programs spanning multiple products
- "Life is program management": having kids, building a house, buying a car → everything has a start and end
	- Tremendous **friction** in every system (decision-making, budgeting), in software or construction: are the right people connected, the right decision-makers in the meeting, the right approvals in a timely manner? → inefficiencies add risk or expose risk late in the development cycle
	- His job: **bringing order into chaos**
- Mentor's advice: every day ask "what are the top three challenges I want to solve today?" (big, impactful ones)
	- Every Monday morning: strategic meeting on what success looks like this week → helps the team understand how success is defined
#### Case study: Balancing company culture and strategic goals
- When culture aligns with corporate strategy and goals, performance is impressive; understanding culture helps you decide if a company fits you, and when to fit within the culture or intentionally push back to create improvements
- **The Family Java** culture: coffeehouse with over 2,000 stores worldwide; culture closely linked to strategy and capabilities → relationship-driven, **employees-first** approach → warm, comfortable, calm environment for employees and customers
	- **Mission**: to provide a welcoming environment where our employees become our family and our guests become our friends
	- **Values**: a place where everyone is welcome; always give our best and hold ourselves accountable for the results; treat others with respect and kindness
	- Spends more on employee healthcare than on coffee beans; substantial training, education scholarships, daycare assistance, growth within the company
	- Focuses on positive attributes and adapts to what works → drives out negativity, empowers employees, aligns with strategic goals
- A project manager's relationship to organizational culture (Avi, new project manager at The Family Java):
	- **Learning the company's values**: asked about culture in the interview; his previous company prioritized profitability over teamwork and mentorship → hard to engage; The Family Java's people-first approach aligned with his values
	- **Clarifying the company's expectations**: role emphasizes team building and keeping morale high → asked his manager about the expected time investment and past practices; with wrong assumptions he might have emphasized speed over collaboration → balance culture expectations with the project workload
	- **Applying organizational culture to a project**: team lunch → one-on-one meetings about working style, professional goals, how to support and remove barriers
		- Miguel starts early and picks his children up at 3:00 → no late-afternoon team meetings
		- Elisa prefers face-to-face or phone over email → talk in person when possible
		- Regular check-ins + weekly "Coffee Chats" (a company tradition) → noticed by executives and stakeholders, given a lot of support for resources
- Key takeaway: each organization's culture is different and can change over time → learning it directly relates to project success

### Understanding change management
#### Introduction to change management
- Sometimes the project's deliverable is a new tool or process that must be adopted by the organization
- **Change management**: the process of delivering your completed project and getting people to adopt it → ensures the organization accepts and adopts the recommendations from the project
	- E.g. a new time tracking system for employees isn't successful if employees don't adopt it
	- **Adoption** is often the first step to the project having the desired impact once it goes live
- A new project manager may not plan all the change management, but can help by understanding their role and how the organization may react
- The people in an organization are directly impacted by changes: processes, budgets, schedules, roles and responsibilities; even aesthetic changes (new wing, renovated lobby, new company logo → swap out all the old stationery)
- Many change management models and strategies exist, but they share three core concepts and best practices:
	1. **Ownership and urgency**: ownership = others feel empowered to take responsibility for the successful completion of their tasks; urgency = they understand the project is important and identify what actions move it along → increases interest, motivation, engagement
	2. **The right combination of skills and personalities** when selecting team members → people whose knowledge and skills complement one another
		- If the team is selected for you → choose who gets which tasks; if not possible → connect with your team so they become **advocates for change**
		- Motivate by clearly communicating your vision and approach and how everyone works together → others share the vision and take ownership
	3. **Effective communication** ("communication is key"): be transparent and up front, make information available, keep the team and the organization up to date → everyone feels included
- After completion you may meet resistance or roadblocks → change doesn't happen overnight; help people adjust, reward their efforts, remind them of the long-term value
- Many principles of **Agile** align with successful change management → being receptive to change is a core Agile value; Agile teams are constantly evolving and adapting
#### A project manager's role in change management
- Project success depends on adoption and acceptance (a new external tool or a process changing operations at a production facility) → the greatest impact is on the people who use the product or process
	- E.g. a new website user interface → impacts users (learn the new navigation); a new brand logo → impacts employees (all company communications must use the new logo)
- Embrace changes and convince the wider audience (end users or members of the organization) to embrace them → a careful approach addresses issues in later stages
- **Integrating project management and change management**: change management is a project in itself; you may not lead the end-to-end process (your manager, a team member, or a senior leader might), but you can support and participate
	- Both aim to increase the likelihood of project success with tools and processes → integrating them is the project manager's responsibility
- Questions to ask yourself:
	- How will the organization react to change?
	- Which influencers can affect change?
	- What are the best means of communication?
	- What change management practices will lead to the successful implementation of my project?
- Best practices:
	- **Be proactive**: proactive, inclusive planning keeps potentially impacted stakeholders aware of upcoming changes
	- **Incorporate change management into your project management steps**: time in team meetings or a feedback document for suggestions and concerns; plan steps near the end to introduce the deliverable (demonstrations, Q&A forums, marketing videos) → less impact on the timeline; if not built into the plan, escalate the importance of a change management plan to stakeholders
	- **Communicate about upcoming changes**: regularly among impacted stakeholders, the change management team, and the project team → how changes provide a better experience for end users
	- **Follow a consistent process**: establish and document the change management process early; the organization may have an overarching plan (when to promote the change, when training occurs, when the launch or release happens, steps for each phase)
	- **Practice empathy**: changes are inevitable but often resisted → be empathetic to the challenges and anxiety change brings
	- **Use tools**: feedback mechanisms (surveys) to capture stakeholder input; **flowcharts** to visualize the development process; **culture mapping** to illustrate the culture and how values, norms, and employee behavior may be affected by the change
- Further resources: Change Management at the Project Level; Change Management Framework (template)
- A project manager's role in change management: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-management-foundations/supplement/fHd1o
#### Participating in change management
- If you are **participating** in change management, someone else is responsible for successfully implementing the changes (a team member, your manager, a senior leader)
	- E.g. you completed a new check-in system but lack the formal authority to get employees to use it → you can still participate: hand off the project, stay involved, communicate with employees throughout about how the system gives customers a better experience → they feel prepared to adjust
- Combine knowledge of structure and culture → same pharmacy check-in project, name change from "Speedy Care" to "Speedy Check-in":
	- **Classic structure + informal culture**: participate by communicating needs through the appropriate channels
		- The CEO emails the **C-suite** (all chief-level officers) to inform their teams to implement the name change
		- Budgets managed separately → marketing requests an extra $10,000 for new printed store posters → request goes up to the **Chief Financial Officer (CFO)**
		- Other cost adjustments and process changes → multiple meetings to help others understand what is changing and why
	- **Matrix structure + formal culture**:
		- Meet representatives from marketing and technology about the overall budget impact
		- Write a document describing all budget, timeline, and training impacts → circulate for feedback and alignment from stakeholders → share with the CEO (full scope; agreement or resistance?)
		- The CEO has someone write a formal memo explaining why the name change is needed and shares it with staff and their teams
- Every organization's structure, culture, and processes differ → use the same questions (how will the organization react? which influencers? best means of communication? which practices?) to prepare for scenarios and support adoption
#### Corporate and project governance
- **Governance**: the management framework within which decisions are made and accountability and responsibility are determined → "who is in charge"; may come up in interviews
- **Corporate governance**: an organization's own standards and practices that direct and control its actions → the framework by which it achieves goals and objectives; balances requirements of stakeholders, management, customers; affects action plans, internal and external controls, performance measurements
	- Governance and change management go hand in hand → effective governance provides clearly defined roles and responsibilities during change (who makes decisions; relationship between those managing and participating in change)
	- **Steering committees**: decide the organization's priorities and manage the general course of operations → an advisory board that helps the project manager and company make and approve strategic decisions
- **Project governance**: the framework for how project decisions are made → keeps projects running smoothly, on time, within budget; covers policies, regulations, functions, processes, procedures, responsibilities (what activities are done and who is responsible)
- How project and corporate governance intersect: project governance is tailored to the organization's needs and concerns the areas of corporate governance related to project activities → projects aligned to larger objectives, delivered efficiently, sustainable:
	- Consider long- and short-term interests of the organization
	- Make thoughtful decisions about which projects to take on; avoid projects without sufficient resources
	- Provide timely, relevant, reliable information to the board of directors and major stakeholders
	- Elicit input and buy-in of senior managers (the decision-makers)
	- **Initiation**: prioritize clear, reachable, sustainable goals to reduce confusion and conflict
	- **Planning**: assign ownership and accountability to an experienced team to deliver, monitor, control
	- **Execution**: learn from mistakes and adapt to new or improved knowledge
- Corporate governance can mean clearing many hurdles before decisions (which can affect the whole corporation), but also supports project governance: oversight on compliance and risk mitigation, guidance for project managers, securing resources, addressing issues, avoiding decision delays, stakeholder buy-in, executive-level visibility
- Key takeaway: consider governance processes like culture and structure when interviewing and starting a role → how decisions are made, who is responsible for what, potential issues
