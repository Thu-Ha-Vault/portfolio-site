### Understanding the key components of project initiation
#### Why is project initiation essential?
- **Initiation** is the first phase of the project life cycle (→ planning, executing, closing), regardless of methodology → a well-planned initiation results in a strong foundation and sets the project up for success
- Initiation begins after a **problem or opportunity** has been identified within an organization; often stakeholders like senior leaders initiate a project to address a specific business need (e.g. roll out a new product, improve employee well-being, reduce costs in a department)
- The project manager's responsibility: identify the project goals, resources, and other details based on initial discussions with stakeholders → figure out all the pieces needed to get the work done, even if someone else came up with the idea
	- Ask stakeholders the right questions, perform research, determine resources, clearly document the key components → solidify the **scope** (the boundary) of the project
- Without proper initiation, things go wrong fast:
	- Without understanding the goals → underestimate the resources needed or how long the project will take
	- Without agreeing on what success looks like → you think the project succeeded while stakeholders think it didn't accomplish their goals
- **Cost-benefit analysis**: the process of adding up the expected value of a project (the benefits) and comparing them to the dollar costs → the benefits should always outweigh the costs
	- Benefit questions: What value will this project create? How much money could it save the organization? How much money will it bring in from existing customers? How much time will be saved? How will the user experience be improved?
	- Cost questions: How much time will people spend on the project? What are the one-time costs? Any ongoing costs? Long-term costs?
#### Key components of project initiation
- Key components of initiation: **goals, scope, deliverables, success criteria, stakeholders, resources** → documented in the **project charter**
	- **Goal**: what you've been asked to do and what you're trying to achieve; often determined by senior company leaders, with your help
	- **Project scope**: the process to define the work that needs to happen to complete the project
	- **Deliverables**: the products and services you create for your customer, client, or project sponsor (product features and functionalities, documentation, processes, ...) → submitted to help reach the project goals
		- **Tangible** (e.g. submitting a chapter of a manuscript when the goal is to publish a textbook) or **intangible** (e.g. scheduling staff training sessions when the goal is implementing new point-of-sale software at a retail store)
	- **Success criteria**: the standards by which you measure how successful a project was in reaching its goals
	- **Stakeholders**: people who both have an interest in and are affected by the completion and success of a project → instrumental in determining goals, objectives, deliverables, success criteria; the PM must understand their needs early and ensure all stakeholders agree on the goals and mission before moving to the next phase
	- **Resources**: the budget, people, materials, and other items at your disposal → think about them early (don't realize halfway through that there isn't enough money or people)
- **Project charter**: a document that contains all the details of the project → clearly defines the project and its goals, outlines what is needed to accomplish them; helps get organized, set up a framework, and communicate details → reviewed with key stakeholders for approval to move into the **planning** stage

#### Performing a cost-benefit analysis
- A **cost-benefit analysis** minimizes risks and maximizes gains; helps communicate with stakeholders and executives, keeps the project on track; uses objective data → reduces biases and stakeholder self-interest
	- Makes a strong **business case**; organizations use it to reduce waste and invest resources responsibly
- Guiding questions:
	- Benefits: value created, money saved, money from existing customers, time saved, improved customer experience
	- Costs: time people spend, one-time costs, ongoing costs, long-term costs
	- **Intangible benefits** (not quantifiable): **customer satisfaction** (retention), **employee satisfaction** (morale, lower turnover), **employee productivity** (fewer overtime hours), **brand perception** (recognition, competitive advantage)
	- **Intangible costs**: flip the questions (might the project put customer retention, employee satisfaction, or brand perception at risk?)
	- Assign values by referencing similar past projects, industry research, or consulting experts
- **Return on investment (ROI)**: calculating costs and benefits; easiest way = compare the upfront and ongoing costs to the benefits over time
	- Formula: ROI = (G − C) ÷ C, with **G** = expected financial gains, **C** = upfront and ongoing costs
	- Example: $6,000 up front + $25/month for 12 months ($300) → C = $6,300; G = $10,000 → ($10,000 − $6,300) ÷ $6,300 = $3,700 ÷ $6,300 = 0.5873 → **58.7%**
	- A strong ROI tends to be anything above **10%** → pursue the project

## Module 2: Defining project goals, scope, and success criteria
### Identifying project goals
#### Introduction: Defining project goals, scope, and success criteria
- Module goals: define and create project **goals** and **deliverables** (the guiding stars of the project); define **project scope** (the boundaries: what is and is not part of the project), identify **in-scope** vs **out-of-scope**, recognize **scope creep**; explain ways of defining and measuring **success criteria**
- Running example for the course: you are the lead project manager at **Office Green**, a commercial landscaping company specializing in plant decor for offices and other businesses
	- The Director of Product's idea: **Plant Pals**, a new service offering high-volume customers small, low-maintenance desk plants (little cacti, leafy ferns) → you manage the rollout
	- At the end, compile everything into a shared document to use as a portfolio for future employers
#### Determining project goals and deliverables
- Before starting, define exactly what the goals and deliverables are → a clear picture of what you're trying to accomplish, how, and how you know it has been accomplished
- **Project goal**: the desired outcome of the project; what you've been asked to do and what you're trying to achieve → a roadmap to your destination
	- E.g. improve the response time to customer inquiries via email by **20%**; Office Green: increase revenue by **5%** through a new service called Plant Pals that offers desk plants to top customers by the end of the year
	- **Well-defined** goals: clear and specific, tell you what to achieve and how (via email, through a new service), and by how much → both **specific** and **measurable**
	- Review the goals with stakeholders: their vision, how it aligns with the company's larger goals and mission → agree to support the goals to avoid issues later
	- JuAnne's example: the goal was to deliver an early version of a feature and collect user feedback; the customer had no one available to try it → the team wasted time debating whether the goal was met → make sure everyone is clear on the goals before starting
- **Project deliverables**: the products or services created for the customer, client, or project sponsor; what gets produced or presented at the end of a task, event, or process
	- E.g. email templates for responding to typical questions (customer response time goal); Office Green: launching the plant service + a finished website highlighting the new plants (tangible outputs showing how revenue will be generated); a common one is a **report** (chart, graph, presentation)
	- Help quantify and realize the impact of the project; usually decided upfront with stakeholders or clients → hold everyone accountable; ask everyone to share their vision and expectations of the deliverables
#### How to set SMART goals
- **SMART goals**: **Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound** → a method to create and evaluate goals; entry-level PMs may not set the main goals but must identify and clarify them
- **Specific**: should answer at least two of these questions:
	- What do I want to accomplish? Why is this a goal (specific reason, purpose, or benefit)? Who is involved / who is the recipient (employees, customers, the community)? Where should the goal be delivered? To what degree (requirements and constraints)?
	- "Improve customer service response time" alone is not specific (1%? after five years? only half the staff?)
- **Measurable**: can be objectively determined as met → tracks progress and keeps people motivated; ask how much, how many, how will I know when it's accomplished?
	- Sometimes a simple yes or no (did you learn to play the guitar?); most goals need **metrics**: what you use to measure something, like numbers or figures (e.g. distance in kilometers for a 5K race; revenue for Office Green)
	- **Benchmarks**: points of reference to choose accurate metrics (e.g. last year's revenue increased by 3% → a 5% increase in a booming economy is reasonable)
- **Attainable**: can it reasonably be reached? A little challenging to encourage growth, but not too extreme
	- E.g. regularly running 2.5 km three times a week → running 5 km within four weeks is attainable (a little over half a kilometer more each week); winning first place in the 5K is not
	- Ask how it can be accomplished → break the goal into smaller parts; Office Green: 5% over a year (four quarters) → at least **1.25%** per quarter; 50% or 100% would be unreasonable unless research showed business improving that quickly
- **Relevant**: does it make sense to reach this goal? Lines up with other goals, priorities, values; is the effort worth the benefits; do the client, project team, and end users feel it's worth supporting?
	- Consider timing (project duration, larger economic and social contexts): can the company sustain the project over time? Will an audience keep using the product or service?
	- If not confident, keep digging and communicate concerns with senior stakeholders and your direct supervisor
- **Time-bound**: the goal has a **deadline** → track progress; time can also be a metric → break down how much needs to be accomplished each quarter, month, week
#### SMART goals: Making goals meaningful
- SMART goals help see the full scope of a goal, determine its feasibility, and define project success in concrete terms
	- **Specific**: no ambiguity for the project team to misinterpret
	- **Measurable**: metrics help the team determine when the objective is met
	- **Attainable**: the team agrees the objective is realistic
	- **Relevant**: fits the organization's strategic plan and supports the project charter
	- **Time-bound**: the team documents a date to achieve the goal
	- Variations of the acronym: "actionable" or "achievable" instead of "attainable", "realistic" instead of "relevant" → same intent (the goal is within reach)
- Focus on **measurable**: quantifiable or tangible metrics (dollar amounts, number of outputs, quantities) → little room for confusion about stakeholder expectations
	- Not every metric has value: e.g. number of meetings software engineers attend weekly is not useful for a productivity goal → better: number of features created per engineer, number of issues flagged per day
- Example of a personal SMART goal: complete a Google Career Certificate (measurable: you receive a certificate) → attainable: one course per month; relevant: supports a career change; time-bound: within six months
	- → "Obtain a Google Career Certificate by taking one course per month within the next six months."
- SMART goals: Making goals meaningful: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/mj0tv
#### Introduction to OKRs
- **OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)**: a popular tool used in many organizations and at Google → establish and clarify goals for an organization, department, project, or person; focus a team's time and effort on activities that drive success
	- Take SMART goals a step further: combine a goal and more detailed metrics to determine a measurable outcome; separate the components of SMART goals instead of grouping everything into one statement
- **Objective (O)**: defines what needs to be achieved; the desired result or outcome (e.g. increase customer retention, improve the employee onboarding process)
- **Key results (KR)**: the measurable outcomes that define when the objective has been met (e.g. 90% customer satisfaction rating by the end of the first quarter)
	- Unlike SMART's attainability, key results should be a little more **ambitious**: Google uses OKRs to set **stretch goals** → accomplishing all key results may mean the OKRs were too easy
- OKRs are set at different levels:
	- **Company-level**: shared across the organization, usually updated **annually**, support the organization's mission
		- Office Green objective: increase customer retention by adapting to the changing workplace environment
		- Key results: 95% of phone, chat, and email support tickets resolved during the first contact; top three most requested new offerings for distributed office environments in pilot by the end of Q2 (→ could become the Plant Pals project); sales and support channels available 24/7 by the end of the year
	- **Team or department-level**: support the company's OKRs, drive team performance, specific to the job function (e.g. sales objective: increase the sales team presence nationwide; KR: new sales offices open in 10 cities by the end of the year)
	- **Project-level**: set during **initiation** to define measurable project goals, tracked through planning and execution; must align with company and department OKRs
		- Plant Pals objective: enroll existing customers in the Plant Pals service; KR: 25% of existing customers sign up for the Plant Pals pilot
#### Creating OKRs for your project
- **OKRs** combine a goal and a metric to determine a measurable outcome
	- **Objectives**: define what needs to be achieved; describe a desired outcome
	- **Key results**: the measurable outcomes that objectively define when the objective has been met
	- **Company-wide OKRs** set an ultimate goal for an entire organization, team, or department; **project-level OKRs** describe the focused results each group needs to support the organization
- For project managers: expand on project goals, clarify the deliverables; establish the appropriate scope (say "no" to requests that get in the way of the objectives); motivate the team (push past what's easily achievable)
- **Set your objectives**: what the team should accomplish in **3–6 months**
	- Examples: build the most secure data security software, continuously improve web analytics and conversions, provide a top-performing service, make a universally-available app, increase market reach, achieve top sales among competitors in the region
	- Strong objectives are **aspirational**, **aligned with organizational goals**, **action-oriented**, **concrete**, **significant**
	- Questions: Does it help achieve the project's overall goals? Does it align with company and departmental OKRs? Is it inspiring and motivational? Will achieving it make a significant impact?
- **Develop key results**: **2–3 key results** per objective; time-bound; stretch the team
	- Examples: X% new signups within the first quarter post launch; increase advertiser spend by X% within the first two quarters; new feature adoption at least X% by the end of the year; maximum 2 critical bugs reported monthly by customers per Sprint; maintain newsletter unsubscribe rate at X% this calendar year
	- Strong key results are **results-oriented (not a task)**, **measurable and verifiable**, **specific and time-bound**, **aggressive yet realistic**
	- Questions: What does success mean? What metrics would prove we've achieved the objective?
- Best practices: objectives are motivational and inspiring, key results are tactical and specific (the objective = what you want to do, key results = how you'll know you did it); about 2–3 key results per objective; document OKRs and link them in the project plan
- OKRs vs SMART goals: similar but with key differences (see the article *Understanding the Unique Utility of OKRs vs. SMART Goals*); more resources: Google's OKR playbook, OKR TED Talk by **John Doerr** (founder of OKRs)
- Creating OKRs for your project: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/Tt5Xr
### Defining project scope
#### Determining a project's scope
- **Project scope**: the boundaries of a project; at Google: "an agreed upon understanding as to what is included or excluded from a project"
	- Who the project will be delivered to and who will use the end result
	- The project's **complexity** (straightforward task list vs extensive research, multiple rounds of approvals, large-scale production over years)
	- The project **timeline, budget, and resources**
	- Poorly-defined scope or major scope changes → changes to the budget, timeline, or even the final outcome
- Office Green example: Plant Pals customers order plants online or from a print catalog, shipped to their work address → scope questions: replacement plants? which customer segments? online catalog as an app, a website, or both? purchasing from phone, PC, Mac, iPhone, Android? paper catalog dimensions, color vs black and white, kind of paper?
- How to determine scope: talk to sponsors and stakeholders, understand their goals, find out what is and (importantly) what is **not** included
	- Questions: Where did the project come from? Why is it needed? What is it expected to achieve? What does the project sponsor have in mind? Who approves the final results?
- Define scope early (initial planning) so everyone agrees on the same expectations → mitigates the risk of big changes later (can still adjust as planning continues); document all details for reference throughout the life cycle
- A clearly defined scope regulates what can be added or removed; the project manager is ultimately responsible for keeping work and resources within scope, but team members and stakeholders help by focusing on the most important tasks
#### Gathering information to define scope
- Scenario: in a restaurant management group, your manager asks you to "update the dining space" and hangs up → missing information; scope defines the boundaries → identify resources, resource costs, and a schedule
- Scope-defining questions:
	- **Stakeholders**: How did you arrive at the decision? Did the request originate from the owner, customers, or other stakeholders? Who will approve the scope?
	- **Goals**: What is the reason for the update? What isn't working in the current space? What is the end goal?
	- **Deliverables**: Which dining space? What exactly needs to be updated? Does it need a remodel?
	- **Resources**: What materials, equipment, and people? Contractors? Floor plan and building permits?
	- **Budget**: What is the budget? Fixed or flexible?
	- **Schedule**: How much time? When does it need to be completed?
	- **Flexibility**: How much flexibility? What is the highest priority: the deadline, the budget, or meeting all quality targets?
- Asking questions reduces expenses, rework, frustration, and confusion → understand the **who, what, when, where, why, and how** of the scope
- Gathering information to define scope: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/EZHIq
#### Monitoring and maintaining a project's scope
- **In-scope**: tasks included in the project that contribute to its overall goal; **out-of-scope**: tasks not included → the PM sets and maintains firm boundaries (e.g. Plant Pals catalog copywriters or designers suggest expanding the types of plants offered → out-of-scope, extra time and budget)
- **Scope creep**: changes, growth, and uncontrolled factors that affect a project's scope at any point after the project begins → common on every project, in any industry
	- Example: updating the language icons' design on a mobile keyboard app → the team also refreshes the search and voice input icons (small, not in-scope) → at a stakeholder review someone suggests designing keyboards for other languages → the scope risks expanding to a complex rollout of multiple keyboard layouts → impacts timeline, resourcing (hire more people or overtime), budget (extra hours, translations)
	- Can be subtle ("Just design one or two more icons!") or obvious ("Can you tack on designing keyboards for other languages?")
- Two major sources:
	- **External** (easier to recognize): the customer requests changes, the business environment shifts, the underlying technology changes
		- Tips: give stakeholders visibility (what will be produced, resources, cost, time); get clarity on requirements and constructive criticism of the initial proposal before contracts are signed; set ground rules and expectations for stakeholder involvement (roles during execution and status reviews); plan how to handle out-of-scope requests (who can make formal **change requests**, how they are evaluated, accepted, performed); get agreements **in writing**
		- A leading cause: not being clear on requirements before defining scope and getting formal approval → specific, measurable goals and deliverables matter
	- **Internal** (trickier to spot, harder to control): team members suggest or insist on process or product changes (e.g. a developer making the product "better" at higher cost; a team lead changing a process without realizing its impact on others)
		- Any change outside scope comes off the bottom line, threatens the schedule, increases risk → "there are no small impacts to project scope"
- Best defense: know the project details inside and out → monitor and protect the scope at all costs
#### Strategies for controlling scope creep
- **Scope creep**: when a project's work grows beyond what was originally agreed upon during initiation → stress on you, the team, and the organization; hinders schedule, budget, resources, overall success
- Best practices for scope management:
	- **Define your project's requirements**: find out exactly what stakeholders or customers want and document it during initiation
	- **Set a clear project schedule**: outline all requirements and the tasks needed to achieve them
	- **Determine what is out of scope**: make sure everyone understands when proposed changes are out of scope; agree on and document potential impacts
	- **Provide alternatives**: suggest alternative solutions, show additional risks, perform a cost-benefit analysis if necessary
	- **Set up a change control process**: how each change is defined, reviewed, approved (or rejected) before being added to the plan; make sure the team knows it
	- **Learn how to say no**: explain how additional tasks interfere with the budget, timeline, and/or resources
	- **Collect costs for out-of-scope work**: document all costs incurred, including indirectly impacted work, and what the charges are for
- Avoiding scope creep requires everyone to agree on responsibilities, boundaries, and timelines, plus clear communication, expectation management, and a well-defined path to the outcome
- Strategies for controlling scope creep: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/fNCow
#### Managing changes to a project's scope
- Managing scope goes hand in hand with goal-setting: redefining the scope can change the goal and vice versa → the PM's overall goal: deliver the project according to the **scope agreements** (within the deadline and approved budget) → continually make compromises and weigh trade-offs
- **Triple constraint model**: the combination of the three most significant restrictions of any project: **scope, time, and cost**
	- **Time**: the project schedule and deadlines
	- **Cost**: the budget, plus resources and the people working on the project
	- All three are linked: a decrease in cost → a change in time or scope; an increase in time → a change in scope or cost, or both
- Know the project priorities: if a deadline must be met → limit scope changes; if the product must look or function a certain way → requirements are the priority, justify changes in cost or time
	- Just because you can make a change doesn't mean you should; changes are still possible with a good reason; the PM consults the project sponsor and stakeholders for approval of scope decisions
- Plant Pals scenarios:
	- Use pots that indicate when plants need watering (scope change), budget can't change → extend the timeline
	- Reduce the budget without changing scope → may need to extend the timeline
	- Finish early without increasing budget → change scope (e.g. limit shipping options → one less shipping contract to negotiate)
	- The deadline is most important → stakeholders increase the budget and change scope requirements as needed
- Change is inevitable → the triple constraint gives you the tools to evaluate scope changes (more in Course 4)

### Measuring a project's success
#### Launching and landing a project
- **Project launch**: delivering the final result of the project to the client or user (tasks completed, deliverables done, goal hit) → but does it work well and achieve the desired outcome?
- **Landing**: actually measuring the success of the project using the **success criteria** established at the outset → a crucial, often overlooked part of goal setting in initiation
	- Analogy: a pilot must not only take off but also know how to land
	- Plant Pals: website launched, catalogs printed and delivered, orders received, revenue rising → but what if customers are unhappy or plants wilt and discolor after a couple of weeks? → launched but not landed
- Launches are only a means to an end; landing is a concept, so define what a successful landing looks like for each project
- **Success criteria**: all the specific details of your goals and deliverables → a guide to know whether you accomplished what you set out to do; set the standards by which the project will be judged
#### Don't forget to land: Measuring project success
- Goal is not just to **launch** but to **land**: landings occur once the project achieves a measure of success → what PMs strive for and celebrate
- **Launching**: delivering the final results to the client or user; project success can't be based solely on client acceptance → **land** it by thoroughly measuring results with the success criteria and metrics defined when setting SMART goals
	- Teams align on a measurable goal beyond launching (increase retention? speed up a product feature?)
- **Launch first, land later** example: an eco-friendly organization creates a recycling training program for middle school students; the county's goal is to increase recycling by **20%** over **five years** → one year to research, develop, produce → handing it to the school district = launch → check back periodically over five years = landing
- **Launch and forget**: a common mistake → the client accepts the delivery but the PM doesn't assess whether deliverables satisfy the customer or user
- A landing done correctly creates greater alignment on the desired end results and better visibility on how to achieve success → review notes, talk with the team, meet the client, return to the intended deliverables and metrics
- Don't forget to land: Measuring project success: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/4qz63
#### Defining success criteria
- **Success criteria**: the specific details of your goals and deliverables that tell you whether you've accomplished what you set out to do → the standards by which the project is judged once delivered to stakeholders and customers; must be **measurable**
	- Clarify what the team is trying to accomplish beyond launching (increase customer satisfaction so they purchase more? enhance a feature to retain customers?) → forcing the conversation can bring up questions and disagreements
- No set process, but key points:
	- Ask "How will I know when it's **successfully** accomplished?" → go through goals and deliverables, review scope, identify measurable aspects (metrics in the goals and deliverables, budget, schedule)
	- Get clarity from stakeholders on requirements and expectations: Who ultimately says whether the project is successful? What criteria will be measured? What is success based on? → document and share
- Need a process for measuring success throughout the entire life cycle (Office Green: goal = increase revenue 5% by year end; deliverable = a website with a gallery of plant selections) → choose metrics closely aligned to the goal, sometimes more than one:
	- **Happiness metrics**: user attitudes and satisfaction, perceived ease of use → surveys (Plant Pals: **85%** customer satisfaction rate within the first three months of launch)
	- **Adoption**: how the customer uses and adopts a product or service without issues (e.g. how many customers initially sign up for and use Plant Pals)
	- **Engagement**: how often or meaningful customer interaction and participation is over time (e.g. how many customers renew Plant Pals, post about it, or share feedback; daily usage of a design feature)
	- **Business-oriented metrics**: sales and growth
- Tracking tools: revenue in a spreadsheet or dashboard (spot gaps and trends); incentivize customers to take regular email surveys; project management tools for **efficiency metrics** (% of tasks completed, progress vs planned timelines)
- Measure success with the team during the project: monthly project review, task checklists by deadlines, live feedback sessions with users or customers
- For each success criterion, include **how** success will be measured, **how often**, and **who** is responsible → share with stakeholders, get **sign-off** → keep the documentation visible and communicate it to the team → greater alignment, better visibility, helps prioritize the most impactful efforts
#### Tracking and communicating success criteria
- Besides SMART goals, success is determined by the **quality of the product**, the ability to **fulfill customer needs**, and meeting **stakeholder expectations**
- **Product quality**: attributes like completeness of features, quality of features, unit cost, usability → a list of **product requirements** (e.g. word processing software: text entry, formatting, saving, printing)
	- Metrics: whether priority requirements were implemented; number of technical issues or defects; % of features delivered or released at the end of the project
- **What is important to customers or stakeholders**: additional expectations (e.g. a stakeholder wants easy table creation; a strategic goal of more collaborative ability than other word processors) → tie back to the business case
	- Metrics: user engagement with the product; stakeholder and customer satisfaction via surveys; user adoption via sales data
- **Document, align, and communicate success**: ask who says whether the project is successful, what criteria will be measured, what success is based on → best practice: key stakeholders or the **steering committee** review and approve the success criteria (mutual agreement)
- All parties need continuous access and alignment to the agreed success criteria to avoid scope creep or failed expectations → document upfront and report throughout the project (Success Criteria Template; article *Same Project, Different Perspectives*, PMI)
- Tracking and communicating success criteria: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/BvetB
#### Using OKRs to evaluate progress
- **OKRs** help project teams define, communicate, and measure shared success criteria
- Communicating and tracking OKRs:
	- **Share your OKRs with your team**: digital document, meeting presentation, internal website → focus and align efforts, monitor which goals fall short, stay motivated
	- **Assign owners** to every key result → clarity and accountability
- Measuring progress: **score or grade** OKRs (not a complete assessment but shows how close you came) → share scores in project updates
	- Scoring methods: % of the objective completed, completion of milestones, a 1–10 scale, **traffic light** (red = no progress, yellow = some progress, green = completed)
	- Simplest: **yes/no** (e.g. "Launch a new widget marketing campaign" → 1 or 0)
	- Advanced: grade on a scale (e.g. "Launch six new features" but only three launched → 0.5)
	- At Google: scale of **0.0 to 1.0** (1.0 = fully achieved); each key result is graded, then averaged for the OKR score
- **Set scoring expectations**: set ambitious OKRs and aim for an average of at least **0.6–0.7** (or 60–70%); lower → the team isn't achieving what it could; higher → aspirational goals not set high enough
- **Schedule checkpoints**: e.g. monthly check-ins on OKR progress; grade each OKR at the end of the quarter
- Templates: OKR Scorecard Template (Doc and Sheet)
- Using OKRs to evaluate progress: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/d1ZF4

### Exploring project team roles and responsibilities
#### Introduction: Working effectively with stakeholders
- Recap: project scope (in-scope / out-of-scope), SMART goals, launching (getting it started) vs landing (whether it was successful)
- This module: **stakeholders** = people interested in and affected by the project's completion and success; each has a set role and responsibilities (project sponsors, customers, team members, the project manager)

#### Accessibility for project managers
- Holly: accessibility education program manager at Google (deaf herself) → having a disability isn't a barrier in itself; the world around us must be made accessible
- **Accessibility**: actively removing barriers that might prevent persons with disabilities from accessing technology, information, or experiences → leveling the playing field
- **Disability**: a physical or mental condition that substantially limits a major life activity (walking, talking, seeing, hearing, learning)
	- Over **one billion** people in the world have a disability (more than the populations of the US, Canada, France, Italy, Japan, Mexico, and Brazil combined)
	- Diverse and intersectional; born with or acquired later; **permanent** (deafness), **temporary** (a broken leg), **situational** (operating a TV remote in the dark)
	- Solutions for people with permanent disabilities unlock secondary benefits for everyone
- In project management, you, your team, or stakeholders may have a visible or invisible disability → the PM must make sure the infrastructure and culture work for everyone; ask others what they need and share what you need
- Many technologies started as accessibility features: **Google Assistant** (voice control of the home), **closed captioning** (watching TV in a noisy bar)
#### Choosing a project team
- Choosing the right people is a big task → consider the project needs:
	1. Make a list of **roles** needed for each task; each role is accountable for specific tasks (e.g. home construction: architect, site manager, construction workers)
	2. Decide **how many people**: small projects 3–4 people, larger projects dozens (at Google sometimes hundreds) → too many = communication becomes difficult, details missed; too few = not enough people to finish tasks
	3. Decide **who does what** based on **skills** → skills can be taught (someone with a positive attitude and attention to detail may still be a great fit), but train them in time to avoid delays
	4. Factor in **availability** (already staffed on another big project?) and **motivation** (visibility for a promotion?) → pick people excited to get involved, but sometimes another manager assigns people → deliver the best work with what you're given
- Office Green questions: Who has office landscaping experience? Who's local to the launch city? Who can be fully dedicated for the next eight weeks?
- No exact formula; key questions: How many people do I need each step of the way? Which team members do I need and when? Are those experts busy on other projects? Who makes the final decisions on project resources?
#### Review: The building blocks of a project dream team
- **Team size**: depends on project size → complex projects with large divisions of work need larger teams; simple projects only a few people → find the right balance
- **Skills and abilities**: on smaller teams one person may fill multiple roles
	- **Technical skills**: specific to the task (Office Green: indoor landscaping design for plant layout, floral design for arranging)
	- **Interpersonal skills** (people skills, soft skills): patience, conflict mediation → blend technical expertise with collaboration, minimize team issues
	- **Problem-solving skills**: a must, especially on large complex projects (the PM can't solve every problem)
	- **Leadership skills** (underrated): navigate organizational boundaries, communicate with stakeholders to generate buy-in
- **Availability**: a big concern, especially in **Matrix organizations** (team members have multiple bosses; may be pulled onto another project) → ideally pick people who stay for the entire life cycle
	- **Pre-assignment**: the sponsor assigns team members to your project (you don't get to pick)
	- Value **diversity** early: acknowledge and highlight it as an asset, encourage conversations about differences → richer understanding, greater creativity; dedicate time early to build **trust** → safe to share different views and constructive feedback
- **Motivation**: a pre-assigned person isn't necessarily uninterested, but a volunteer may be more motivated → the PM engages the team and keeps it motivated (respectful engagement, positive outlook during adversity)
- Team size, skills, availability, and motivation are the building blocks → a PM doesn't just select dream teams but **creates** them through collaboration under great leadership
- Review: The building blocks of a project dream team: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/m6Uhe
#### Defining project roles
- Many moving parts → need confidence that the team has the skills and motivation; lay out each role's responsibilities from the start
- Some roles aren't fixed: in small companies or with limited resources, one person may take several roles (e.g. project manager, designer, and marketer)
- Project roles:
	- **Project sponsor**: the person accountable for the project who ensures it delivers the agreed-upon value to the business; vital leadership role, sometimes funds the project, communicates with managers and key stakeholders
	- **Team members**: the heart of the operation, doing the day-to-day work
	- **Customers**: people who get value from a successfully landed project; their needs usually define the project's requirements (the buyers)
	- **Users**: the people who ultimately use the product (e.g. corporation ABC buys an instant message application = customer; everyone at ABC using it daily = users)
	- **Stakeholders**: anyone with a vested interest in the project's success
		- **Primary stakeholders**: expect to benefit directly from the project's completion
		- **Secondary stakeholders**: play an intermediary role, indirectly impacted (contractors, partner organization members)
	- **Project manager**: plans, organizes, and oversees the whole project
- Office Green (SMART goal: roll out a new service providing office plants to top clients by the end of the year):
	- Sponsor: the **Director of Product** → approves the budget, keeps the vision (inexpensive, easy-to-maintain live plants improve employees' work environments)
	- Team: people across departments (e.g. marketing); the landscape designer is also the website designer (more than one role)
	- Customers: office buyers (office managers, procurement teams); users: the office employees who enjoy the plants
	- Secondary stakeholders: Office Green's investors (funding the launch), the receptionist (answering customer questions after launch) → must be informed
#### Essential project roles
- **Project manager**: responsible for the overall success of the team and the project
	- Pays close attention to **team dynamics**: team-building, motivation, influencing, decision-making, coaching
	- Integrates all project work: develops the project management plan, directs the work, documents reports, controls change, monitors quality
	- Balances scope, schedule, and cost by managing stakeholder engagement → communication, political and cultural awareness, negotiation, trust-building, conflict management
- **Stakeholders**: "stakes" = important parts that might be at risk if something goes wrong; holding a stake = being invested in its success
	- **Primary (key) stakeholders**: directly affected by the outcome (team members, senior leaders, customers) → e.g. construction company building an event space for a catering company: the catering company owners (paying), the construction company CEO if directly involved
	- **Secondary stakeholders**: indirectly affected (e.g. the point of contact in legal who processes the contract)
	- Know who they are, what they need, and how to communicate with them
- **Project team members**: primary stakeholders; vary with the type, complexity, and size of the project → right technical and interpersonal skills; if you can't select the team, champion diversity and build trust
- **Sponsor**: a primary stakeholder who initiates the project, presents the **business case**, signs the **project charter**, releases resources to the PM → communicate frequently in all phases (the CEO could also be the sponsor)

### Evaluating stakeholders
#### Completing a stakeholder analysis
- **Stakeholder analysis**: a visual representation of all the stakeholders → avoid surprises, build partnerships, involve the right people at the right time; shows opportunities and risks, who takes which responsibilities
- Three key steps:
	1. Make a list of all the stakeholders the project impacts
	2. Determine the level of **interest** and **influence** for each stakeholder
	3. Assess their ability to participate and find ways to involve them
- **Influence**: how much power a stakeholder has and how much their actions affect the project outcome (Office Green: the Director of Product has huge influence; the greenery vendor has less)
- **Interest**: how much the stakeholder's needs are affected by the project operations and outcomes (HR may have less interest than the sales department)
- **Power grid**: a two-by-two grid for stakeholder analysis (interest vs influence) → position determines their role; higher interest and influence = more important (the **key stakeholders**)
	- **High influence, high interest** (top right): key players → **closely partner** with them
	- **High influence, low interest** (top left): **consult** with them and **meet their needs** (e.g. the Director of Product may not be vested in day-to-day activities)
	- **Low influence, high interest** (bottom right): **show consideration**, keep them up-to-date (e.g. the customer success team working directly with clients)
	- **Low influence, low interest** (bottom left): **monitor** them, keep them in the know
- **Steering committee**: high-influence, high-interest stakeholders; the most senior decision-making body → authority to change budget, approve timeline or scope updates; the PM isn't a member but brings the right information for quick decisions
- **Stakeholder buy-in**: the process of involving stakeholders in decision-making to reach a broader consensus on the organization's future → pay attention to high-impact stakeholders, explain how the project helps them achieve their goals
- Communication tips:
	- One main stakeholder → likely highly influential, needs constant communication; many stakeholders → less involved day-to-day
	- Stakeholders needing time to decide → **over-communicate early** (frequent meetings, daily end-of-day progress emails)
	- Match the level of detail (the facilities team delivering the product doesn't need vendor pricing or website updates; the sales team needs pricing and availability changes → weekly check-in)
#### Prioritizing stakeholders and generating their buy-in
- Balance stakeholder requirements, get their buy-in, know when and how to involve them
- Stakeholder analysis steps:
	1. List all stakeholders: Who is invested? Who is impacted? Who contributes?
	2. Determine interest and influence → identify key stakeholders, prioritize their needs
	3. Assess ability to participate: **active stakeholders** (more opinions and touchpoints) vs **passive stakeholders** (high-level updates only) → factors: physical distance, existing workload
	- Pro tip: a **steering committee** of key stakeholders with high power and interest can influence multiple departments → release more resources to the PM
- **Power grid**: four quadrants, upper half = higher influence, right half = higher interest
	- **Quadrant 1: High influence, high interest** (upper right, "manage closely"): sponsors, key executives, regulatory authorities → engagement and involvement in decisions, address concerns promptly, regular meetings tailored to their preferences
	- **Quadrant 2: High influence, low interest** (upper left, "meet their needs"): senior managers → **executive summaries** (impact on organizational goals), periodic updates on major milestones and critical changes
	- **Quadrant 3: Low influence, high interest** (bottom right, "show consideration") → regular updates on progress and risks, answer queries promptly, seek their feedback
	- **Quadrant 4: Low influence, low interest** (bottom left, "monitor"): lower-level employees, departments not directly impacted → general communication without overwhelming details, minimal engagement
	- Pro tip: tools organize information, but success depends on knowing stakeholders' motivations and inspirations (time, interpersonal skills, insight into internal politics)
- **Generating buy-in** (so the project isn't deprioritized or deprived of resources):
	- Clearly map the project work to the stakeholder's goals
	- Describe how the project aligns with the goals of their department or team
	- Listen to their feedback and incorporate it into the project charter where appropriate
	- Present a realistic view of your team's abilities: don't over-promise and under-deliver
- Prioritizing stakeholders and generating their buy-in: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/xYge2
### Assigning project team roles and responsibilities
#### Elements of a RACI chart
- **RACI chart**: helps define roles and responsibilities for individuals or teams so work gets done efficiently → clear roles and direction for each team member
	- **Responsible**: those doing the work to complete the task
	- **Accountable**: those making sure the work gets done
	- **Consulted**: those giving feedback (subject matter experts, decision-makers)
	- **Informed**: those needing to know the final decisions or that a task is complete
- How to create it:
	1. Write the roles or people's names in a row across the top (pro tip: use **roles** rather than names if some people take more than one role)
	2. Write the tasks or deliverables in a column on the left (not too specific → simple and easy to read)
	3. For each task ask: Who's responsible for doing this? Who's accountable if it doesn't get done? Who has strong opinions and should be consulted? Who needs to be informed? → assign R, A, C, I
- Office Green example: create price points for different packages and delivery frequencies → **Head of Finance** = Accountable (stay in budget and make money), **Financial Analyst** = Responsible (determines optimal pricing), **Director of Product** = Consulted (oversees product offerings), **sales team** = Informed (final pricing)
- Several roles can be Informed or Consulted, but **never more than one person Accountable** (clear ownership); the Accountable person may also be Responsible
- Causes of role confusion that RACI analysis resolves or prevents: **unbalanced workloads**, **unclear hierarchy** (who to seek help from), **unclear ownership of decisions** (who makes the final call), **overlapping work**, **excessive communication** (information overload → miss something important)
#### Building out a RACI chart
- Also called a **Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RAM)**, RACI diagram, or RACI matrix → clarifies each person's role; can be organized in different formats
- **Responsible** (who gets the work done): every task needs at least one; best to limit the number → questions: What department does the work fall under? Who will perform the work? (Office Green: the Financial Analyst develops price points)
- **Accountable** (who makes sure the work is done): **only one** per task; has the authority to approve the deliverable of the responsible party → questions: Who will delegate the task? Who will review the work? → separate from Responsible where possible (Office Green: the Head of Finance approves the price points)
- **Consulted** (who gives input or feedback): no minimum or maximum, but each needs a reason; frequent **two-way communication** with the responsible party → questions: Who will the task impact? Who has input or feedback? Who are the **subject matter experts (SMEs)**? (Office Green: the Director of Product, who knows potential product changes affecting price points)
- **Informed** (who needs to know the outcome): often many people; only need status updates, no feedback → questions: Who cares about the task's completion? Who will be affected by the outcome? (Office Green: the Sales Team needs final price points to sell)
	- Pro tip: keep a large Informed group updated without labor-intensive effort (e.g. **view-only access** to the project plan or meeting notes)
- The RACI chart also helps analyze and balance the team's workload; may take many revisions, but doing it upfront saves time and prevents miscommunication
- Building out a RACI chart: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/WC2rP
#### Certificate completers: Staying motivated in the program
- Certificate completers' tips: someone else is always going through the same thing; believe in yourself ("just do it, you can do it"); learn on the go with the mobile app; build a support network (a friend or family member as a coach); don't be afraid to ask for help from peers, coaches, friends, family
#### Getting the most out of a RACI chart
- Defining who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed keeps control of stakeholder roles
- **Workload balance**: tally the number of **Rs** per stakeholder → avoid overloading one person; mitigate **single points of failure** (**silos**: knowledge and responsibility for a task fall on one person); delegate and avoid burnout; don't over-rely on top performers; if you lack the right people, re-evaluate the team
- **Put your RACI into practice**: share it with sponsors and stakeholders for **buy-in and sign-off** → clear expectations, alignment; document acknowledgment in the project charter, meeting notes, and the RACI chart itself; keep checking in → streamlined communication and decision-making
- **When to use a RACI chart**: evaluate complexity → a very small team, few stakeholders, clear roles, short timeline → a RACI chart could slow things down; larger projects or many stakeholders benefit greatly; creating one is always good practice even if you don't use it
- Getting the most out of a RACI chart: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/caqAJ

#### Why projects fail: Initiation missteps
- Some failure factors are out of your control (technology unavailable, a stakeholder drastically changes goals); others are more in your control (missing the agreed time, not fulfilling the stakeholder's vision)
- **Unclear expectations**: not asking essential questions at the start (end goal? deliverables and schedule? budget? stakeholders?), not documenting decisions, not understanding the true scope → "without directions, you can never reach your destination"
- **Unrealistic expectations**: e.g. trying to complete a two-week project in one week → not enough resources, quality issues → understand requirements before agreeing to deadlines; best practice: **don't commit to firm dates during initiation** (more information in the planning phase)
- **Miscommunication**: information not timely, missing pertinent information (risks, decisions, scope changes), or sent to the wrong stakeholders → use a stakeholder analysis + RACI chart for a communication strategy
	- Set expectations about how communication will occur; understand needs (some prefer phone, some writing, some face-to-face) → a strong system incorporates all of these
- **Lack of resources** (team members, budget, materials): team members juggling multiple tasks, specific skill sets required; incorrectly calculated expenses (e.g. a $10,000 budget with $10,000 of materials, but also shipping and installation) → clarify resource needs and confirm availability with leadership up front
- **Scope creep**: the scope grows and the impacts aren't captured (e.g. three deliverables + two more requested, with no change to schedule, budget, or team) → capture impacts on schedule, budget, and quality; document everything in initiation; plan how to handle scope creep and who can approve scope changes
- Every failure is an opportunity to learn, grow, and do better (article: *Seven Lessons to Learn from a Failed Project*)
- Why projects fail: Initiation missteps: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-initiation-google/supplement/Zbb7B

#### Essential project resources
- During initiation ask: what do we still need to acquire to complete the project? → **project resources** usually include **budget, people, and materials**, managed with **tools**

- **Budget**: an estimate of the amount of money a project will cost to complete (materials, software, vendors, marketing)

- **People**: the team executing tasks (the PM, a marketing manager creating ads, people outside the company with unique skills)
- **Materials**: items needed to get the project done (e.g. lumber for a construction project)
- **Tools**: aids that make it easier to manage resources and organize work (track tasks, manage budgets, collaborate) → productivity tools like **Google Docs**, work management software like **Asana**; keep them top of mind at all phases
- Office Green (goal: increase revenue by 5%): research the cost of launching Plant Pals (new website, promotional materials, shipping and delivery), budget for tools (project management software) → a realistic budget; list people and external vendors (e.g. the person managing client communications, a new plant supplier)
#### Managing resources to get the job done
- **Project resources**: who and what you depend on to complete a project (budget, materials, people) → all depend on one another (no work without materials, no materials without budget)
- **Budget**: like a personal budget (food, transportation, rent) → overspending in one category impacts the others
	- Common aspects: **team** (cost of the people performing the work), **services** (outside vendors), **materials** (tangible items purchased)
	- Some budgets have no margin for error, others are flexible → keep the budget aligned with the project scope and stakeholders' asks
- **People**: you can't complete the project on your own → get commitment and buy-in on the hours needed; the best partners are aligned to the project goals or most interested in its work
- **Materials**: differ on every project (IT project: hard drives, computers) and can be **intangible** (online storage, software programs, employee training)
- Interview stakeholders and potential team members about the resources they think they'll need (materials not budgeted, people with expertise)
