### Tracking and measuring project progress
#### The importance of tracking
- Once execution gets going, how do you know the work is getting done? → **tracking** and **measurement** (a big part of project management)
- **Tracking**: a method of following the progress of a project's activities
	- Measuring project performance regularly to identify **deviations** from the project plan helps keep the project on track
- **Deviation**: anything that alters your original course of action → can be **positive** (ahead of schedule because a technical problem is less complex than estimated) or **negative** (a natural disaster shuts down your testing team)
- Benefits of tracking:
	- Makes key project information **transparent** → transparency is essential for accurate decision-making; centralizes information so everyone understands the status, helps identify knowledge gaps, and you don't risk forgetting details
	- Keeps team members and stakeholders in touch with **deadlines and goals** → a project plan that works for you and your team, everyone on the same page
	- Recognizes **risks and issues** in a timely fashion → take corrective action, focus on areas at risk
	- Builds **confidence** that the project will be delivered on time, in scope, and within budget → keeps the team motivated
#### Common items to track
- **Project schedule**: tasks and activities that ensure the project is heading toward its completion date (goal: deliverables on time)
- **Status of action items, key tasks, and activities** → make sure the work is actually getting done; new tasks come up all the time
- **Progress toward milestones** (→ [Course 3: tasks and milestones](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- **Costs** → don't overspend or underspend on project tasks; even if you don't oversee the whole budget, your tasks and resources may have budget implications
- **Key decisions, changes, dependencies, and risks**, including agreed-upon **scope changes** → team and stakeholders aligned on what needs to be done
#### Different tracking methods
- The project plan always includes at least one tracking method (sometimes more) → choose something the **entire team can easily understand, reference, and keep up to date**
- **Gantt chart**: measures **tasks against time**, includes who owns each task and the order of tasks (→ [Course 3: Gantt chart in the project plan](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- Useful for staying on schedule, projects with many **dependencies**, and teams with a lot of people (ownership laid out visually)
	- Looks like a waterfall as you move sequentially → commonly used in **Waterfall** project management
	- Each task = a horizontal progress bar (length = time allotted); bars stacked → the task at the top must be completed before the one below
	- Lives in the project plan, updated as the project progresses
- **Roadmap**: best for tracking **big milestones**; illustrates how a project should evolve over time to the team and key stakeholders
	- **Goals** at the top (e.g. increase online business-to-consumer sales by **20%** year-over-year; increase holiday sales to existing customers by **8%** over last year) + **approach** (main tactics)
	- **High-level project overview**: a concise summary, usually 3–4 sentences, stating objectives and priorities
	- Table mapped by **quarters** (**quarter**: a three-month period on a company's financial calendar) → key milestones per quarter + tasks per team member or department
	- Example: Q1 milestone = finalize inventory for the holiday season (marketing and sales teams); product and engineering work in Q1 and Q2 toward launching the refreshed online store in Q3 → tasks sometimes done in advance to unblock another team or milestone
- **Burndown chart**: the most granular; measures **time against the amount of work done and remaining**
	- Keeps the team on top of target completion dates and aware of **scope creep** as it occurs
	- Best for projects needing a detailed review of each task and where **finishing on time** is the top priority
	- **y-axis** = number of tasks left; **x-axis** = time; progress tracked from the upper left down toward zero remaining tasks and right toward the end date
	- Dotted line = expected/projected progress; solid line = actual progress
- Choosing: communicate milestones to a large team → roadmap; many dependencies → Gantt chart; tracking tasks against a deadline → burndown chart; you may combine them
	- Elita at Google: Gantt chart to scope work at the beginning, then a burndown chart in the weeks before a launch

#### Choose the right tracking method for your project
- **Gantt charts**: popular, for all types of projects; live in the project plan
	- Useful for: staying on schedule; projects with lots of tasks, dependencies, and milestones; large teams
	- Practice: Asana free trial, Gantt chart templates in Google Sheets or Microsoft Excel; Asana help article "New to Gantt charts? Start here."
- **Roadmaps**: track individual and project progress toward milestones, best for **big milestones**
	- Useful for: **high-level tracking** of large milestones (overall snapshot, like points of interest and mile markers); illustrating how a project should evolve over time
	- Can be built in a document; Smartsheet free trial and Roadmap template (Google Sheets); Smartsheet articles on Project Roadmaps and free Product Roadmap templates
- **Burndown charts**: typically used by **Agile Scrum** teams → show how quickly the team is working (work left vs. time remaining) (→ [Course 5: Agile Project Management](/blog/agile-project-management/))
	- Main uses: keep the team on top of targeted completion dates, make them aware of **scope creep**; display it so everyone can see it and update it regularly
	- Useful for: projects that need a detailed review of tasks; projects where finishing on time is the top priority
	- Helps the PM understand how the team works and what influences their ability to finish tasks on time → address issues early, plan the next project better
	- Jira free trial and Burndown chart template; Jira help article and ProjectManager.com article on burndown charts

#### Project status reports
- **Project status report**: an overview of all the project's common elements summarized in a **snapshot** → an efficient communication tool for the team and stakeholders
- Key components:
	- **Project name**: specific to the purpose so the overall goal is understood at a glance
	- **Date**: reports created weekly or monthly (depends on stakeholders' needs and project pace) → reference point + history log of the project's status
	- **Summary**: goals, schedule, highlights, lowlights in one place; followed by or grouped with the timeline summary and overall status
	- **Status**: actual vs. planned progress → **RAG (red, amber, green)** or Red-Yellow-Green status reporting (traffic light pattern)
		- **Red**: issues need resolution; project may be delayed or go significantly over budget
		- **Amber/Yellow**: potential issues with schedule or budget, likely resolvable with corrective actions
		- **Green**: schedule and budget fine, project on track
		- Use for overall project status and milestone status; make sure everyone understands what each color means for your project
	- **Milestones and tasks**: in the project plan items are "not started", "in progress", "completed"; in the status report summarize them into **key accomplishments** (what happened) and **upcoming** (next big milestones)
	- **Issues**: current roadblocks and potential risks → set expectations; if red or amber, flag what's preventing progress, state your plan to get back to green, ask for resources or help
- Formats: complex project for the team → **spreadsheet**; updates for senior stakeholders → **slideshow** with only the key points
- Key takeaways: improve and simplify communication; keep everyone informed; request resources and support; create structure and transparency in a centralized place

### Managing changes, risk and dependencies
#### Why risks and changes occur
- **Risk**: a potential event that might occur and could impact your project → hypothetical, but the PM must identify and plan for them (→ [Course 3: risk management](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- Examples: a contractor missing a deadline, a tool that leads to communication breakdown, unexpected work because of an unforeseen policy
- When any risk occurs, the consequence is a **change** to the project plan
- **Change**: anything that alters or impacts the tasks, structures, or processes within a project → typically unexpected, more often negative, *sometimes* positive
	- Any variance from the original plan regarding the **triple constraint**: priorities and scope, budget and resources, timeline
- Types of changes (home renovation example):
	- **New or changing dependencies** (**dependencies**: tasks, activities, or milestones reliant on one another) → a new sink can't be installed until the vanity and plumbing are in place
	- **Changing priorities** → the client's in-laws move in, so the spare bedroom work moves ahead of the bathroom remodel
	- **Capacity and people available** → replace the plumber because of issues on the job site
	- **New limitation on budget or resources** → reduce bathroom design costs by 10% because electrical quotes came in higher
	- **Scope creep**: changes, growth, and other factors affect the project scope → the client wants new tile in all bathrooms (→ [Course 2: controlling scope creep](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
	- **Force majeure**: an unforeseen circumstance that prevents someone from fulfilling a contract due to a major (national or international) crisis → a union strike, a pandemic halting production; uncommon
- Measure changes against the **baseline estimates** of scope, budget, and time → watch for **knock-on effects** (e.g. the client's "hardwood floors" under old carpet turn out to be rotting → timeline and budget take a hit)
- Managing changes is the PM's responsibility (but not alone) → refer to the **Statement of Work** and **RACI chart**; know the process for requesting changes
- **Change request form**: used by you and stakeholders to manage changes; must be self-explanatory and thorough (template: a 2-by-10 table)
	- Project name; discussion owner; discussion type (risk, opportunity, ...); teams involved; expected outcome (change in priorities, schedule change, official call on an issue); target date for discussion; milestones or goals impacted
	- Short description of the current situation, the change, and the difference to the **plan of record** (before and after snapshot) → in-depth proposal with **trade-offs** → background information
	- If milestones are at risk → get **customer sign-off** before changing scope, deadline, or budget, and inform all parties
#### Identifying and tracking dependencies
- **Dependencies**: the links that connect one project task to another → often the **greatest source of risk** to a project; like a line of dominoes (→ [Course 3: visualizing dependency relationships](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- Types (construction company example):
	- **Internal dependency**: relationship between two tasks within the same project → choose the foreman and PM, sign off requirements, timeline, budget before choosing the crew
	- **External dependency**: tasks reliant on outside factors (regulatory agencies, other projects) → wait for city approval before demolishing; not always in the PM's control
	- **Mandatory dependency**: tasks legally or contractually required → pour the concrete foundation and have it inspected by the city before continuing to build
	- **Discretionary dependency**: defined by the project team → test-pour a portion of the foundation with concrete from a new supplier to estimate the total amount needed
- **Dependency management**: the process of managing all interrelated tasks and resources within the project to ensure it is completed successfully, on time and in budget
	1. **Proper identification**: brainstorm all possible dependencies with the team and categorize them
	2. **Recording dependencies**: create a **risk register** (a table or chart with your list of risks and dependencies) → description, date, all activities or tasks that may be impacted
	3. **Continuous monitoring and control**: regular meetings to check on interrelated tasks and changes that impact other tasks
	4. **Efficient communication**: keep the team and stakeholders updated
#### Techniques to help manage risks
- **Risk management**: the process of identifying potential risks and issues which could impact a project, then evaluating and applying steps to address their effects
- Manage **changes and dependencies** and **scope creep** → other risks become easier (dependencies met on time → less likely to fall behind; tight scope → fewer budget or timeline changes)
- **Brainstorming** with the team: one of the most effective techniques for identifying risks (teammates' experience from previous projects)
	- Create a **risk register**; ask what could improve the outcome or hurt/hinder it → list risks as **if/then statements**
- **Risk exposure**: a way to measure the potential future loss resulting from a specific activity or event → used to prioritize risks
	- Matrix with two variables: **risk impact** (horizontal axis, top) and **probability** (vertical axis, side), each marked high, medium, low → place each risk at the cross section
	- Anything with **high impact**, even with low probability → have a **mitigation plan** in place
- **ROAM technique**: **Resolved, Owned, Accepted, Mitigated** → manage actions after risks arise (it's almost impossible to account for every risk)
	- **Resolved**: risk eliminated, won't be a problem
	- **Owned**: a team member takes ownership and handles it, monitored through to completion
	- **Accepted**: agreed that nothing will be done about it
	- **Mitigated**: action taken to reduce the likelihood or the impact
	- Then the team discusses each risk and decides which to prioritize
#### Case study: Using risk management tools
- **Paw Snacks**: online retailer of pet treats, grew from a startup to a 350-person organization in three years → launching a new line of treats for puppies
- **The issue**: six weeks before launch, the commercial bakery tells PM **Naja** that the bone-shaped cookie cutters haven't arrived; baking must start the next day; teammate **Abe** learns the order is delayed by a product shortage → arrives **two days** late
	- The launch date can't move: marketing already bought **nonrefundable advertising placements** for launch day
- **Planning ahead**: months earlier the team brainstormed risks into a **risk register** (often paired with a **probability and impact matrix**) → cookie cutter delay = **medium probability, high impact** → Abe created a **mitigation plan** (steps to decrease the chances of a risk occurring or its impact), approved by the sponsor and stakeholders
- **Managing the issue**: two options in the plan → (1) work with the bakery to slightly increase production to make up the two days; (2) order from a second bakery → chose option 1
	- Brainstormed risks of the new plan: a smaller order → minor but manageable impact on projected growth for 2021 → **accept the risk**; met with the sponsor to communicate and recommend accepting → approved; Abe communicated the plan to the bakery → launch on time
- Pro tip: **mitigation plan** vs. **contingency plan** (often used interchangeably)
	- **Mitigation plan**: a planned risk response strategy for known risks to schedule, cost, or scope, made early in the project
	- **Contingency plan**: mostly related to **funds** kept aside (outside the planned project budget) to support known risk responses that go beyond the planned amount or to manage unforeseen risks during execution
- Key takeaway: risk management planning before work starts → respond to problems quickly

### Communicating issues to stakeholders
#### Escalating issues
- **Escalation**: the process of enlisting the help of higher-level project leadership or management to remove an obstacle, clarify or reinforce priorities, and validate next steps (→ [Capstone: escalating problems](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
	- Not negative in project management → should be encouraged, used often, even celebrated
- Benefits: a **checks and balances** tool; speedy decision-making; an objective third party can make the call when two team members can't agree; encourages participation → trust and shared responsibility
- Before work starts, the PM, the team, and the **project sponsor** establish **escalation standards and practices**: who issues are raised to, how, and the forum for discussion
- When to escalate: at the first sign of **critical problems** → delay to a major milestone, budget overruns, loss of a customer, pushed-back completion date → anything affecting the **triple constraint** (time, budget, scope)
- Escalation prevents:
	- **Trench wars**: two peers or groups can't agree and neither will give in → standstill, delays
	- **Bad compromises**: two parties settle on a so-called solution but the end product still suffers → compromise while keeping the larger project goals in mind; help make hard choices for the greater good
#### Communicating changes to the team
- Even the smallest changes are meaningful to someone and should be communicated → tailor tactics to the subject and recipient
	- Elita's preference: a quick coffee or hallway chat, then a follow-up email noting what was agreed (useful for quick agreement or sensitive subjects)
- **Small change affecting an individual** → email (avoid emotional topics or anything needing in-depth discussion; give a heads-up and set a meeting time); weekly meetings may not be needed if the agenda is short → pivot to email or another forum
- **Big change** affecting more than one person and likely to change budget, deadline, or scope → **team meeting**
- **Timeout**: taking a moment away from the project to take a breath, regroup, and adjust the game plan → may disrupt momentum but sets you up for long-term success
	- E.g. the client wants to redefine scope; team members reassigned and need backfill
- **Retrospectives**: meetings held throughout the project life cycle to discuss successes, setbacks, and future improvements
	- Focus on identifying the **contributing causes** of an incident or pattern of incidents **without blaming** one individual; assume everyone had good intentions and did the right thing with the information they had
#### Writing an effective escalation email
- Set **escalation standards** with stakeholders before the project begins; some problems (major budget or timeline changes) need stakeholders' final decision → a clear, direct email
- Five keys of an effective escalation email (→ [Capstone: escalation email](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/)):
	1. **Maintain a friendly tone**: open with goodwill ("I hope you're doing well"), **blameless tone**, friendly and professional, thank the recipient
	2. **State your connection to the project**: name, role, relationship (one sentence); if they know you, reinforce your responsibility
	3. **Explain the problem**: enough context but concise; avoid long dense paragraphs
	4. **Explain the consequences**: how the issue negatively impacts (or could impact) the project
	5. **Propose a course of action and make a request**: the central piece → solution(s) + what you need from the recipient
- Example: **Sayid**, PM at a gift basket company (Holiday Scents product line, launch in October)
	- Subject: "[Action required] Decision needed to make progress on Holiday Scents project"
	- Problem: ordered **5,000 candles** from Candlemakers, Inc. by Friday; received 3,000, many fail quality standards (damaged packaging, broken candles)
	- Consequences: customer satisfaction at risk; launch postponed **three weeks**; additional cost of **$20,000**
	- Request: sourced two backup suppliers (five-star reviews, on-time track record) → meet tomorrow to onboard one
- These best practices also help write effective prompts for a **generative AI** tool

## Module 2: Quality management and continuous improvement
### Understanding quality management
#### Introduction: Quality management and continuous improvement
- Module content: managing quality and meeting **quality standards**; **quality assurance** and **quality control**; **continuous improvement** with data-driven frameworks (**DMAIC**, **PDCA**); conducting **retrospectives**; keeping a positive, **blameless** tone
#### Key quality management concepts
- If any element of the **triple constraint** (time, scope, budget) suffers, overall **quality** suffers too
- "Quality" ≠ "done": the project must meet the customer's standards of quality, not just be completed
- **Quality**: fulfilling the outlined requirements for the deliverable and meeting or exceeding the needs or expectations of your customers
- Four main concepts of quality management (example: **Office Green**, plant decor for offices → **Project Plant Pals**, desk-friendly plants for top clients) (→ [Capstone: quality management concepts](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/)):
	- **Quality standards**: requirements, specifications, or guidelines to ensure products, processes, or services are fit for achieving the desired outcome
		- Set with your team and customer at the **beginning** of the project; well-defined standards → less rework and schedule delays
		- **Reliability standards**: each planter arrives on time and in good condition; suppliers have enough plants to meet demand
		- **Usability standards**: planters won't cause allergic reactions or illness, suitable for all people and animals; website easy to navigate on phone, computer, tablet
		- **Product standards**: supplier meets the brand's look and feel, uses specified materials, delivered intact
	- **Quality planning**: actions to establish a process for identifying which quality standards are relevant to the project and how to satisfy them → plan the procedures
		- Ask: what outcome do customers want? what does quality look like for them? how to meet expectations? how to tell if quality measures lead to success?
		- E.g. plan with the plant provider to **test the durability** of planters
	- **Quality assurance (QA)**: evaluating whether the project is moving toward delivering a high-quality product or service → spans the **whole project life cycle**
		- Regular **audits**, check-ins, and reporting to stakeholders; make sure you and clients get exactly what was contracted (inspect planter options, sit in on durability testing, track the provider's progress)
	- **Quality control (QC)**: techniques to ensure quality standards when a problem is identified or the quality plan isn't executed as desired → **corrective actions**
		- Monitoring project results and delivery; also helps the next project land better
		- E.g. a final walk-through of offices to swap broken planters or plants damaged in transit
- Quality plan + QA throughout + QC corrections → high-quality deliverable that exceeds customer expectations
#### Recap: Quality management concepts
- **Quality standards**: must be met for the product, process, or service to be considered successful by the organization and the customer; set at the beginning
- **Quality planning**: identify which standards are relevant and how to satisfy them; plan procedures
- **Quality assurance (QA)**: a review process evaluating whether the project is moving toward high quality; regular audits
- **Quality control (QC)**: monitoring results and delivery; a **subset of QA** activities
	- QA seeks to **prevent defects before** they occur; QC aims to **identify defects after** they have happened and take corrective action
- Additional reading: What Are Quality Standards?; What Is a Quality Plan?; Quality Assurance & Quality Control
- Recap: Quality management concepts: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/fvoTU
#### Fostering customer relationships with communication skills
- Communication is the lifeblood of the project, from before it begins through the end
	- Project Management Institute: most projects experience a communication breakdown, even though PMs spend about **90%** of their time on communication
- Soft skills with customers: **negotiation**, **empathetic listening**, **trust building** → a key practice is **asking open-ended questions** and actively listening to understand the customer's **current state vs. desired state**
- Set clear expectations about when you'll communicate (e.g. **weekly progress updates**)
	- Not every issue needs to reach the customer (e.g. replacing a designer who quit without skipping a beat); use judgment on visibility
	- If you can't move forward without their input → communicate calmly and with **empathy**
- Plant Pals example: acceptable broken planters = **2 out of every 50**; a shipment arrives with **5** broken → negotiate: is 5/50 acceptable, or invest in a higher tier of sturdier planters (affects budget, possible trade-offs)? → goal: **customer satisfaction**
- Past customer service experiences show what good service looks like (going back to the same hair salon, bakery, coffee shop)
- **Feedback** from customers → iterate on the product; timing planned in initiation (during or after the project)
	- E-commerce site → user feedback to optimize the shopping experience; on-demand cookie delivery → feedback after delivery
	- User feedback closes gaps between the customer's expectations and the project's needs
#### Measuring customer satisfaction
- Best way to know what customers want: ask them → surveys or tests
- **Feedback surveys**: users give feedback on features they like or dislike → during design/before launch (do people like and understand it?) or after launch (improve the user experience) → launch or iterate
- **User acceptance test (UAT)**: a test that helps a business make sure a product or solution works for its users; must meet agreed requirements and deliver expected results
	- Assesses the **end-to-end experience**; near the end of development; sometimes called **beta tests**
	- UAT agenda: welcome users → present the product (testing guidelines, demo) → UAT test cases through **critical user journeys** (**critical user journey**: a sequence of steps a user follows to accomplish tasks in your product) → collect feedback → identify **edge cases** → recap findings, identify bugs or issues, prioritize → close
	- Give users a visual representation, mock-up, or demo (construction: 3D models, digital blueprints, samples)
	- Focus on a **call to action**: e.g. homeowners want a dishwasher that opens with little force and is quiet → load dishes, start the cycle, "On a scale of 1 to 10, how much force was required?"
- **Edge cases**: rare outliers the original requirements didn't account for → extreme maximums and minimums of parameters
	- E.g. an app allowing unlimited photo uploads (users rarely upload more than **1,000** per session) → what if someone uploads thousands or millions?
#### Ensuring accessibility during feedback collection
- **Holly** (from an earlier course on accessibility, → [Course 2: Accessibility for project managers](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/)): make feedback collection and quality measurement fair and **accessible**
- **Live interviews**: offer **accommodations** when setting up sessions (live captioning, an interpreter, questions in advance for people with anxiety or on the autism spectrum); what works for one person may not work for another with the same disability
- **On location**: accessible path into the building and room, hallways clear of clutter (wheelchair, walker, visual impairment)
- **Surveys / technology**: check systems are fully accessible → ask the system owner about compliance with the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)**; be ready to collect responses in an alternate manner
- Make accessibility part of the conversation **from the beginning** → otherwise launch delays or a product unusable by part of the population
	- Developers familiar with accessibility requirements at the start; include **testers with various disabilities** in usability testing; at least test adherence to accessibility guidelines
#### User acceptance testing: Goals, best practices, and management
- Main objectives of **UAT**: show the product, service, or process behaves as expected in **real-world scenarios**, works as intended, and identify issues before project completion
	- Answers: do users recognize its purpose? how do they interact with it? how much time? do they notice all features? is it accessible to everyone? + how users **feel** (emotions, identities, appeal)
- Best practices:
	- **Define and write down acceptance criteria** (**acceptance criteria**: pre-established standards or requirements a product, service, or process must meet) → e.g. a new employee handbook must be a digital PDF accessible on mobile and desktop
	- **Create test cases** for each item (**test case**: a sequence of steps and its expected results) → e.g. download the handbook PDF on mobile or desktop
	- **Select users carefully** → actual end users
	- **Write UAT scripts based on user stories** (**user story**: an informal, general explanation of a feature written from the end user's perspective) → "As a new employee, I want to use the handbook to easily locate the vacation policy and share it with my team via email"
	- **Communicate with users** about what to expect
	- **Prepare the testing environment**: credentials and access, tested ahead of time
	- **Step-by-step plan** to guide users (digital document or spreadsheet shared in advance)
	- **Compile notes in a single document** and record issues, including users' opinion on severity → prioritize fixes
- Managing UAT feedback (positive comments, bug reports, change requests):
	- **Bugs or issues**: track in a spreadsheet, prioritize → critical (can't access, download, or search the handbook) before non-critical (cover art)
	- **Change requests**: manage and prioritize; share with primary stakeholders; may need to adjust the timeline
- Key takeaway: leave time in the schedule for proper testing and issue resolution

### Pursuing continuous improvement
#### Continuous improvement and process improvement
- **Continuous improvement**: an ongoing effort to improve products or services → steadily toward the best possible outcome
	- Starts by recognizing when processes and tasks need to be **created, eliminated, or improved** → plan and implement changes
- **Process improvement**: the practice of identifying, analyzing, and improving existing processes to enhance team performance, develop best practices, or optimize consumer experiences
- Controlled environment / experiment:
	- **Control**: an experiment or observation designed to minimize the effects of variables
	- **Control groups**: representative samples that show the differences between experimental groups and the norm are due to your changes → eliminate alternative explanations
	- **Hypothesis**: an educated guess about what's causing the problem and how you'd fix it → change **one variable**, keep the control group the same, observe results
- Plant Pals example: demand grows, suppliers ship all plants in a **one-size-fits-all box** → small plants get more padding and arrive intact; large plants are squeezed and sometimes arrive damaged (per customer surveys)
	- Hypothesis: would more large plants arrive intact in bigger boxes with the same padding?
	- Half of large plants in original boxes (**control group**), half in bigger boxes; everything else identical (shape, thickness, supplier, delivery addresses) → new survey → confirm the hypothesis or try something else
#### Data-driven improvement frameworks
- **Data-driven improvement frameworks**: techniques used to make decisions based on actual data
- **DMAIC** (**Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control**) → five steps toward continuous improvement (→ [Course 1: Lean and Six Sigma, DMAIC](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/))
	1. **Define**: business problem, goals, resources, project scope, timeline
	2. **Measure**: performance metrics and data collection → baselines, measure success
	3. **Analyze**: find **root causes** of problems and their impact
	4. **Improve**: implement a reasonable solution
	5. **Control**: implement the changes and monitor the updated processes
- **PDCA** (**Plan, Do, Check, Act**): a four-step process → identify a problem, fix it, assess whether the fix worked, fine-tune the fix
	1. **Plan**: identify the issue and root cause, brainstorm solutions → e.g. one plant species isn't selling, the warehouse is full and the plants may die → move it from the bottom to the top of the sales page, or an email campaign with a buy-one-get-one offer
	2. **Do**: fix the problem → the sponsor doesn't want to give plants away → move the plant to a prominent spot on the website (hypothesis: put old inventory where customers can't miss it)
	3. **Check**: compare results to the goal → wait one week, see if sales go up
	4. **Act**: fine-tune the fix → reorganize the website so all overstocked plants get a prominent place
- Both frameworks help identify issues, reduce errors, and optimize processes, at work and in daily life
#### Differentiating projects from programs
- **Project**: one single-focused endeavor; **program**: a collection of projects; **portfolio**: a collection of projects and programs across the whole organization
	- Projects *can* exist inside programs, which *can* exist inside portfolios (projects can also be separate, unrelated initiatives); all rely on **continuous improvement**
- Roles (names vary by company):
	- **Project manager**: oversees individual projects, **short-term, concrete deliverables**
	- **Program manager**: supervises groups of projects (and other PMs), **long-term business objectives**
	- **Portfolio manager**: supervises a grouping of projects and programs, **centralized management**
- Example: a PM offers monthly **cross-departmental trainings** (coverage when someone is out of office) → also act as inadvertent team-builders → the program manager rolls them out across all projects (program-wide improvement)
- Office Green: launching Plant Pals = a **project** (short-term, temporary) → keeping the service running indefinitely = a **program** (long-term business objective) → Plant Pals plus other projects and programs = the company's **portfolio**
	- PDCA fix for overstocked plants (featured at the top with a small discount) → becomes a best practice and a new process → applied company-wide for other sites and products → less waste, more revenue; benefits the program and the portfolio's profitability

### Using retrospectives for project success
#### The purpose of a retrospective
- **Retrospective** (**retro**): a workshop or meeting that gives project teams time to reflect on a project → throughout the life cycle, mostly after major milestones or (most commonly) after the project is completed; a form of **process improvement** (→ [Capstone: retrospectives](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
- Three main purposes:
	1. **Team building** → understand different perspectives
	2. Improved **collaboration** on future projects
	3. **Positive changes** in future procedures and processes (instead of recycling old, potentially bad habits)
- When: something falls through the cracks; missed deadlines or expectations; miscommunications between stakeholders; end of a **sprint** (a series of ordered tasks ending in a goal); after product launches and landings → record key lessons for others
- Format varies: formal in-person (sticky notes, documents) or virtual/online (surveys help organize thoughts)
- Best practices:
	- **Blameless** → everyone comfortable giving candid feedback; anonymous or private feedback for sensitive subjects
		- **Changing perspective**: put yourself in someone else's shoes (blaming the delivery company for late plants → was their route optimized and tested? maybe that should have been a task)
		- Switch from **"you" language to "we" language**: "You didn't make it clear we had no contingency budget" → "The lack of a contingency budget wasn't made fully clear from the get-go, and that's something we can improve upon next time"
	- Don't focus only on the negative → what went well, what was fun, what to carry forward (sales and marketing bonded; hire the contracted website designer full-time); celebrate (dinner or dessert)
	- **Enact the changes** discussed → otherwise people won't want to participate
- Xem thêm: [Course 5: Sprint Retrospective](/blog/agile-project-management/) – retrospectives as a Scrum event
#### Conducting a retrospective
- No exact formula; consider your team's needs
- Before you begin: maintain a **positive tone**; be considerate of **teams outside your own** (involve partner teams or share findings; cross-team interactions and deliverable handoffs are frequent topics)
- Retrospective template (standard document to guide the conversation):
	- Go over the **chain of events** in order: planning stage (what could have gone better? where did we get lucky?), execution stage
	- **Lessons learned**: what to do differently next time, which risks materialized, gaps between plan and execution, how the team felt
		- E.g. the website launch missed its deadline → sales missed monthly numbers, marketing changed dates on content and ads, the sponsor faced eager investors → next time prioritize a task with so many dependencies
	- **Action items** table: "What action should we take as a result of our lessons learned?" → action item, **type** (tool, process, team, ...), **owner**, relative **links** (where it's tracked)
	- **Future considerations** table: risks that could become issues next quarter; passing off ownership → type, **contact** (resource for later), links (documentation, risk register)
- Can be more interactive: color coding, sticky notes, columns with emojis → carry lessons learned into the next projects
#### Gernot: Using retrospectives to get back on track

- Projects go off track almost daily → retrospectives as a **continuous exercise** along the way, not only at the end
- Example: halfway through a Google Search launch, the team found a better way with much bigger user benefits → got the whole team together: what's done so far, current state, options to address shortcomings → collectively **replanned** toward an adjusted definition of success
- Be flexible: continuing in a bad direction is the worst thing → quickly analyze options and course-correct, while making it a **data-driven decision** with stakeholders and sponsors on board
- Most important to track: tightly manage **scope**, **resources**, **timeline**; compare progress to the original plan and course-correct

## Module 3: Data-informed decision-making
### Gathering data for a project
#### Introduction: Data-informed decision-making
- Questions: how do I use data in my project? which types of data to look for? what do stakeholders need to know?
- Data is part of daily life (athletes' stats, social media screen time, health monitoring); e.g. online job search sites use your experience, location, degrees, certificates to suggest matching jobs
- Module content: project data informs decisions and prevents major and minor issues; reaching **success metrics**; common project data (tasks, issues) and how analyzing it impacts productivity and quality; **storytelling with data** to convince stakeholders how to resolve problems
#### The value of data
- **2.5 quintillion bytes** of data are created each day
- **Data**: a collection of facts or information → through **data analysis** you draw conclusions, make predictions and decisions
	- E.g. a runner uses GPS watch data (time, distance, **minutes per kilometer** pace) to track performance over time
	- Companies source data on customer behavior and demand → better services, new products; **Netflix** uses genres, ratings, repeated viewings to recommend shows
- PMs use data daily to make better decisions, solve problems, understand performance, improve processes, understand users
	- Plant Pals: buying patterns show best sellers are all **tropical plants** → better decisions on new plant orders, better understanding of user preferences
	- Project tracker data (tasks completed, escalations, issues around an internal process) → find where most issues stem from → focus process improvement
#### Common types of project data
- **Metric**: a quantifiable measurement used to track and assess a business objective → based on selected goals, varies per project
- **Productivity metrics**: measure progress and output over time → effectiveness and efficiency
	- **Milestone**: an important point in the schedule indicating progress, usually completion of a deliverable or phase
	- **Task**: an activity to be accomplished within a set period of time
	- **Projection**: predicting an outcome based on the information you have now (e.g. with current resources, completion in six months)
	- **Duration**: total time to complete a project from start to finish (also for milestones and deadlines); hours, days, weeks, months, years
- **Quality metrics**: relate to achieving acceptable outcomes
	- **Number of changes** → monitors risks; inconsistencies from initial requirements; compounding small changes may be an early warning of a bigger issue
		- **Change log**: a record of all notable changes on a project → explain to stakeholders why something takes longer or costs more
	- **Issue**: a known and real problem that may affect the ability to complete a task (delayed legal approval for an ad; missing confirmed seats to secure a conference venue)
	- **Cost variance**: the difference between the actual cost and the budgeted cost (budgeted for **250** conference attendees, **275** show up and the venue charges extra)
- Tools: **Workfront**, **Jira** (track activity, measure project health); **Tableau** (data visualization)
#### Common data metrics for project management
- Benefits of analyzing data: better decisions, problem solving, improved performance and processes, understanding users (customer buying patterns, project tracker data)
- **Data** = information (numbers and feedback); **metrics** = how you measure data (productivity, quality, engagement); **analytics** = using data to answer questions, discover relationships, and predict unknown outcomes
	- Ask: what do the metrics mean? how will you use them? patterns to make predictions? ways to optimize? lessons learned?
- **Productivity metrics**: progress and output over time
	- Tasks or milestones completed in a time frame; % of tasks on time; how much longer than anticipated → **on-time completion rates** (high = meeting goals, low = missing deadlines)
	- **Duration** → improves the accuracy of timeline estimates
	- **Projections (forecasts)** of productivity trends, durations, costs, performance, quality → e.g. team **velocity**
- **Quality metrics**:
	- **Changes** (differences from what was planned or required) and **issues** (problems that may affect task completion, often result in a change) → identify patterns, refine processes
	- **Cost or budget variance**: low variance = accurate budget estimates; high variance = re-evaluate estimation (under/over-estimating, or not tracking expenses)
- **Happiness metrics** (used at Google, related to quality): aspects of user satisfaction such as visual appeal, likelihood to recommend, ease of use → surveys, revenue, customer retention, product returns
	- **Customer satisfaction scores**: user attitudes, satisfaction, perceived ease of use; usually a combined metric → e.g. appearance **6/10**, ease of use **7/10**, likelihood to recommend **8/10** → overall **7/10**; agree acceptable scores with stakeholders
- **Adoption**: whether a product, service, or process is accepted and used; **engagement**: the degree it is used (frequency, time spent, range of use)
	- Party analogy: adoption = people accepted the invitation and showed up; engagement = how active they were, whether they invited friends, how long they stayed
	- Adoption metrics: conversion rates, **time to value (TTV)**, onboarding completion rates, frequency of purchases, providing feedback, completing a profile
	- Engagement metrics: daily usage rate of a feature, orders, customer interactions
	- Also monitor **stakeholder engagement** (frequency of communication, email responses, meeting attendance, input) and **team member engagement** (more engaged → more productive, higher quality)
	- Want these to increase or at least meet the goals set with stakeholders
- Key takeaway: know which metrics stakeholders care about most and what affects the team's ability to deliver quality on time and within budget

### Prioritizing and analyzing data
#### Discerning important data
- Analogy: normal body temperature **98.6°F**; at **100.4°F** or higher the brain receives signals (sweaty forehead, muscle aches, dehydration) → pay attention to signals that threaten the project's health
- **Signal**: an observable change → helps determine the overall health of the project and spot early signs that something isn't right
- How to determine what data is important:
	- Observe the team's **productivity and output** → which tasks contribute most to the overall goal
	- Prioritize data or metrics **most valuable to stakeholders**
- Example: manufacturing company releasing a portable home appliance by **Q3**; stakeholder worried about the deadline
	- It's Q1, **$2,000 over budget**, but **30 days ahead of schedule** per the burndown chart
	- Tasks increased **10%** over the last three weeks (stakeholders adding features) → team burning out, productivity slowing
	- Stakeholders care more about the deadline than budget → watch signals related to **time and scope** → can meet the deadline if new feature requests stop; use productivity metrics to **forecast** and communicate to the stakeholder
- Keep the **project plan** up to date with priorities and accessible → high-level overview of answers, success criteria, artifacts, project health → avoid resetting expectations again and again
#### Data ethics considerations
- Data from focus groups, interviews, questionnaires often holds **PII (personally identifiable information)**: information that could be used to directly identify, contact, or locate an individual
- **Data ethics**: the study and evaluation of moral challenges related to data collection and analysis (generating, recording, curating, processing, sharing, using) (→ [Data Science course 3: data ethics, privacy and bias](/blog/prepare-data-for-exploration/))
	- Businesses apply it to comply with regulations, show trustworthiness, ensure fair usage, minimize biases, build positive public perception
- **Data privacy**: proper handling of data (purpose of collection and processing, privacy preferences, managing personal data, individual rights) → collect, process, share, archive, delete in accordance with the law
	- **Increase data privacy awareness** (team, vendors, contractors, external stakeholders know the security and privacy protocols)
	- **Use security tools** (encrypted storage, password managers; access settings in Google Workspace, Microsoft OneDrive)
	- **Anonymize data**: **data anonymization** = techniques such as **blanking, hashing, or masking** personal and identifying information → names, telephone numbers, social security numbers, email addresses, photographs, account numbers
- **Data bias**: a type of error that tends to skew results in a certain direction → slanted survey questions, unrepresentative or non-inclusive samples (e.g. no people with disabilities), rushed responses from short answer times
	- **Sampling bias**: a sample not representative of the population as a whole (no one over 65, excluded socioeconomic groups)
	- **Observer bias**: different people observe things differently (stakeholders in different parts of the world draw different conclusions)
	- **Interpretation bias**: interpreting ambiguous situations in a strictly positive or negative way (a team member reads inconclusive survey results negatively)
	- **Confirmation bias**: searching for or interpreting information to confirm pre-existing beliefs (asking only stakeholders likely to share your perspective)
- PMI **Code of Ethics & Professional Conduct**: ethics is about making the best possible decisions concerning people, resources, and the environment → leadership depends on ethical choices
- Data ethics considerations: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/hTEoP
#### Using data analysis to inform decisions
- Personal example: saving for a big purchase → budget shows weekly expenses exceed allowance, mostly eating out → spend less on dining out
- **Data analysis**: the process of collecting and organizing information to help draw conclusions → solve problems, make informed decisions, support goals; reveals insights and patterns
	- PMs look for **repeated behaviors** and solutions based on data predictions
- Ride-share example: high driver demand midweek during **rush hour** in one city → riders can't get pickups
	- Data points: peak traffic times, average daily rider requests, number of available drivers → solution: **incentives** for drivers during peak times → drivers feel appreciated, customer satisfaction increases
- **Quantitative data**: statistical and numerical facts (number of rider requests over time)
- **Qualitative data**: subjective qualities that can't be measured numerically (user feedback about the service) (→ [Data Science course 2: qualitative vs. quantitative data](/blog/ask-questions-to-make-data-driven-decisions/))
#### The six steps of data analysis
- Six main steps: **Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyze, Share, Act** (→ [Data Science course 1: the six phases of data analysis](/blog/foundations-data-data-everywhere/))
- **Ask**: frame the analysis → what is the problem? compare the current state to the ideal state; be specific, focus on the problem not the symptoms
	- Gym losing memberships: "Why do we keep losing members?" → better: "What factors are negatively impacting the member experience?"
	- Identify **stakeholders** and their expectations; clarify what they ask (all types of business risk, or only weather/seasonal risks?)
- **Prepare**: collect and store the data → member surveys in three categories (**upkeep of the facility, customer service, membership cost**) + write-in responses; organized system for tracking and filing
- **Process**: **clean** the data → enter into a spreadsheet or other tool, eliminate inconsistencies, inaccuracies, **duplicate responses**, biased data; check completeness, typos, errors
- **Analyze**: draw conclusions, make predictions, decide next steps → transform and organize data, create visualizations to find trends or patterns
	- **50%** wrote in "outdated equipment", **75%** cited "expensive membership fees" → side by side: the experience isn't worth the price → invest in new equipment
- **Share**: data visualization in a clear, digestible format → insights for data-driven decisions
- **Act**: the business puts the insights into action to solve the original problem

### Presenting and visualizing data
#### Presenting data to tell a project's story
- Presenting = telling the story of your project
- **Storytelling**: the process of turning facts into narrative to communicate something to your audience → brings data to life; beginning, middle, end (→ [Capstone: storytelling with data](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
- Six steps of storytelling (Elita's **Google Maps** example: apply a label to every business in the world with a small team of engineers; audience = VPs of Google Maps and Google Search):
	1. **Define your audience**: sponsors, executives, or team members? what do they want to know, most urgent concerns, which data points influence the story?
	2. **Collect the data**: find data from trustworthy sources that connects to the question (project plan, work management software) → "Where should we focus our attention first?" → internal business database + what types of businesses users search for
	3. **Filter and analyze the data**: vet for credibility → search queries showed **restaurants, cafes, hotels** searched most; gas stations, museums, etc. a much smaller share of geo-specific search traffic
	4. **Choose a visual representation**: dashboards, charts, infographics, mappings → a **pie chart**
	5. **Shape the story**: one cohesive narrative (what to achieve, points to make, questions to answer) → most geo-specific queries are covered by a small number of business categories → improving that category data in a few major markets improves results for **more than 50%** of geo searches → VP agreement
	6. **Gather your feedback**: trial run with someone not connected to the project (interesting? made sense? questions?)
- Key to effective storytelling: be **organized, intentional, and prepared**
#### Data visualization tools
- **Data visualization**: the graphical representation of information to facilitate understanding (graphs, maps, tables) (→ [Data Science course 6: data visualization](/blog/share-data-through-the-art-of-visualization/))
	- Filters information to the most important points, summarizes efficiently, helps viewers make sense of and **remember** information
- **Dashboard**: a type of user interface, typically a graph or summary chart, giving a **snapshot** of project progress or performance → centralized place for stakeholders; summary of metrics, stats, and **KPIs**
	- **KPI (key performance indicator)**: a measurable value or metric that demonstrates how effective an organization is at achieving key objectives → signals progress toward success criteria
	- E.g. goal of a **95%** customer satisfaction score after three months → show the average score instead of a spreadsheet of thousands of survey responses; countdown of days to launch; % of issues resolved
	- Can summarize project plans, documents, reports (% of tasks in progress, complete, not started)
- **Burndown chart**: a line chart measuring time against work done and work remaining (outstanding work on the vertical axis, time horizontal)
- **Column charts**: compare different activities or progress over time (e.g. number of customers and plants delivered year-over-year)
- **Pie charts**: composition, **parts-to-whole** relationship
- **Infographics**: visual representations of information (data or facts), typically a **"one-pager"** or **"one-sheeter"** at Google → concise summaries mainly through graphics, with added text; communicate without extra explanation when you're not present
- Use visuals for changes over time, frequency, relationships/correlations, value and risk analysis; make visualizations **accessible**
#### Different ways to visualize data
- First be clear on what you want to show: a new trend, relationships between data sets, comparing values, composition, trends and behaviors over time
- **Show relationships** → **scatter plot** (scatter chart/graph): dots for values of two variables; a **trend line** across the center shows the direction
	- E.g. happiness score (y-axis) vs. life expectancy (x-axis) → as happiness increases, so does life expectancy
	- Best practice: start the **y-axis at 0**
- **Comparing values** → **bar graphs**: size contrast to compare two or more values (time of day vs. level of motivation: low at the start of the workday, higher by the end); clarify trends, identify patterns
	- Best practices: consistent colors; accent colors for important points or changes; horizontal labels
- **Demonstrating composition** → **pie chart**: how much each part makes up the whole (activities in a day, half spent working)
	- Best practices: avoid too many categories; slices add up to **100%**; order slices by size
- **Analyzing trends and behaviors** → **line graphs**: change over time (popularity of cats vs. dogs, two line colors; dogs always higher, general upward trend)
	- Best practices: no more than **four categories**; put highly variable data at the top

#### Effective presentation techniques
- Example speaker: **Brené Brown** (research professor at the University of Houston), 2010 TED Talk *The Power of Vulnerability* with **60 million** views → teaches leaders about courage and empathy
- Data alone isn't enough to convince people; many chances to present (kickoff meeting, weekly status updates, final project presentation)
- Ask: what do I want my audience to **know, think, or do** as a result? → big picture, keep it simple
- Three ways to give an effective presentation:
	- **Precise**: identify the problem you're solving, remove content that dilutes the narrative → **"designing for five seconds"**: the audience should understand a slide within five seconds
	- **Flexible**: stakeholders may leave or arrive late → be ready to shorten from an hour to 30 or even 5 minutes; prepare ahead (practice with your team, anticipate questions and objections)
	- **Memorable**: tie in data analysis and visualization; use stories and repetition
- Body language: upright posture, hands at your side, elevate your tone for emphasis, intentional pauses, speak at about **half your normal speed**, short sentences, eye contact, warm facial expressions; have **confidence**
#### Preparing an effective presentation
- **Preparation**:
	- Be clear on goals and purpose → "We need two engineers who have worked in this industry before" instead of "We need more resources"
	- Seek input from your manager or stakeholders; understand what the requestor hopes to gain
	- **Delivery plan**: a **headline** for each slide (one-sentence main point) + a couple of supporting points (anecdotes, charts, data); **signposts** to show where you're going; limit slides; hidden **backup slides** for challenges, difficult questions, trade-offs, alternatives
	- Respect the audience's time: invite only those who need to be there; send the presentation ahead
	- Make it memorable: stories, repeat key points; a **strong intro** (nerves are highest at the start)
- **Practice**:
	- Guide the audience ("Building on this point...", "As I mentioned before...")
	- **Mock presentation** with the team; coordinate co-presenters and handoffs; practice a **Q&A** (including questions you can't answer); be ready to run the whole meeting yourself
	- Practice in front of a mirror or record yourself
	- Be prepared for surprises: summarize quickly if time runs short, pivot to what matters most
- **Presentation and pace**: get right to the point (problem up front, why you're there, what you'll cover); set ground rules for questions; check your pace using audience clues
- **Follow up**: email with summary notes, action items, time frames; debrief with your manager or key audience members; review next steps
- Preparing an effective presentation: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/qz5H4
#### Making presentations accessible
- **Clear, simple slides**: avoid too many graphics, text, or animation (harder for people with visual or cognitive impairments); don't make important content disappear; avoid flashing or flickering animation (distracting, can trigger **seizures**)
	- Use at least one slide with your main points → voice-only talks are hard for people with language barriers or hearing or cognitive impairments
- **Alt text** (alternative text) for images, drawings, diagrams → for screen readers (Google Slides or PowerPoint: select the object, right-click, "alt text"); state the takeaway of data-heavy charts on the slide or in speaker notes
- **Use text for critical information**: never rely only on color (color-blind or unable to see the screen) → add a textual cue such as "new"; written summary at the end of image-heavy presentations
- **Captions** for audio and video (check YouTube automatic captions, request closed captioning); **real-time captioning** (deaf or hard-of-hearing, diverse accents and languages, fast speakers, microphone issues, chatty audiences)
- **Contrast and text size: more is better** → **contrast ratio** (difference between text and background color), ideal **7:1** (online contrast checkers); bigger text, check from the back of the room; avoid **all caps** (harder for people with dyslexia)
- **Share content in advance** (a few days before) → screen-reading software on their own device; otherwise a bulleted outline; a **glossary** for acronyms and technical terms (helps sign language interpreters and captioners)

## Module 4: Leadership and influencing skills
### Cultivating effective teams
#### Introduction: Leadership and influencing skills
- Module content: value of a high-functioning team; **stages of team development** and managing **team dynamics**; motivating team members with personalized engagement; techniques to **influence** others
#### The necessity of project teamwork
- Being a great PM = supporting people to do their best work and build things they're proud of; the best leaders get people to work together
- **Team** (Google's definition): a group of people who plan, solve problems, make decisions, and review progress in service of a specific project or objective → members rely on each other (e.g. a software engineering team creating one seamless experience)
- **Workgroup**: based on organizational or managerial hierarchy → work coordinated, controlled, or assigned by a single person (e.g. QA testers each assigned a test plan, working independently, coordinating mainly with the manager)
- **Teamwork**: an effective collaborative way of working in which each person is committed to and heading toward a shared goal → maximizes each member's individual strengths
- Why teamwork matters:
	- Fosters **creativity** (diverse perspectives, skills, experiences → better solutions for more diverse user needs)
	- Encourages **accountability** (your tasks impact the rest of the team)
	- Helps you **get stuff done** (big complex projects need capable people)
#### The factors that impact team effectiveness
- Individuals have different motivators (serving the organization, supporting family, working on cool projects) → learn about your people (→ [Capstone: empathy](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
- Google research → five factors of team effectiveness, in order of importance:
	1. **Psychological safety**: an individual's perception of the consequences of taking an interpersonal risk → safe to take risks without being labeled ignorant, incompetent, negative, or disruptive; comfortable seeking differing opinions and resolving conflict
		- Elita's team: **"be direct and kind"** → accountability in a safe, secure space; pitching out-of-the-box ideas to directors without fear
	2. **Dependability**: members are reliable and complete work on time → setting, **negotiating**, and meeting expectations; a two-way relationship (someone with competing deadlines on two projects should feel safe to share constraints)
	3. **Structure and clarity**: an individual's understanding of job expectations, how to meet them, and the consequences of their performance → clear roles, plans, goals; sloppy project tracking → sloppy output and tension; diligent tracking → clarity and unity
	4. **Meaning**: finding a sense of purpose in the work itself or its results (supporting themselves financially, helping the team reach goals, reaching a new community of users)
	5. **Impact**: the belief that the results of one's work matter and create change → help teammates see how they drive impact; milestones show how individual tasks contribute
		- Elita's team (routing in Google Maps): help people get where they're going on time → make that experience incrementally better
### Effective project leadership
#### Leading high-functioning teams
- Ways PMs build **high-functioning teams**:
	- **Create systems that turn chaos into order**: standardized, measurable, repeatable, scalable workflows and processes (e.g. a process for teammates to report a completed task instead of chasing them) → like a connect-the-dots puzzle without numbers
	- **Communicate and listen**: daily or weekly status update emails, regular team meetings (also a space for feedback and questions); some people prefer private channels; learn each person's **communication style** (small talk vs. straight to the point)
	- **Promote trust and psychological safety**: welcome different opinions, stay respectful in challenging conversations; open, inclusive discussions in weekly meetings; model it by asking for help; encourage contributions regardless of role or rank; critiques welcomed from anyone
	- **Demonstrate empathy and create motivation**: "no I in team" but teams are individuals with lives outside work → be present, listen, ask questions, avoid assumptions in one-on-ones; **recognize** good work publicly (meeting, group email) → good work, not just heroic efforts
	- **Delegate responsibility and prioritize**: delegating lets teammates add value with their skills and frees you to focus on the whole project; prioritizing reduces ambiguity → build consensus, explain your rationale for buy-in
	- **Celebrate team success** throughout and at the end (milestones, positive stakeholder feedback) → group lunch, small gift, congratulatory email → morale and engagement
- The principles of teamwork stay the same as you lead bigger, more complex teams (→ [Course 1: leadership and team dynamics](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/))
#### Providing "air cover" to your team
- **Air cover**: support for and protection of a team in the face of out-of-scope requests or criticism from leadership → sometimes prioritize the team's needs over stakeholders' wants
	- Requires a **trusting relationship** with stakeholders; risky if the team still can't deliver → be confident in the team's progress first
- Case study: PM for a supermarket coffee brand launching three flavors (**vanilla, hazelnut, mocha**); well into execution, the sponsor asks to add **caramel** → team already at maximum capacity
- **Saying "No" without explicitly saying "No"**:
	- Gently push back: not possible under current constraints (scope, time, cost)
	- Politely offer to get back to them → time to understand and consult trusted teammates (they may reconsider or forget)
	- Always offer **alternative solutions** → e.g. a fourth flavor only by pushing the launch back **two months**
	- Gentle pushback + alternatives protects the team while preserving the relationship
- **Intervening from behind the scenes**: don't share the request with the whole team (avoid extra stress) → consult **two trusted team members** → one knows two new flavors are slated for the **fall lineup in six months** → sponsor accepts launching caramel in the fall
	- Loop in teammates on a **need-to-know** basis
- Key takeaway: balance the needs of stakeholders and the team; it takes practice
- Providing "air cover" to your team: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/gH4jD
#### Emilio: Learning to lead
- Emilio Garcia: **responsible innovation program manager** at Google, from Orange County, California
- Grew up spending summers at the school of his mother (teacher → assistant principal → principal) → her leadership style: connect with people so they feel **seen and empowered**, working toward a collective vision
- Advice: **"deference gives you the experience to be able to lead"** → PMs don't have all the technical or domain expertise; like a principal working with custodial staff, PTA, teachers, the union, parents → build a network that helps you get things done
#### Team development and managing team dynamics
- Psychologist **Bruce Tuckman**'s five stages of team development → from a disparate group to a high-functioning unit:
	1. **Forming**: everything shiny and new, people getting to know each other, eager and excited → PM clarifies project **goals, roles, and context** (people seek guidance)
	2. **Storming**: work begins, people interact more and disagree; frustration with inefficient processes or teammates, complex tasks; disagreements on time and effort estimates, independence, priorities → PM focuses on **conflict resolution**, listens, shares insights
	3. **Norming**: conflict resolved by establishing **new norms** (processes, workflows) → PM **codifies** the norms and reinforces them (e.g. budget time in the weekly agenda for discussing issue solutions)
	4. **Performing**: the team works seamlessly to complete tasks and reach milestones → PM focuses on **delegating, motivating, providing feedback**
	5. **Adjourning**: project wraps up, team disbands (bittersweet) → PM sets up a **celebration** and makes sure each member knows what's next
- **Team dynamics**: the forces, both conscious and unconscious, that impact team behavior and performance → assess and adjust your management style; especially important in forming and storming
- Why manage team dynamics:
	- Different skill sets, autonomy, competing priorities → forge consensus; clear purposes, goals, dependencies, accountability
	- Creates a collaborative, **psychologically safe** environment → people help each other and accept help → schedules on track; use norming to reach performing faster
	- Helps you **motivate** the team → more contribution, empowerment, calculated risks, innovative solutions
#### Rowena: Delegating the details
- Rowena: Senior Program Manager, Systems Productivity and Reporting Team, Google Cloud (→ [Course 3 instructor](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- PMs are usually not the direct manager → key soft skill: **influencing without authority** (communicate goals, take people along on a shared vision and journey)
- Early mistake: trying to do everything herself and not asking for help → learned to **delegate** and let **SMEs** do what they're good at
	- Mentor's advice: find people in the organization who **enjoy** doing something (even if not their role) and delegate it to them → focus on overall project/program management
- **Psychological safety** brings diversity of thought → **inclusive meetings**: encourage quiet participants; even better, have a coffee with them beforehand and ask what they think of the upcoming topic
- **Sponsorship**: showcase people's best work across the project team (morale); **mentorship**: delegate smaller tasks to junior members to build confidence, or something fun outside their normal work
#### Ethical and inclusive leadership
- **Ethical leadership**: a form of leadership that promotes and values **honesty, justice, respect, community, and integrity** → define and align values within the team, show how they benefit the organization's mission
	- E.g. employees calling for ethical policy changes and positions on current events → forums where employees raise viewpoints, are heard, and get follow-ups
- **Inclusive leadership** (Google): everyone's unique identity, background, and experiences are respected, valued, and integrated into how the team operates → better culture, collaboration, innovation, output
	- **Diversity**: the set of differences each of us possesses, visible or invisible, giving a unique perspective; **inclusion**: what the team does with that diversity
- Three ways to lead inclusively:
	- **Foster a culture of respect**: role model, set the tone, model organizational values, act on misconduct, make it comfortable to raise concerns, recognize contributions regularly
	- **Create equal opportunity to succeed**: everyone has access to information and resources (regular communication, accessible documentation, check-ins); spot work for someone who wants it but shies away from asking (know their aspirations)
	- **Invite and integrate diverse perspectives**: psychological safety; invite thoughts, ideas, concerns regardless of role or rank
- Requires regular practice, whether the team is 2, 20, or 200 people
#### A framework for ethical decision-making
- **Ethics**: the principles of conduct governing an individual or a group → no universal standard; differs by culture, profession, industry, organization → usually an organization's **code of conduct** (e.g. Google's)
- Framework from the **Markkula Center for Applied Ethics at Santa Clara University** (example: a past vendor sends a generous holiday gift just before you select a vendor):
	1. **Recognize an ethical issue**: could the decision negatively impact a person or group? does it go beyond what's legal or efficient?
	2. **Get the facts**: consult the right people and consider all options → check company guidelines on gifts from external parties, or ask HR
	3. **Evaluate alternative actions**: most good and least harm? respects the rights of all stakeholders? treats people equally or proportionally? serves the whole community? acts as the person I want to be? (subjective → ask others) → "decline the gift"
	4. **Make a decision and test it**: imagine the reaction of someone whose opinion you value → discuss with your manager, HR, or a trusted colleague
	5. **Act and reflect on the outcome**: respectfully decline, noting the reason (e.g. no gifts valued over **$20** from vendors or contractors)
- Trust in your decision-making makes it easier to **influence without authority**
- A framework for ethical decision-making: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/ab8b6
### The elements of influencing
#### Steps to effective influencing
- **Influencing**: the ability to alter another person's thinking or behaviors
- Plant Pals example: email a partnership manager at a well-known **chocolate manufacturer** to propose a **bundle deal** (a chocolate bar added to each plant purchase)
- Leadership expert **Dr. Jay A. Conger**'s four steps (→ [Capstone: Conger's influencing steps](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/)):
	1. **Establish credibility**: why should they listen? → from **expertise** and **relationships**
		- "I'm Elita, a lead project manager at Office Green. Your colleague, Alex, passed on your contact information. Alex and I worked together to launch new services..." → expertise (role, past launches) + relationship credibility (shared contact who can vouch)
	2. **Frame for common ground**: how your idea benefits your audience → understand their values → research shows their push to work with lifestyle and wellness brands
	3. **Provide evidence**: hard data + persuasive **storytelling** (numbers need stories) → client surveys where their brand came up again and again
	4. **Connect emotionally**: show emotional commitment and **match their emotional state** → reference their Instagram posts on chocolate and a balanced lifestyle; friendly closer
- Conger's four common mistakes: approaching **too aggressively**; **resisting compromise**; focusing too much on the argument instead of the four steps; assuming agreement in **just one conversation**
#### Using sources of power to influence
- Researcher **Terry Bacon**, *The Elements of Power: Lessons On Leadership and Influence* → power sources fall into two buckets
- **Organizational sources of power**:
	- **Role**: position within the organization or team → PMs have power over the project
	- **Information**: access to and control over information → still ask questions, consider all ideas, share information regularly with the team
	- **Network**: professional and personal connections → shows you can get stakeholder support or budget approval; help colleagues make connections (increases their influence)
	- **Reputation**: how others perceive you overall → be a good collaborator (easy to work with, keep commitments, help others reach their goals)
- **Personal sources of power** ("personal superpowers"):
	- **Knowledge**: expertise, unique abilities and skills, ability to learn
	- **Expressiveness**: ability to communicate (storytelling, connecting emotionally)
	- **History**: level of personal history with another person → built through team relationships
	- **Character**: others' view of your qualities (honesty, integrity, kindness); also trustworthiness, authenticity, wisdom
- Elita's superpower: **knowledge / innate curiosity** → no software engineering background; asks the most experienced engineer to teach her how everything works → lunches build knowledge, personal history, and network
- Identify your own superpowers and the combination of influencing techniques that suits you
#### Chris: Influencing others by demonstrating project impact
- Chris: **Diversity Program Manager** at Google
- To get sign-off, think about **what's in it for them** → distill the impact on the business or consumer → frame the ask to the executive stakeholder
- Everyone has influenced since childhood (playing with friends, favorite meal) → but as an adult, think about the value for the other person
- Example: as a new diversity program manager he saw **redundant programs** → influenced the owners to merge into **one program** → showed examples of the impact on the target population and each person's role; programs "mature into a new identity" rather than stop; took time and relationship-building as the new guy
- Advice: **keep it simple** → they need when and basic how, not the weeds; tailor to the audience (researchers → data points; marketing → splashy high-level elements, target demographics)
- If the work doesn't excite you, it won't excite somebody else
#### Creating an effective influencing statement
- **Influencing** = altering another person's thinking or behaviors; an **influencing statement** opens the conversation and sets the tone
- **Jay A. Conger**, *The Necessary Art of Persuasion* → four steps:
	1. **Establish credibility**: expertise ("a history of sound judgement"; build it through education, research, or an expert) + relationships (trustworthiness over time)
	2. **Frame for common ground**: how the idea benefits the audience → listen and gather information in meetings
	3. **Provide evidence**: numbers paired with **vivid language** (stories, examples, metaphors)
	4. **Connect emotionally**: show emotional investment and match the audience's emotional state
- Example: **Carmen**, PM in HR at a small marketing agency → new onboarding process for **graphic design hires** (few designers, no role-specific info) → a **digital welcome packet** (software installation guidelines, processes, design-specific documents)
	- *Provide evidence*: **80%** of recent graphic design hires rated onboarding negatively; follow-ups show lack of relevant information
	- *Frame for common ground*: graphic design leaders agree it could increase new hires' productivity in their first weeks
	- *Establish credibility*: a similar process in her previous role increased new hire satisfaction by **60%**
	- *Connect emotionally*: joining a new company can be overwhelming → a smoother, personalized experience sets the tone for support

## Module 5: Effective project communication
### Communication tools and techniques
#### Introduction: Effective project communication
- Strong communication is incredibly important to teamwork and managing projects → importance of project communication, how to communicate effectively, methods and tools
#### Communicating in different ways
- The PM connects the team to the information they need → main resource for goals, action items, progress, updates; carry information forward **consistently and coherently** (→ [Course 3: communication plan](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
- Communication hubs: check-ins, meetings, and **project documents** (project plans with schedules, trackers, meeting notes) → be clear about how documents are used, who has access, how often they're updated; plus emails, instant messages, meetings
- The PM coordinates incoming and outgoing information → who needs what information and when
- Communicate important information **multiple times, in multiple ways** → some learn by listening, some by watching, some by doing
- Tools: email and messaging, in-person meetings, video conferencing, work management and collaboration tools
#### Principles of effective email writing
- Four principles:
	1. **State what you want clearly**: request in the **subject line** and within the first two paragraphs; specific **call to action** (reply, review, RSVP); clear, concise sentences; define terms, avoid unknown acronyms
	2. **Keep the content concise**: remove anything that doesn't serve the goal; aim for **"question-less"** and **"self-standing"** emails; know your audience (executives may not read more than a few sentences or click links)
	3. **Structure your writing**: **bullets** (strong action verbs at the start), **labels**, **hyperlinks** instead of lengthy details, a strong **topic sentence**
	4. **Check grammar, punctuation, and spelling** (turn on suggestions in your email app)
- Example: **Issac**'s email about the 2021 Annual Team Building Retreat
	- Original: no date or location, the registration link buried at the bottom, too many links, no bullets or labels, spelling and punctuation errors
	- Revised: date and location up front (**October 13–15, Hotel San Francisco**), Annual Awards Dinner, bold label "Sign up now!", bulleted hyperlinks (retreat, hotel rooms, lakeside activities), correct grammar
- Best practices for using **AI** to write emails:
	- Check your organization's **AI policy** (sensitive information)
	- Use the **prompting framework**: specify the task, provide necessary context, include references as examples (style, tone, format), evaluate your output, iterate for better results
	- Use AI to generate **templates** (e.g. project updates); customize for your audience (the AI output is only a draft)
	- Use AI responsibly: always review outputs for accuracy → you are the **human-in-the-loop**, final judge of accuracy, tone, appropriateness
- Principles of effective email writing: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/xyxE6
#### Common communication tools
- **Email and messaging**: email is standard for business → streamline the number you send
	- Tips: carefully select recipients and know **why** you're sending; subject states what it's about (add urgency or response needed to the subject and first line); short, on topic; put lots of information in a linked or attached doc and summarize; state the specific action and when; avoid slang, acronyms, text shortcuts; appropriate tone (friendly, motivating, clear, specific)
- **Instant messaging** (Google Meet chat, Slack): quick questions or updates, alert someone to check their inbox → reduces back-and-forth
	- Caution: distraction, informality, non-work or sensitive/confidential information, hard to track later; ask people's preferences
- **Virtual meeting tools** (Google Meet, Zoom): easy to access and learn; real-time interaction boosts morale and sense of team for remote work; share files during meetings
- **Work management and collaboration tools** (Google Drive, Asana, Smartsheet): two-way information sharing → team members update progress without extra meetings
	- Track tasks, deliverables, milestones; manage budgets, charts, diagrams, contracts, licenses; keep stakeholders informed; information in context of tasks and timeline; real-time collaboration for remote teams; more efficient than manual tracking (→ [Course 2: project management tools](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
- Caution: sharing confidential or need-to-know information externally (financial and business data, user data, product information) is likely prohibited without explicit permission → control who has access
### Organizing and facilitating project meetings
#### How to organize effective meetings
- Effective meetings are **structured, intentional, collaborative, and inclusive**
- **Structured**: start and end on time; carefully selected attendees (people who contribute or are directly affected; share notes with others); prioritized topics; a designated **note taker**
	- **Timeboxing**: setting a time limit for discussion of a topic, with a buffer of a few minutes → don't overpack the agenda; several topics → a few shorter meetings; adjust pacing after the first few meetings; end early if done
	- Be clear about how, when, and to whom meeting outcomes are shared
- **Intentional**: a clearly stated **purpose** and expectations in the agenda and the invite (make a decision, assign tasks, propose an idea, review last month's sales and set goals, choose event coordinators, have a supervisor prioritize tasks) → send **pre-reading** materials in advance
- **Collaborative** (**collaboration**: people working together to produce or create something):
	- Agenda not just presentations where people are talked at; clear objectives (decision vs. information and discussion)
	- A shared digital meeting document for comments (notes will be shared → active listening and participation)
	- Respect each person's preferred communication style (verbal, chat, meeting notes)
- **Inclusive** (**inclusivity**: the practice or policy of including people who might otherwise be excluded or marginalized):
	- Appoint a **moderator/facilitator** who helps participants ask questions while someone else presents
	- Leave space for quiet participants → go around the room: a **"final flight check"** before everyone leaves
	- Accessible meetings and presentations; for weak internet provide a **phone dial-in** option (Google Meet, Zoom), cameras off is okay
#### Facilitating inclusive and accessible meetings
- Inclusion → connection, people do their best work; meetings can be intimidating → consistent, predictable procedures
- Creating an inclusive environment:
	- **Formalize initial check-ins**: ask each person for an update on work or life; open-ended, varied questions (humor, analogies, "finish the sentence") → "A highlight of my week has been...", "This week I have gratitude for..."; model professionalism, vulnerability, empathy
	- **Give everyone your full attention**: don't interrupt; eye contact, turn toward the speaker; avoid head shaking, looking away, looking at your phone
	- **Help all participants be heard**: if someone is interrupted, redirect attention back; ask those who haven't spoken; speaking up can be daunting (virtual calls, people not in the majority, entry-level employees) → tell them in advance they'll be asked to share; solicit input after the meeting
	- **Help participants share different perspectives**: encourage competing views, aim for at least **three points of view**, follow up for more thoughts
	- **Use images that reflect the diversity of the world** (race, gender, age, ability, culture, religion, location)
- **Accessible**: easily used, accessed, or adapted for use by people experiencing disabilities → consider visual impairments and blindness, hearing loss and deafness, mobility disabilities, neurological disorders
	- **Visual impairments**: presentations with large font (minimum **22 pt**), high-contrast colors, alt text, accessible electronic format shared ahead, describe meaningful graphics; handouts with minimum **18 pt** font, black lettering on white matte paper, simple fonts (no compressed fonts or italics), **1.25 to double** line spacing
	- **Deaf or hard of hearing**: face the person (speech readers); speak clearly at moderate pace and volume without exaggerating; ask for clarification; put all spoken information on slides; closed captions or subtitles on videos (YouTube Help)
	- **Mobility impairments**: ample circulation space, accessible seating throughout, **half-round seating** facing the speaker
	- **Neurological disorders**: agenda or task list in advance; closed captioning on video calls; record the meeting and share it; simple page layouts; break up text with images; avoid moving or flickering content or background audio that can't be turned off
- Key takeaway: inclusive and accessible meetings improve engagement, productivity, and morale
- Facilitating inclusive and accessible meetings: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/6Ypg2
#### Checklist for productive meetings
- Google internal study → productive meetings have: **active participation**, a **clear and concise agenda** that is followed, the **correct attendees**
- **Before the meeting**: share an agenda with purpose and goals; invite only those who need to be there (clarify roles; non-essential = optional); alternate recurring times across **time zones**; cancel if email will do; schedule **shorter meetings** (meetings expand to fill the time); prepare (read materials, review agenda)
- **During the meeting**: state goals at the start, stick to the agenda; review previous action items in recurring meetings; phones and laptops away; **active listening** (verbal: "That makes sense. Tell us more."; non-verbal: nodding, eye contact); give everyone a chance including remote participants → "What does everyone think?" instead of "Does everyone agree?"; start with personal questions ("How was your weekend?"); capture key points, action items, decisions and assign owners
- **After the meeting**: send a recap (decisions, action items, timelines, notes); schedule follow-ups with context; assess the need and frequency of recurring meetings
- Pro tip: if new to a team, learn and apply their typical meeting practices before making major changes
- Checklist for productive meetings: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/eMpLs
#### Common types of project meetings
- Four general types:
	- **Project kick-off**: the official beginning of a project → align the team's understanding of goals with plans and procedures; team members + senior management and key stakeholders for buy-in (→ [Course 3: facilitating a project kick-off meeting](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	- **Status updates**: regular team meetings to align on updates, progress, challenges, next steps; the PM may present performance reports and formal status updates
		- Topics: **task updates** (most urgent tasks, completed vs. open items), **schedule status** (behind, ahead, on pace), **budget status**, **current or anticipated issues** (changes, risks, resources, vendors; especially quality and scope), **action items** (**action item**: a task on your list that needs to be completed → every action item has an **owner and a due date**)
		- Use a relatively fixed agenda and time, keep a tight schedule; hold regularly so the PM can report up-to-date information anytime; cadence depends on complexity, team size, sponsor/client needs
	- **Stakeholder meetings**: get **buy-in and support**; understand stakeholders' challenges and respond
		- One-on-one for depth, or groups focused on the most influential stakeholders (e.g. senior managers of each group); others informed by email or notes
		- Start with a **2–5 minute** project status update; seek feedback; make decisions or resolve major issues with senior leaders and the sponsor (**go/no-go**, choosing options, signing off on investments)
		- More formal, pre-reading materials; frequency depends on the stakeholder's role (e.g. a consultant in a **RACI** vs. just informed) (→ [Course 2: RACI chart](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/))
	- **Project reviews (retrospectives)**: at the end of the project or a phase → lessons learned (what's going well, what to keep doing, what to improve) + **celebrate** the project's success

## Module 6: Closing a project
### Understanding project closure
#### Introduction: Closing a project
- Module content: why closing a project is important (avoids unfavorable scenarios); determining when a project is complete; the closing process; **completing ≠ closing** a project; stakeholders and clients in closing; the team and **retrospectives** in a new context; **celebrating** the team; presentations and documentation the PM needs
#### The importance of project closure
- **Project closing**: the process performed to formally complete the project, the current phase, and contractual obligations (→ [Capstone: closeout report](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
- **Completing ≠ closing**: like a restaurant, finishing your meal doesn't end the experience → you still have to pay the bill
- Three criteria for closing a project:
	1. **Assure all work is done** → review to catch overlooked tasks (e.g. Plant Pals: months later a customer can't find **allergy information** on the site → the allergy documentation task was missed after reprioritizing)
	2. **Ensure agreed-upon project management processes are executed** → administrative work can slip (e.g. the contract with the plant provider was never signed by either party → both vulnerable)
	3. **Formal recognition and agreement** from key stakeholders that the project is complete → otherwise they may still request adjustments (e.g. contracted web developers keep billing hours)
- Closing serves its own purpose like initiation, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling → nothing falls through the cracks; protects the team's effort, time, credibility (→ [Course 1: phases of the project life cycle](/blog/foundations-of-project-management/))
- Types of projects to avoid:
	- **Never-ending project**: deliverables and tasks can't be completed → tasks delegated to people without the skills, poorly communicated deadlines, UAT with too many non-launch-blocking bugs, client unsatisfied despite meeting requirements → protect the **scope**; if the customer wants more, commit to a **follow-up project** and close this one
	- **Abandoned project**: inadequate **handoff** of deliverables → the final deliverable never reaches the customer → plan an adequate handoff or transition
- Improper closing may leave you on the hook for incomplete contracts, incomplete scope, or non-compliant practices
#### Case study: The impact of skipping project closure steps
- Closure is sometimes improperly conducted or skipped → impact on profitability and success; your job isn't done until all closure steps are complete
- **Tilly's Toys**: a small children's toy manufacturer → new interactive **piggy bank** that speaks and plays songs to teach number recognition, counting, adding
	- **Oversight #1 – not all work completed**: the box lacked the safety disclaimer (small parts, not for children under **three**), included in the original **Statement of Work** → new boxes at significant cost, missed the launch date before the holiday season → lost revenue, extended timeline and resources
	- **Oversight #2 – an agreed PM process not completed**: the customer (a regional toy store chain) required all contractors to sign an **NDA**; one educational expert never got it and posted about the toy on social media months before launch → **breach of contract**, significant legal risk
	- **Oversight #3 – no formal recognition that the project was done**: PM **Ames** released the team; the customer then sent additional design changes → too late → the customer was unhappy and might use a different manufacturer
- Skipping closing steps can: impact launch dates; create legal risk; cause significant financial losses; undermine your and your team's credibility; damage the customer relationship → plan for closing like any other phase

### The steps of the closing process
#### The closing process for clients and stakeholders
- Formal closing → stakeholders feel their needs were met; loose ends can hurt relationships with customers, users, vendors and your credibility
- Decide: a **small closing process at the end of each milestone**, a **formal comprehensive closing phase** at the very end, or both → close a milestone formally if it's **final** (won't be readdressed)
	- E.g. Plant Pals **website launch** = a one-time milestone (ongoing maintenance, but not launched again) → hand over deliverables, documentation, notify stakeholders that this portion is closed
- **Closing after each phase or milestone**:
	1. Ensure the project satisfied its **strategic goals** → refer to the **SOW, RFP, risk register, RACI chart**: was all required work done? all issues addressed? all tasks completed? (→ [Course 3: procurement documentation and Statement of Work](/blog/project-planning-putting-it-all-together/))
	2. Put together **closing documentation** (closeout reports), review with team members, review **retrospective** notes → team gets a sense of closure
	3. **Administrative closure of procurement**: close contracts, pay vendors, retrieve final deliverables from contracted workers
	4. **Formally recognize completion** of the phase (an email or a larger meeting)
	5. **Follow-up work**: final feedback, closing surveys, offer support for future issues
- **Closing at the very end**:
	1. Provide **training tools, documentation, and capabilities** (manuals, how-to guides)
	2. Ensure the project satisfied its goals and outcomes (all tasks and deliverables, full scope)
	3. **Document acceptance** from all stakeholders (clients, sponsors) → written proof: retrospectives, a project completion document, formal sign-off
	4. Review all contracts and documentation with the team (SOW, RFP, RACI chart, risk register, procurement documents)
	5. Document **lessons learned** in a formal retrospective (team, other teams, stakeholders, outside vendors)
	6. **Disband and thank** the project team
- **Impact reporting**: a presentation at the end of a project for key stakeholders (typically those from the kick-off meeting) → demonstrate how the project went and the impact of the product or service, on your terms
	- Cover how the project landed on **time, scope, and budget**; when the product or service launched; user feedback; how desired outcomes were achieved
#### Demonstrating project impact to stakeholders
- **Impact reporting**: a presentation or formal report prepared for key stakeholders at the end of a project → shows the impact on the organization against targets set at the beginning (goals, objectives, budget, schedule, **KPIs**) (→ [Capstone: impact report](/blog/capstone-applying-project-management-in-the-real-world/))
	- Answer: what was the problem we were trying to solve, and how did we solve it?
- Key performance areas: (1) the goals and objectives set; (2) how you met them against your **KPIs** (**KPI**: a measurable value that demonstrates how effective a company is at achieving its objectives); (3) **schedule and budget performance** (cost savings, efficiencies, deadlines met, within budget)
- Metrics to showcase results: improvement in schedule performance, revenue growth, positive **ROI**, increased external user counts, increased % of internal users, cost vs. margins, high customer satisfaction, reduced overhead, fewer technical issues, time saved → collect data throughout, pair with visuals tied to larger goals
- Effective impact report presentation:
	- **Be concise** (bullet points, no extraneous details)
	- **Understand your audience** (avoid jargon)
	- **Use visuals** (Google Slides, Microsoft PowerPoint, Canva; charts, graphs, images, icons)
	- **Describe your learnings** (lessons learned, areas for improvement)
	- **Keep stakeholders engaged**: **Show** (demo videos, testimonials, case studies), **Storytell** (anecdotes related to the data), **Engage** (questions, surveys, quizzes)
- Demonstrating project impact to stakeholders: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-execution-google/supplement/etOc4
#### The closing process for the team
- Retrospectives: no better tool for reflection and improvement; we also do informal retrospectives daily (talking about our day, thinking how to improve past interactions)
- **Retrospective**: a meeting to discuss successes, failures, and possible improvements → after major milestones and at the end of a project; part of closing whether per phase or at the end
- Three benefits for the team: **team-building** (understand differing perspectives), **improved collaboration** on future projects, **positive changes** in future procedures and processes
- Resist the temptation to skip reflection → build a culture of **constant improvement**; solicit feedback on planning, scheduling, execution, communication, team dynamics (including processes you led) in a **safe space**
- **Recognize and celebrate** a job well done → team-building, uplifting rather than monotonous, fuels positive change → play a game, eat some cake, spend quality time together
#### Sarah: Why I love retrospectives
- Sarah: program manager on Google's **Engineering Education** team → cross-functional teams designing, launching, growing Computer Science Education programs; works with organizations in **New York City** to provide tech education to youth
- Incident: during one of the biggest tech education moments of the year, a **third-party platform** broke while thousands of students and teachers were about to code online → floods of emails, bugs, requests
	- Advice: **don't panic**, communicate openly and quickly; once fixed, run a retrospective and a clear **post-mortem** to prevent and mitigate it for future teams
- Google's **culture of blamelessness** (engineering and operational roles): instead of blaming engineering or comms, the whole team takes responsibility and learns
- Capture both big **strategic** lessons (build more flexibility into timelines) and small **tactical** changes to improve efficiency
#### The closing process for the project manager
- Closing lets the PM reflect on performance, ensure every task is completed, and prevent future confusion
- **Project closeout report**: a document created by project managers for project managers → three purposes:
	1. A blueprint documenting what the team did, how, and what they delivered
	2. An evaluation of the **quality** of work
	3. An evaluation of performance on **budget and schedule**
	- A **transfer of knowledge** to future PMs (a similar project or continuation may go to someone else) → assume readers are unfamiliar, be detailed; fewer questions for you later
- Contents:
	- **Executive summary**: process and purpose of the project, a few sentences to a paragraph max (could an executive understand the highlights from this alone?)
	- **Key accomplishments** and overall impact
	- **Lessons learned**: what went well and why, what went wrong and why, major effects of problem areas like **scope creep** and **schedule slip**
	- **Open items**: things not finished, ideas for changes with more time
	- **Next steps**: expected follow-up projects, ongoing maintenance
	- **Schedule and deadlines**: milestones and how they were chosen, project duration, stayed on track?, major setbacks
	- **Resources and team members**: who was involved and their roles (acknowledge contributors)
	- **Resources and project archive**: links to the original project plan, stakeholder communication and feedback (meeting notes), tracking/monitoring/reporting documents, technical information (user guides, manuals)
- Benefits: visibility among team members, more efficient future projects
