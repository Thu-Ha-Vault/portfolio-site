### Understanding the planning phase components
#### The benefits of project planning
- Before planning, the **initiation phase** must be complete (→ [Course 2](/blog/project-initiation-starting-a-successful-project/)): project manager assigned, goals approved, scope and deliverables approved, people assigned with roles and responsibilities, stakeholder sign-off on the **project charter**
- Planning: determine the processes and workflows needed to meet goals; draw from previous experience but try new approaches (every project is different)
- Benefits of planning:
	- Maps out the full project → understand the work needed to achieve goals
	- Coordinates efforts and timelines with other teams, contractors, and vendors
	- Time to **identify and prepare for risks** (timeline delay, departure of a critical team member, change in direction from a primary stakeholder) and brainstorm ways to mitigate them
	- Get **buy-in** (support for your plans) from key team members
	- Shows stakeholders the team is starting with a detailed plan
	- **Teamwork**: planning together creates a **shared understanding** among all parties
#### Launching the planning phase
- Planning phase = the **second phase** of the project life cycle; plans don't have to be perfect the first time and will likely change as the project evolves
- Three big things worked out in planning:
	- **Schedule**: a timeline of the project → start date, end date, and dates in between (determined with **time estimation** techniques)
		- E.g. **Office Green – Project Plant Pals** (desk-friendly plants for top clients, launch by end of year): dates to request proposals from plant vendors, kick-off with web designers/developers, plants ready for delivery, webpage design approval, target launch date
	- **Budget**: total cost to complete the project, broken down by project elements (e.g. designing and launching a webpage, hiring the plant vendor)
	- **Risk management**: searching for possible problems and planning ahead to mitigate them → where might the schedule get off track, where might the budget exceed estimates → **risk management plan**
		- E.g. developer estimates push you beyond the launch date → reduce or adjust **scope**, or negotiate a new launch date with stakeholders
#### Facilitating a project kick-off meeting
- **Project kick-off meeting**: the first meeting in which a project team comes together to ground everyone in a **shared vision**, gain a shared understanding of the project's goals and scope, and understand each person's role
- Invitees: team members from the **RACI chart** (created in initiation; defines roles and responsibilities), plus **stakeholders** and the **sponsor**
- Why not just the project charter: establish a shared vision, align on scope, build team rapport, let teammates ask questions, set expectations
- Sample agenda (about **1 hour**, adjust to your project):
	1. **Introductions** (~10 min): names, roles, fun fact
	2. **Project background** (~5 min): how the project came to be, why it matters, shared vision
	3. **Goals and scope** (~5 min): in-scope vs. out-of-scope, target launch date, important milestones
	4. **Roles** (~5 min): what each person is responsible for
	5. **Collaboration** (~10 min): tools as a communal source of information (project plan spreadsheet, work management software like **Asana**), how to communicate (daily email updates, team chat room, weekly check-ins)
	6. **What comes next** (~10 min): expectations and each teammate's next actions
	7. **Questions** (~15 min): gain clarity; benefit from diversity of thoughts
- Rowena's tips: let teammates add questions/feedback in a shared document; poll **confidence** on a 1–5 scale (1 = not confident at all, 5 = extremely confident) and ask how to help → deliver on any promises you make
- Logistics: put the agenda into a meeting agenda template, send it **a day or two ahead**; ask a teammate to **take notes** and record action items; recording is helpful for large or dispersed teams (get permission first); send a **follow-up email** with key points, outcomes, action items, invitation for questions
#### Tips for leading a successful kick-off meeting
- Kick-off meeting: first meeting among the project team, stakeholders, and sponsor at the start of a new project or **new project phase** → shared vision, goals and scope, roles and responsibilities
- Best practices:
	- **Set the right time**: works for everyone, mind time zones
	- **Set the right length**: no more than one hour; share key information first, then questions and team building
	- **Invite the right people**: those who play a role in development and execution (team members, stakeholders, sponsor)
	- **Designate a notetaker**: document feedback, changes, questions (tools: Chorus Notetaker, Google Keep, Google Docs, Microsoft OneNote)
	- **Set the agenda**: introductions, background and purpose, goals and scope, roles and responsibilities, collaboration process and tools, what comes next, questions and discussion
	- **Share the agenda** in advance by email and identify speakers for each topic
	- **Stick to the agenda**: redirect off-topic discussions
	- **Follow up after the meeting**: meeting summary with notes and action items
- Tips for leading a successful kick-off meeting: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/Xl6FA
### Defining tasks and milestones
#### Understanding tasks and milestones
- **Project milestone**: an important point within the project schedule that indicates progress and usually signifies the completion of a deliverable or phase → significant checkpoints to keep the project on schedule
	- E.g. completing the first draft of a report (goal: publish the report); receiving sign-off from the customer on a major deliverable
- **Project task**: an activity that needs to be accomplished within a set period of time → multiple tasks are needed to reach a milestone
	- E.g. milestone "first draft of a report" ← tasks: hire a writer, conduct research, draft sections
- Project Plant Pals example: deliverable = launch a website (orders, customer support)
	- Milestones: secure approval on the website design, implement feedback from user testing
	- Tasks for the design milestone: designer creates initial mockups → you review → designer implements feedback
- Tasks **ladder up** to milestones, which are crucial for project tracking
#### The importance of setting milestones
- Why break the project down instead of a quick to-do list:
	- **Clear understanding of the amount of work**: milestones → manageable chunks → tasks (launching a website may be more work than you think)
	- **Keep the project on track**: clear deadlines for deliverables to check against during execution
	- **Uncover areas to adjust** scope, timelines, or resources (e.g. more tasks than anticipated → ask a stakeholder to reduce scope)
	- **Motivate the team** (moment of celebration on long projects) and **show progress to stakeholders** (check-in point)
- Milestones must be completed **on time** and in **sequential order** (the next milestone usually depends on the previous one)
	- Plant Pals: design approval → website development → implement user testing feedback (can't build without approved design; can't test without a website)
	- Missing a milestone → schedule setback, overtime or additional resources; e.g. design not ready for Friday approval → development starts later; may also delay or lose a **client payment** tied to the deliverable
	- Be extra mindful of milestones with **non-negotiable** deadlines
#### How to set milestones
- Step 1: **evaluate the project as a whole** → refer back to the **project charter** for the goal → list what the team needs to do
	- Big items that indicate progress (completion of a deliverable or phase) = **milestones**; smaller items (e.g. anything a stakeholder wouldn't need to review) = **tasks**
	- Office Green website: milestones = design approval, website development complete, user feedback implemented; tasks = mocking up initial designs, building a landing page
	- No right number of milestones: some projects have many, others two or three
- Step 2: **assign each milestone a deadline**
	- Space milestones out to give the team time for the tasks (don't expect several milestones in one week on a months-long project)
	- Ask teammates for **estimates** of how long the tasks will take
	- Consider **stakeholder needs**: when will they expect to see a deliverable; they want regular indications of progress
#### Setting milestones: Best practices
- **Project task**: an activity that needs to be accomplished within a set period of time and is assigned to one or more individuals
- **Project milestone**: an important point within the project schedule that usually signifies the completion of a major deliverable
- Set tasks to identify milestones:
	- **Top-down scheduling**: the project manager lays out higher-level milestones, then breaks the effort down into tasks (with the team, to capture all tasks)
	- **Bottom-up scheduling**: the project manager looks at all individual tasks, then rolls them into manageable chunks that lead to a milestone
	- E.g. milestone "approval on the first draft of an article" ← tasks "develop outline", "write first draft", "send to the editor"; then another set of tasks for the milestone "revise the article"
- Integrate milestones into the schedule: set milestones for the **most important events** (where you achieve major goals), not a target number
- Pitfalls:
	- **Too many milestones** → importance downplayed; too small or specific milestones make the project look bigger than it is
	- **Mistaking tasks for milestones**: milestones are moments in time; tasks are how you get there
	- **Listing milestones and tasks separately**: visualize them together in one place (e.g. a project plan)
- Setting milestones: Best practices: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/7xIgX
#### Creating a work breakdown structure
- **Work breakdown structure (WBS)**: a tool that sorts the milestones and tasks of a project in a **hierarchy**, in the order they need to be completed → breaks intimidating challenges (publishing a report, organizing a conference) into manageable chunks
- Common format: a **tree diagram**, e.g. Plant Pals website launch:
	- Level 1: project name
	- Level 2: milestones → securing design approval, developing the site, implementing user feedback
	- Level 3: tasks under each milestone (e.g. design approval → mocking up designs, collecting feedback)
- The WBS diagram usually isn't included in the official project plan → input its tasks into a **spreadsheet** or **work management software** to assign owners
- After a WBS: discrete tasks laddering up to each milestone + ready to assign tasks
- Assigning tasks:
	- By **role** (web designer mocks up the design → you review and give feedback → designer implements feedback → web developer develops the site)
	- Same role: consider each person's **familiarity** with the tasks (one developer builds the landing page, another the "contact us" page)
	- Consider **workload**: time on the project vs. other work; keep workloads balanced; overload → quality suffers, timeline at risk
- Assigning in **Asana**: start each task with a **verb** ("mock up the website", "add images to the website", not "website"); add an **assignee** and a **due date**; add details (description, links to files/attachments, comments)
- Benefits of assigning tasks: frees you to manage the project; creates **personal responsibility** and ownership (more invested, personal growth); builds your skill as a **supportive delegator**; keeps the team motivated
	- If full ownership feels overwhelming → encourage teammates to support one another (builds rapport)
#### Breaking down the work breakdown structure
- **WBS**: a deliverable-oriented breakdown of a project into smaller components; sorts milestones and tasks into a hierarchy, in the order they need to be completed
- A thorough WBS gives a visual representation of the project → easier to estimate costs, develop a schedule, assign roles and responsibilities, track progress (smaller tasks ladder up to larger tasks or milestones)
- Steps to build a WBS (example: planning a company event):
	1. Start with the **high-level project picture**: brainstorm major deliverables and milestones ("secure venue", "finalize guest logistics", "establish agenda")
	2. Identify the **tasks** needed to meet those milestones ("secure venue" → "research venues", "tour and decorate space", "make down payment")
	3. Break tasks down further into **sub-tasks** ("tour and decorate space" → "organize decorating committee", "purchase decorations", "assign decorating responsibilities")
- Further reading: *How to Create a Work Breakdown Structure and Why You Should*
- Breaking down the work breakdown structure: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/tfW01

## Module 2: Building a project plan
### Getting started with a project plan
#### Introduction: Building a project plan
- Coming up: why to create and manage a **project plan**, what goes into one (including the **project schedule**), **time estimation** methods to prevent project failure, tools and best practices to build a project plan
#### Components of a project plan
- **Project plan**: documents the scope, tasks, milestones, and overall activities of the project; useful for any project, big or small
	- At the center: the **project schedule** → estimate how long the project will take, track progress against goals
- Five basic elements (vary by company):
	- **Tasks**: activities to accomplish within a set period of time, assigned by role and skills
	- **Milestones**: important points in the schedule that indicate progress (completion of a deliverable or phase)
	- **People**: team members and their roles → each understands their tasks (frees you to manage, creates personal responsibility)
	- **Documentation**: links to the **RACI chart**, **project charter**, budget, risk management plan
	- **Time**: estimated time → basis of the schedule (the **anchor** of the plan): task start/completion dates, milestone dates, project start and end dates (determine which resources you need and when)
#### Putting together the pieces of a project plan
- A project plan is a **living artifact** that serves as the team's roadmap
- Besides the schedule, include:
	- **Scope and goals**: captured initially in the **project charter** → link it in the plan; reminds the team of objectives and of requests that go beyond what was agreed
	- **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)**: tasks visible in one place with clear descriptions, **owners**, and **due dates**; detailed milestones and statuses to visualize progress; also link the RACI chart (best practice: store/link documentation in one place for transparency)
	- **Budget**: managed and monitored throughout the life cycle; link it to the plan; if another department manages the funds, have **regular check-ins** with them
	- **Management plans**: change management plan, risk management plan, communication plan
- No two project plans are the same; at Google many different tools and templates are used → know your end goals and what's essential to your team
- Putting together the pieces of a project plan: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/bkx1m
### Using estimation to set project timelines
#### Making realistic time estimates
- The project manager identifies and helps assign tasks, then estimates how long they'll take (with the team) → estimates form the overall **project schedule**
- **Time estimation**: a prediction of the total amount of time required to complete a task (overall duration, including **inactive time**)
- **Effort estimation**: a prediction of the amount and difficulty of active work required to complete a task
	- E.g. painting a wall: effort = **30 minutes**, time = **24 hours** (23.5 hours of drying) → during idle time the teammate can do other tasks (paint the mailbox, window trim)
- Unrealistic effort estimates (usually **underestimating**) hurt the schedule; the culprit is often **optimism** → overlooking risks and setbacks
	- Avoid it by asking the teammates assigned to each task (they understand the work best); you can ask follow-up questions or gently push back
	- **Sub-tasks**: smaller tasks required to complete a larger task
	- Plant Pals: contact list of top customers assumed to take 1 day → sub-tasks: meet the global sales team to identify clients, gather contact information, determine client language preferences, build a spreadsheet → teammate estimates **2 days**
- Estimates can still be wrong (e.g. Sales team out for team-building until after the weekend → task delay)
- **Buffer**: extra time added to the end of a task or a project to account for unexpected slowdowns or delays
	- **Task buffers**: extra time on a specific task → mainly for tasks **outside the team's control** (e.g. ask a plant vendor for a cost estimate by Monday though you need it Thursday); use sparingly for tasks within the team's control (only difficult or unpredictable ones, like how long plants take to grow); a buffer on every task lengthens the schedule unrealistically
	- **Project buffers**: extra time added to the overall schedule, towards the end (e.g. **2–3 days**), used as needed when deadlines are missed
- Rowena's example: a new hire great at coding kept missing deadlines → not enough buffer for **testing** → asked about workload and task complexity to decide where to add buffer
- Goal: a **realistic timeline** (hitting the goal two months late may not count as success)
#### Case study: Run fast, pay later
- Flawed time estimation is the **root cause of many failed projects**
- Case: **Kendra**, new project manager on a competitively bid project; saw the timeline was almost impossible but kept quiet → rushed planning, created all planning documents **without team or stakeholder input** → team raised concerns (not enough time, no time for reviews) → told them to "work faster" → delays, rework, unaccounted tasks, stressed team, concerned stakeholders → **missed the deadline** → lessons in a retrospective
- Analysis:
	- **Escalating concerns**: should have gathered information supporting her concerns and shared it with management
	- **Working carefully**: thorough planning with the team could reveal solutions in advance:
		- **Elimination of tasks** (unnecessary work)
		- **Increased team size** (request more resources early)
		- **Streamlining of activities** (tasks done in parallel instead of sequentially)
	- **Gathering input from the team**: team members, peers, management to build and review the plan; address concerns of those doing the work
- Turn pain points (timeline concerns) into opportunities to modify the plan
- Key takeaway: be realistic about time and effort, evaluate risks, talk to the team, don't be afraid to **escalate** to management; optimism can hurt time estimation

#### Overcoming the planning fallacy
- **Planning fallacy** (Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, **1977**, behavioral economics): the tendency to underestimate the time, costs, and risks of a task due to **optimism bias**
- **Optimism bias**: believing you are less likely to experience a negative event (e.g. walking the dog between meetings without considering weather, other dogs, sniffing around)
- Happens to anyone, regardless of experience with similar tasks → be **"optimistically realistic"**: push for the best outcomes while planning for the proper time each task may take
- Case: **David**, home construction project
	- WBS: 1.0 House project → 1.1 Foundation (1.1.1 Blueprint, 1.1.2 Labor to dig), 1.2 Construction (1.2.1 Order materials, 1.2.2 Labor to build structure, 1.2.3 Labor to build roof), 1.3 Finishing (1.3.1 Plumbing, 1.3.2 Electrical, 1.3.3 Paint, 1.3.4 Landscaping)
	- Estimates: Foundation **2 weeks**, Construction **4 weeks**, Adjustments **4 weeks** → total **10 weeks** (meets delivery requirement)
	- Mindful of the planning fallacy → considers risks (weather delays, crew calling out sick), meets team and stakeholders → adds **task buffers** to some tasks
- Key takeaways: look out for **"what-ifs"**; use the project team to uncover risks
- Overcoming the planning fallacy: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/B89Qc
#### Capacity planning and the critical path
- **Capacity**: the amount of work that the people or resources assigned to the project can reasonably complete in a set period of time
- **Capacity planning**: allocating people and resources to project tasks and determining whether you have the resources needed to complete the work on time (e.g. you may need a second web developer or a third writer)
	- Plant Pals: deliver plants to **100 customers** in **5 days**; one driver averages **4 deliveries** per 8-hour day → hire at least **5 drivers**
	- Even someone at 100% on your project has limited capacity (meetings, urgent tasks, ...)
- **Critical path**: the list of project milestones you must reach to meet the project goal on schedule, plus the mandatory tasks that contribute to each milestone; anything else is **off the critical path**
	- Plant Pals: hiring plant vendors, developing a new website, fulfilling deliveries = on the critical path; adding flowers to the product lineup = nice to have, not on it
	- The bare minimum tasks and milestones to reach the goal → if any is late, the project may be delayed
- Determining the critical path: list all tasks and milestones (use the **WBS**) → identify **dependencies** (tasks that can't begin until another is complete) → time estimates with the team, map each task start to finish → the **longest path** is the critical path
- Factors that impact capacity planning:
	- **Parallel** tasks (can happen at the same time → efficiencies; e.g. hiring delivery drivers and developing the website) vs. **sequential** tasks (must happen in a specific order → prioritize early; e.g. budget approval before hiring a vendor)
	- **Fixed start date**: the date you must start a task to achieve your goal (e.g. contract says deliver 100 plants on a specific date → picking up the plants has a fixed start one day prior)
	- **Earliest start date**: the earliest date you can begin a task (e.g. new vendor: contracts signed and purchase order approved, about **3 weeks** at Office Green → vendor's earliest start is 3 weeks after the kick-off with the vendor)
	- **Float** (or **slack**): the amount of time you can wait to begin a task before it impacts the schedule and threatens the outcome → critical-path tasks have **zero float**; tasks with float are not on the critical path (e.g. shipping plants to a priority customer on a requested date = zero float)
#### Creating a critical path
- The critical path is a framework that tells you where you are, where you are headed, and when you'll get there → shows essential tasks and durations, which late tasks would delay completion, needed resources, **baselines**, and schedule flexibility
- Steps (example: building the structure of a house):
	1. **Capture all tasks** (use the WBS; focus on "need to do", not "nice to do"): A) Excavation, B) Foundation, C) Framing, D) Roof, E) Plumbing, F) HVAC, G) Electrical, H) Insulation, I) Drywall + Paint, J) Flooring
	2. **Set dependencies**: which task must happen before this one, which can finish at the same time, which happens right after
	3. **Create a network diagram**: sequences tasks by dependencies → shows the path from start (excavation) to end (flooring), parallel tasks (HVAC and plumbing) vs. sequential (plumbing then insulation), and non-essential tasks off the critical path (driveway pavement, landscaping, trim, appliances)
	4. **Make time estimates** with key stakeholders (inaccurate estimates can change the critical path; review and update during the project)
	5. **Find the critical path**: add up durations of essential tasks and find the **longest** path (only tasks whose non-completion affects the finish date)

| Task | Duration | Dependency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A) Excavation | 1 day | – |
| B) Foundation | 3 days | A |
| C) Framing | 15 days | B |
| D) Roof | 3 days | C |
| E) Plumbing | 4 days | C |
| F) HVAC | 3 days | C |
| G) Electrical | 3 days | C |
| H) Insulation | 2 days | E, F, G |
| I) Drywall + Paint | 15 days | H |
| J) Flooring | 7 days | I |

- Calculation approaches (useful for **earliest/latest start dates** and **slack** = how long a task can be delayed past its earliest start without delaying the project):
	- **Forward pass**: start at the first task and add up durations of critical-path tasks to the end
	- **Backward pass**: start with the final task or milestone and move backwards to find the shortest path to completion → with a hard deadline, shows which tasks are truly critical (cut some or do them later)
- Further reading: *How to Use the Critical Path Method for Complete Beginners*; *Critical Path Method: A Project Management Essential*
- Creating a critical path: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/cyLyh
#### Getting accurate time estimates from your team
- The person assigned to a task has the best sense of how long it takes and of their own capacity → estimates are a two-way discussion
- **Soft skills**: personal characteristics that help people work effectively with others (communication, interpersonal skills) → understand what blocks someone's best work
- Three ways to get accurate estimates:
	- **Asking the right questions**: treat it like an interview; use **open-ended questions** (cannot be answered with yes or no)
		- Closed: "Can you complete the mock-ups in one week?" → open: "How long does it typically take you to mock up a website design like this one?"
		- Follow-ups: How complex are the steps? What are the risks? When do you think you can have this ready?
	- **Negotiating effectively**: bridge high-level goals and the team's day-to-day perspective; teammates may have **competing priorities** → collaborate on an outcome that works for everyone
		- E.g. designer estimates **2 weeks**, you hoped for **1 week** → gently challenge: does it include multiple pages? Can one or two pages be shared sooner? → is the estimate flexible or do you need an additional designer → **shared ownership**
	- **Practicing empathy**: **Empathy** = the ability to relate to the thoughts and feelings of others → builds trust
		- Ask about workload (incl. work outside the project), work-life balance, scheduled vacation or leave, crucial holidays
		- E.g. designer also designing a website for another Office Green team with overlapping timelines → work with the other project manager to balance workloads
		- Always be appreciative of the team's work, collaboration, and support
#### Angel: The value of interpersonal skills in time estimation
- Angel: Program Manager at Google, mechanical engineer by degree ("You're an okay engineer, but your people skills are off the charts")
- Soft skills = **emotional intelligence**: read other people, read the team's needs, know yourself and how you influence the team
	- Ask quieter people for input: they are often thinking deeply about the problem and have insights
	- Get the full view from the team on estimations or costs (labor or dollars)
- Ask good questions, not "I need you to go faster": How much faster could you go if I gave you this? What is stopping you? Which teams need to be involved? Are there similar issues elsewhere in the project?
- A Program Manager's role: **see patterns** where things slow down or stop; acknowledge problems without blaming → learn, fix, move on
- Relate to team members; have people **walk through the estimate out loud** → may reveal time savings
- Project management skills are not tied to a field (labels machines, locomotive engines, plywood) → it's the method, process, and how you structure a team
### Utilizing tools to build a project plan
#### Developing a project schedule
- The anchor of a good project plan: a clear **schedule** with all tasks, their **owners**, and when they need to be completed → build the plan around it with tools like spreadsheets and **Asana**
- **Gantt chart**: a horizontal bar chart that maps out a project schedule (named after American engineer **Henry Gantt**, popularized in the early 1900s)
	- Highly visual: who is responsible for each task, when it's due, how tasks connect; almost like a calendar (start and end dates, bar length = time devoted)
	- E.g. **Leon** creates the project charter (Friday, Monday, Tuesday) → **Kylie** reviews and edits it (Wednesday); bars **cascade** down over time
- Gantt chart in a spreadsheet:
	- Left columns: **task title**, **task owner**, **start date**, **due date**, **duration**, **percent of task complete** (list tasks and milestones from the WBS; rows ordered by start date)
	- Right columns: the **weeks** from project start to finish, with bars for when tasks take place
	- Extra tabs to house or link other documents (RACI chart, project charter, risk management plan, communication plan) → one place saves time, keeps everyone organized, no searching through emails; alternatively a digital document linking all documents
- Alternatives: a digital document with tables or task lists, owners, due dates, links (for simple projects); **Kanban boards** (cards to track and visualize task progress)
- Whatever the tool: include the project's **goals**, **tasks**, **owners**, **start and end dates**, and relevant **planning documentation**
#### Project plan best practices
- Five best practices for a plan that stays useful through execution and closing:
	1. **Carefully review deliverables, milestones, and tasks**: the charter (goal, scope, deliverables) becomes more **granular** in planning → e.g. new website deliverable → milestones (kick-off with the web developer, stakeholder approval) → tasks (mock up the design, develop a landing page), each with an owner and start/end date; do this for **every** deliverable
	2. **Give yourself time to plan**: planning is time-intensive; think realistically about what the team can and cannot accomplish (effort estimation, capacity planning); allow **buffer time**
	3. **Recognize and plan for the inevitable (things will go wrong)**: identify the most likely risks and plan to prevent or mitigate them (buffer; **risk management plan** later in the course)
	4. **Stay curious**: you aren't an expert on every task → ask teammates lots of questions (deeper insights, trust); understand stakeholders' and vendors' expectations, priorities, risk assessments, communication styles (e.g. how stakeholders want to be kept in the loop, vendor availability)
	5. **Champion your plan**: Can teammates use the tool? Is the information clear for stakeholders? Will it be a **single source of truth** that saves time? → tell the team why staying on top of the plan benefits them → buy-in, regular updates
#### Creating a project plan: Tools and templates
- The project plan is the project's **blueprint**: all activities and milestones needed to complete the project; templates vary (some companies require standard templates)
- General rule: **spreadsheet** for a simple project, **project management software** for a complex one
- Key information in any tool:
	- **Task ID numbers or task names** (easy reference among dozens to thousands of tasks)
	- **Task durations** (estimated time per task → organize and prioritize)
	- **Start and finish dates** (track whether you're on time)
	- **Who is responsible for what** (roles and responsibilities; assign an **owner** to each task)
- Spreadsheets: great for less complex projects with clear task assignment; manual input but more control; customizable (example: website launch plan)
	- Pro tip: incorporate **WBS numbers** as task ID numbers for consistency
- Templates: Smartsheet Project Plan Templates for Microsoft Word / for Google Sheets, Google Project Plan Timeline Template, Microsoft Gantt Chart Template
- **Gemini** can speed up project plans: *Collaborate with Gemini in Google Sheets*, *Tables in Google Sheets with Gemini*
- Work management tools (preferences vary by company, PM, customer): **Smartsheet**, **Asana**, **Jira**, **Trello** → collaboration and communication at the task level
- Key takeaway: the tool should help collect and track project details, manage the schedule, and visualize progress
- Creating a project plan: Tools and templates: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/3giHb
#### Introduction to Kanban boards
- **Kanban board**: a visual tool to manage tasks and workflows (whiteboards, magnetic boards, poster boards, software); tasks written on **cards** placed in **columns** representing progress
	- Most suitable for teams using an **Agile** approach (iterative, continuous releases, customer feedback with every iteration)
- Purposes: quick visual understanding of work details and critical task information; facilitate **handoffs** between stakeholders (e.g. development → testing); capture metrics and improve workflows
- Before creating a board: gather tasks, status, dates, durations
- Example columns: **To do → In progress → Testing → Done**; can add rows for resources (team or person) to show who is working on what; customizable
- Card contents (physical cards often use both sides):
	- Front: **title and unique identifier**; **description of work** (fits on an index card); **estimation of effort** ("small", "medium", "large"); **who is assigned** (ideally one person per card)
	- Back: **start date** (metrics, tracking, checking estimates); **blocked days** (days the task is halted, e.g. waiting on a deliverable); **finish date**
- Software: **Asana**, **Trello** (evaluate which is best for you)
- Introduction to Kanban boards: https://www.coursera.org/learn/project-planning-google/supplement/LyKYD

## Module 3: Managing budgeting and procurement
### Understanding project budgets
#### Introduction: Managing budgeting and procurement
- Coming up: creating and managing a real-world **project budget** (components, stakeholders' role), **procurement**, **vendor management** in Agile and traditional settings
- Legal and contractual documentation acronyms: **NDAs**, **RFPs**, **SoWs**; the role of **legal teams** and **ethics** in procurement (sourcing materials and vendors without considering ethics can get PMs into hot water)
#### The importance of budget setting
- **Project budget**: the estimated monetary resources needed to achieve the project's goals and objectives
	- Consider all potential and projected costs; break the budget down by **milestones** and list activities and tasks with their costs
	- **Forecast**: a cost estimate or prediction over a period of time; the budget is reviewed frequently and evolves through the life cycle
	- Typical items: **labor**, **operating costs**, costs of materials (hardware, software, equipment)
- Why it matters: a budget is a **deliverable** and a **success metric**; communicates what is needed and when to stakeholders; directly affects the company's **financial viability**
- Budget creation starts in the **initiation phase**, adjusted throughout; you may not be the sole creator (ownership of budget and vendor relationships depends on company size, support team, org chart) → still know the budget's ins and outs (budget and milestones go hand in hand)
- **Signing or spending policy**: who has the authority to commit resources or incur costs on behalf of the company → the PM may need to obtain spending approvals (e.g. don't rent a property without checking the cost)
- Budgeting happens together with **scheduling** (schedule steps depend on costs); PM collaborates with people on the project for estimates → **project sponsor** or a key stakeholder (even CEO or COO) reviews, approves, reallocates; Office Green: the **director of product** has sign-off; often also **finance department** sign-off
- Prioritize where funds go for maximum output; projects aim to improve productivity, increase revenue, or save costs
- Don't go **over budget** (extra cost) or **under budget** (may affect next year's budget)
	- Publicly traded companies (Google) and public sector organizations report financial performance to shareholders or auditors → show the requested money was used to secure budget for future projects
	- Smaller businesses: tighter budgets → be especially careful not to overspend
