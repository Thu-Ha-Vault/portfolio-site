## Purpose
Landscape assessment expands beyond a single restoration site to understand the broader ecological, social, and land-use context influencing ANR success.

## Why Landscape Assessment Matters
- Ecosystems function across large areas.
- Activities outside the restoration site can affect outcomes.
- Restoration benefits increase when coordinated across landscapes.

## Key Landscape Elements

### 1. Land Use Patterns
- Forests.
- Agricultural areas.
- Settlements.
- Water bodies.
- Degraded lands.

### 2. Ecological Connectivity
- Wildlife corridors.
- Seed dispersal pathways.
- Connections between forest patches. (→ [L8 Case studies (enrichment)](/blog/sinh-hoc/assisted-natural-regeneration-practitioner-training-course-2026/week-4-case-studies-of-anr-implementation-practices-and-outcomes/lesson-8-case-studies-of-anr-part-2-with-enrichment-planting/))
- Habitat fragmentation levels.

### 3. Drivers of Degradation
- Agricultural expansion.
- Logging.
- Fire.
- Overgrazing.
- Infrastructure development.

### 4. Stakeholders and Governance
- Communities.
- Landowners.
- Government agencies.
- NGOs and restoration partners.
- Traditional authorities.

## Assessment Tools
- Landscape mapping.
- Remote sensing and satellite imagery.
- GIS analysis.
- Participatory mapping with local communities.
- Stakeholder consultations.

## Landscape Opportunities
- Areas with high natural regeneration potential.
- Existing forest remnants that can serve as seed sources.
- Watersheds requiring protection.
- Sites suitable for corridor restoration.

## Planning Outcomes
- Identification of priority restoration zones.
- Coordination among stakeholders.
- Integration of ANR into broader land-use planning.
- Development of landscape-scale restoration strategies.

## Key Takeaway
Successful ANR depends not only on site-level conditions but also on understanding the wider landscape, including ecological connections, land-use dynamics, and stakeholder interests.
